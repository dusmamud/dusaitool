The best accessibility tool is not the one with the longest feature list. It is the one that works on the phone or computer you already own, on an Indian mobile connection, without a VPN, and without asking you to pay before you know whether it helps you. That is the test every tool in this article had to pass. If a tool needed a US account, a credit card for its free tier, or a VPN to open in India, it did not make the list.

The short answer for India is this: if you are blind or have low vision, start with [Be My Eyes](https://www.bemyeyes.com/) and [Seeing AI](https://www.seeingai.com/) on your phone, and add [NVDA](https://www.nvaccess.org/download/) on a Windows computer. If you are deaf or hard of hearing, start with the free tools already built into Android — Live Transcribe with Sound Notifications, and Live Caption — and add [Otter.ai](https://otter.ai/) only when you need a written record of meetings. If reading printed or on-screen text is the barrier, try [Speechify](https://speechify.com/) free first, and use the text-to-speech already built into your phone before you pay for anything. If your speech is hard for standard voice assistants to understand, [Voiceitt](https://www.voiceitt.com/) is worth a careful trial, but go in knowing its free period is a 30-day trial, not a free plan.

None of these tools replaces a person, a teacher, a doctor, or an official service. An AI description of a medicine label, a road crossing, or a legal notice can be wrong, and a wrong caption in a classroom or a workplace meeting can change the meaning of what was said. Use these tools to gain independence in everyday tasks, and double-check anything where a mistake would cost health, money, or safety. Prices and free limits change often, so before you pay for any plan, or promise a deadline that depends on a tool, re-check the official pricing and help page of that tool. Where sources disagreed or a limit was unclear, this article says so plainly instead of guessing.

## For people who are blind or have low vision

### Be My Eyes: an AI description, or a real volunteer, on demand

Be My Eyes is the first tool to try if you want to know what is in front of your camera. You point your phone at something — a packet, a letter, a room, a street sign — and Be My AI gives you a detailed spoken or written description, or you can call a volunteer who sees through your camera and talks you through the task. The company describes its app as free across 150 countries and 180 languages, with a large volunteer community, and available on iOS, Android, Windows, and macOS. That breadth matters in India, because it means you are not depending on a small local volunteer pool at 2 a.m.

Use Be My AI for quick, private questions: what colour is this shirt, what does this label say, is this the right bus number. Use a volunteer call for tasks where judgement matters more than speed, such as sorting similar-looking medicines or checking whether a document is the one you meant to sign. Do not use either for a task where an AI mistake could hurt you, such as reading a dosage alone for the first time. For that, ask a trusted person to confirm, and keep the AI description as a second check rather than the only check.

### Seeing AI: Microsoft's free visual assistant

Seeing AI is a free app from Microsoft that narrates the world around you. Point the camera at printed text and it reads it aloud, with audio cues to help you line up a page. Take a photo and it describes the scene; you can then ask questions about what is in it. It can scan barcodes to identify products, recognise currency notes, identify colours, and give an audible tone for the brightness around you. It also describes photos and videos shared from other apps, which is useful when someone sends you an image on WhatsApp without any description.

Seeing AI is listed on Google Play in India, so Android users in India can install it directly without a VPN, and it is also available on iOS. One honest limit: Seeing AI is an ongoing research project, in Microsoft's own words, and its features keep changing. If a feature you relied on moves or disappears after an update, that is the trade-off of a free research app. Keep Be My Eyes installed alongside it, so you always have a second way to get a description.

### Google Lookout: the Android alternative worth knowing

If you use Android and want a Google-built option, Lookout is made for people who are blind or have low vision. It uses your phone's camera and sensors to recognise objects and text and describe what it finds. It needs Android 6.0 or later, which covers most phones in use in India today. Its supported languages include Hindi, Bengali, and Gujarati alongside English, which matters because a tool that only works well in English is only half a tool for many Indian users.

Lookout, Seeing AI, and Be My Eyes overlap, and that overlap is a strength, not a waste of storage. One app may read a crumpled Hindi label better than another on a given day. Try the same everyday task — reading a grocery packet, identifying a note, describing a room — in all three, and keep the one that is most reliable for your phone, your light, and your language.

### NVDA on Windows, and the screen readers already on your phone

A screen reader turns everything on a screen into speech or Braille output. On a Windows computer, the most important free option is NVDA, short for NonVisual Desktop Access. It can be downloaded free of charge by anyone, and NV Access, the organisation behind it, says it keeps NVDA free because everyone, especially the world's poorest blind people, deserves access to computers. It runs on Windows 10 and later. Older Windows versions need older NVDA versions, so check the download page for your exact version rather than assuming the newest release will run.

NVDA reads many Indian languages, and Indian screen-reader guides note that NVDA and JAWS are the two screen readers generally used in India. The difference that matters most is price: NVDA is free, while JAWS is a commercial product you buy through a dealer. If a training centre or employer tells you that only a paid screen reader will work, ask them to show you exactly which task NVDA cannot do, because for everyday study, office work, and browsing, NVDA is the tool most Indian users should try first.

On phones, you may not need to install anything at all. Android phones come with TalkBack, and iPhones come with VoiceOver. Both are full screen readers built into the operating system. Turn one on in your phone's accessibility settings and learn its gestures before you judge it. Many people who think they need an expensive app actually need an hour of practice with the screen reader already on their phone, plus a pair of earphones so the speech stays private in public.

## For people who are deaf or hard of hearing

### Live Transcribe and Sound Notifications: free, on your Android phone

Live Transcribe turns speech around you into text on your screen in real time, so you can follow a conversation, a shopkeeper, or a teacher without asking them to write everything down. Sound Notifications, in the same Google app, listens for important household sounds — a smoke alarm, a doorbell, a baby crying, an appliance beeping — and alerts you on your phone. Google's own help pages say the audio Sound Notifications hears stays on your device and is not sent to Google without your permission, which is a meaningful privacy point for a tool that listens all day.

Both are free, and both work without a VPN in India on ordinary Android phones. The practical limits are the ones Google itself warns about: Sound Notifications listens continuously, so it uses more battery, and it works best when the phone is in the middle of the room with nothing blocking it. Live Transcribe needs a reasonably clear voice and a steady connection for its best results, and like every automatic transcription tool, it will mishear names, numbers, and accented speech. For a classroom or a workplace meeting, treat the transcript as a live aid, not as an official record, and ask for a written summary for anything you must act on later.

### Live Caption: captions for everything playing on your phone

Live Caption automatically captions speech in media on your device — videos, podcasts, phone calls, video calls, and audio messages — with a single tap. Google says all captions are processed locally, never stored, and never leave your device. That on-device design is why it can caption a call without sending your conversation to a server.

Availability depends on your phone. Live Caption works on Pixel phones and selected Android phones, and the language list grows with newer models: Hindi is supported on Pixel 4 and Pixel 5 series and selected Android phones, and on Pixel 6 and later alongside many other languages. If your phone does not show Live Caption, check with your phone's manufacturer rather than assuming the feature does not exist in India — on some phones it is hidden under accessibility settings, and on others it genuinely is not included. When you first turn it on, you need Wi-Fi to download the default language pack, so set it up at home, not on mobile data on the day you need it.

### Otter.ai: when you need a written record, not just live captions

Live Caption helps you follow something in the moment. Otter.ai helps you keep it afterwards. It records meetings and lectures, transcribes them, identifies speakers, and lets you search the text later. Its Basic plan is free and includes 300 transcription minutes a month, along with live transcription, speaker identification, and apps for iOS and Android. That is enough for a few lectures or meetings a month, but not for a full-time student or a professional who attends meetings all day.

Otter's paid Pro plan is priced in US dollars on its pricing page, and the exact rupee cost will depend on taxes and exchange rates at the time you pay, so check the checkout total before subscribing rather than trusting a converted figure in any article, including this one. Also remember the privacy trade-off: unlike Live Caption, Otter processes your audio on its servers so it can store and search it. Do not record a meeting on Otter without telling the other people in it, and do not upload a recording that contains someone else's private information.

## For reading, writing, and speaking

### Speechify: listening instead of reading

Speechify turns web pages, PDFs, and documents into spoken audio, with voices that sound much more natural than the robotic voices of a decade ago. It is useful for people with dyslexia, low vision, or attention difficulties, and for anyone whose reading load is simply too large to finish by eye. Its free plan is genuinely free — no credit card required and no automatic conversion to a paid plan, according to Speechify's own explanation — but it has real limits: voices on the free plan are more basic, and playback speed tops out at 1.5x, while premium voices go faster. Monthly premium voice minutes are also capped on the free plan.

Before you pay, try the free text-to-speech already on your device. iPhones have Spoken Content and Android phones have Select to Speak, and for short passages they are perfectly good. Speechify earns its money for hours-per-day reading: better voices, faster speeds, optical character recognition for printed pages, and syncing across devices. If you read for an hour a day or more, that difference matters. If you read for ten minutes a day, the built-in reader is probably enough, and paying would be a waste.

For voice tools more broadly, see our guide to [free AI voice tools in India](/blog/free-ai-voice-tools-india/).

### Voiceitt: speech recognition for non-standard speech

Most voice assistants are trained on typical speech, and they fail people whose speech sounds different — because of cerebral palsy, Parkinson's, a stroke, Down syndrome, or simply a strong accent combined with a speech difference. Voiceitt is built specifically for non-standard speech. It is a web app that learns your unique speech patterns: you record phrase cards to teach it, it turns your speech into clear synthesised speech for other people, or into text you can dictate, and it can add personalised captions to video calls.

Go in with honest expectations. Voiceitt starts everyone with a free 30-day trial, and it requires 50 recordings before it is ready for you to try, with more recordings improving it over time. The company itself says it cannot promise 100 percent accuracy. That means Voiceitt is a tool to evaluate carefully during the trial, with the help of a speech-language pathologist if you have access to one, and not a purchase to make on the first day. If after the trial it does not understand you reliably, that is a finding, not a failure on your part — the technology is still learning, and you should not pay for a tool that does not work for your voice.

## Using these tools in India: language, data, and cost

India is where many accessibility tools meet their hardest test, and it is worth naming the three points of friction honestly. The first is language. A tool that works beautifully in American English may struggle with Hindi, Hinglish, Bengali, Tamil, or Marathi. Live Caption supports Hindi on newer phones, Lookout lists Hindi, Bengali, and Gujarati, and NVDA reads many Indian languages, but support is uneven across tools. Test every tool in the language you actually use every day, not just in English, before you rely on it.

The second is data and battery. Tools that listen, watch, or caption continuously use battery and, in some cases, mobile data. Prefer tools that process on your device — Live Caption and Sound Notifications both claim on-device processing — when you are on a limited data plan or a phone with a small battery. Download language packs, voices, and apps over Wi-Fi at home, and carry a charger if you plan to use a listening tool all day.

The third is cost. The free tools in this article — Be My Eyes, Seeing AI, Lookout, NVDA, TalkBack, VoiceOver, Live Transcribe, Sound Notifications, and Live Caption — cost nothing beyond the phone or computer you already own. That is the right starting point for most people. Paid plans like Otter Pro and Speechify Premium are worth considering only after the free tier has proved useful for at least a few weeks, and only after you have checked the official pricing page on the day you pay. Prices, free minute limits, and trial lengths change, and an article written months ago cannot promise you today's price. If a tool asks for payment before you have tested it, or hides its free limit until after signup, treat that as a warning sign.

## What to avoid

Avoid paying for an accessibility tool before you have tested its free tier on your own phone, in your own language, on your own daily tasks. A demo video recorded on a flagship phone in a quiet studio tells you almost nothing about how the tool behaves on your phone on a noisy Indian street.

Avoid trusting an AI description, caption, or transcript for anything where a mistake could harm you. Medicine labels, bank messages, exam instructions, legal notices, and safety warnings all deserve a second check by a trusted person or an official source. AI tools are aids to independence, not replacements for verification.

Avoid sharing sensitive information with a tool that does not need it. Do not point a camera app at bank cards, identity documents, or private letters unless you have checked what the app stores and where. Prefer on-device tools for private conversations, and read the privacy page before you upload recordings of other people.

Avoid tools that promise a cure, a diagnosis, or a guaranteed result. An app that claims it can diagnose a condition, replace therapy, or guarantee that a child will learn to speak is selling hope, not help. This article is general information only, not medical, legal, or professional advice, and for health, education, or benefits decisions, check official and professional sources.

Avoid installing five similar apps at once and judging none of them properly. Pick one tool for one task, use it for a week, and only then decide whether to keep it, replace it, or add a second tool for a different task. Accessibility is a daily habit, not a download count.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which free AI tool is best for a blind user in India?**

Start with Be My Eyes and Seeing AI together, both free. Be My Eyes gives you AI descriptions plus volunteer calls, and Seeing AI reads text, describes scenes, and identifies products and currency. Add NVDA on a Windows computer for study and office work. Test all three in your language before settling on one.

**Do these tools work in Hindi or other Indian languages?**

Some do, and some only partly. Live Caption supports Hindi on newer Pixel and selected Android phones, Lookout lists Hindi, Bengali, and Gujarati among its supported languages, and NVDA reads many Indian languages. Always test in the language you use daily, because English-only testing hides the gaps that matter.

**Is NVDA really free, or is there a hidden charge?**

NVDA is free to download for anyone, and NV Access runs it on donations and grants rather than a licence fee. There is no hidden charge for the download itself. You may still need to pay for a computer, for training, or for a Braille display if you use one, but the screen reader software is free.

**Can Live Transcribe replace a human interpreter?**

No. Live Transcribe is a live aid that turns speech into text on your phone, and it will mishear names, numbers, and fast or accented speech. For a job interview, a medical appointment, a court matter, or an exam, arrange proper human support and use the transcript only as a backup, not as the official record.

**Does Live Caption work on every Android phone in India?**

No. Live Caption is available on Pixel phones and selected Android phones, and the exact language support depends on the model. If your phone does not show it, check your manufacturer's accessibility settings and help pages. Do not install a random third-party app that claims to add Live Caption, because it may misuse your microphone.

**Is Otter.ai free forever?**

Otter.ai has a free Basic plan with 300 transcription minutes a month, which is a real free tier, not a short trial. But 300 minutes runs out quickly if you use it daily, and the paid Pro plan is priced in US dollars, so check the checkout total in rupees before subscribing. Prices and limits change, so re-check the official pricing page before paying.

**Should I pay for Speechify, or is my phone's built-in reader enough?**

Try the built-in reader first. For short daily reading, your phone's Spoken Content or Select to Speak is enough. Pay for Speechify only if you read for hours a day and the free plan's basic voices and 1.5x speed limit genuinely slow you down. Test the free plan for a week before deciding.

**What if Voiceitt does not understand my speech?**

That is a real possibility, and Voiceitt itself says it cannot promise 100 percent accuracy. Use the 30-day trial to record the required 50 phrase cards and test it on your everyday phrases. If it does not work reliably for you, do not pay — ask a speech-language pathologist about other communication aids, and keep the trial as useful information about what does and does not help.

**Are these tools safe for children?**

Most of these tools are general tools, not children's products, so a parent or teacher should set them up, check privacy settings, and supervise early use. Do not let a child share photos, recordings, or personal details through a volunteer or AI service without understanding where that data goes. For school use, ask the school what it has approved.

**Where should I start if I feel overwhelmed by all these options?**

Start with exactly one tool for your most frequent daily task. If reading labels is the daily problem, start with Seeing AI. If following conversations is the problem, start with Live Transcribe. If using a computer is the problem, start with NVDA or your phone's built-in screen reader. One tool used daily beats ten tools installed and forgotten.

## Final verdict

Start free: Be My Eyes and Seeing AI for vision, Live Transcribe and Live Caption for hearing, and NVDA on Windows — check limits before paying.
