If you practise law in India, AI can genuinely save you hours — but only if you use it for the right jobs and check its output like a sceptic. The strongest use is not asking a chatbot to tell you the law from memory. It is searching a real Indian case database, getting a first draft from your own facts, and letting software handle diary dates, client updates and invoices. The weakest use, and the one that has already caused trouble in Indian courts, is copying an AI-generated citation into a filing without opening the judgment it claims to cite.

**Important disclaimer: this article is general information only, not professional advice.** Nothing here replaces your own judgment, your bar obligations, or the primary sources themselves. Before anything is filed, advised on, or relied on by a client, verify it against the judgment, the statute, or the official record. Prices, free limits and features change often; re-check the official pricing and help page of any tool before you pay for it or promise a client a deadline based on it.

The short answer is this. For free, everyday case research, start with Indian Kanoon. For serious litigation research with citation checks, take a trial of Manupatra or SCC Online and compare them on your own matters before subscribing. For drafting, use a tool that works from facts and documents you supply, not one that invents authorities. For running the practice itself, prefer an India-built practice tool over a foreign one that does not understand GST invoicing or Indian court diaries. And for every AI output, apply one rule without exception: never cite what you have not opened and read yourself.

## Indian Kanoon: the free research baseline every Indian lawyer should know

Indian Kanoon (indiankanoon.org) is a free search engine for Indian law. It indexes judgments of the Supreme Court, the High Courts and several tribunals, along with statutes and rules, and its basic search and reading are free. It is widely used by advocates precisely because it removes the paywall from everyday research: you can find a judgment, follow the cases it cites and the cases that later cite it, and read the full text without a subscription.

Its free features are unusually generous and worth knowing in detail. The free tier gives access to the entire database of orders, judgments and laws, advanced search, cross-linking of documents through "cited by" and "cites" lists, a structural breakdown of longer judgments into facts, issues, arguments, analysis and conclusion, and 25 court-copy downloads a month. That is enough for a junior advocate, a law student, or a solo practitioner checking an authority before a hearing.

The paid side is called Prism, a suite of research and drafting aids built on the same database, including an AI legal expert, a document chat feature, draft generation and comparison, a case-prediction and counter-argument tool, and research notes. Comparison sources report Premium at around ₹5,000 a year and Pro at around ₹15,000 a year plus GST, with a free plan offering a small number of Prism credits. Treat those figures as reported, not as a quote: Indian Kanoon's own pages describe the tiers, but prices and credit allowances change, so check the site before paying.

Two cautions apply. First, Indian Kanoon is an independent resource, not a government website, so for filing purposes you still need the certified or official copy where the court or authority requires one. Second, free does not mean verified: a judgment you found for free still has to be read, and its current status — whether it has been overruled, distinguished or stayed — still has to be checked in a citator or in the later cases that cite it.

## Manupatra: deep research, AI summaries and the ManuWorks drafting toolkit

Manupatra is one of the two established commercial research databases Indian litigators rely on, alongside SCC Online. Its vendor claims cover more than 10 million legal documents, Supreme Court full text since 1950, and hundreds of Indian and international journals. For a practising lawyer, the practical difference from a free search engine is depth and trust signals: wider tribunal and journal coverage, editorial headnotes, and good-law flags that help you see whether an authority is still safe to cite.

Its published research plans, as reported from its own subscription page, run from ₹14,250 a year for a Starter plan covering the Supreme Court plus one High Court, through ₹25,700 for all High Courts, ₹31,860 with tribunals, and up to ₹55,460 for full access, with GST extra. A limited free trial is offered, reported as 24 hours of full-database access from activation. That short window means you should plan the trial: pick three live matters, run your real searches, and test the features you would actually pay for, rather than browsing casually.

The AI layer matters here. Manupatra offers AI Search, AI Compare and AI Summary features, with AI summaries sold as add-ons reported at around ₹5,000 a year each for Supreme Court or High Court coverage. Its ManuWorks.ai toolkit adds drafting and document tools: a drafter, a summary generator, OCR, query-based retrieval over your documents, a translator, document comparison and timeline building. ManuWorks pricing itself is quote-only, offered on a demo basis, so do not budget for it from a web page — ask for a written quote.

Use Manupatra when the stakes justify a paid database: a contested matter, a tribunal you do not know well, or a brief where a missed overruling would be embarrassing. Use its AI summaries as a reading aid, not a substitute for reading. An AI summary of a judgment you have not opened is exactly how a wrong proposition slips into a note.

## SCC Online AI Pro: cited answers from India's most-cited database

SCC Online is the other pillar of Indian litigation research, published by Eastern Book Company, and its AI Pro product is built to answer legal questions with citations grounded in its own database, which it describes as more than 65 lakh documents. The pitch is the right one for lawyers: instead of a chatbot answering from general training data, you get an answer tied to cases and statutes inside a database the profession already trusts.

The honest difficulty is pricing. SCC Online's own AI Pro page carries no published price, and comparison sources disagree with each other: some list Web Edition packs at around ₹33,500 and ₹49,500 a year, with AI Pro bundles reported at roughly ₹51,500 to ₹67,500 per user per year plus 18 percent GST, while other sources describe the product as quote-only. Where sources disagree like this, the safe statement is that there is no single reliable public price, and you should take a trial and a written quote before committing. The same applies to the free trial: trials are offered for the Web, Corporate and Tax editions, but no trial length is stated for AI Pro itself.

What to test on a trial is straightforward. Ask it questions from your own recent matters, where you already know the correct authorities, and check whether its citations are real, correctly attributed, and still good law. Ask it about a point where the law changed recently — for example, anything touching the new criminal codes — and see whether it cites the current provision or the repealed one. A tool that passes on your own test matters is worth paying for; a tool that sounds confident and fails on a known answer is not, at any price.

## CaseMine: AI citation analysis for lawyers who already research online

CaseMine positions itself between free search and the big commercial databases, with AI-driven citation analysis and contextual research over Supreme Court, High Court, tribunal and commission decisions. Its free core search makes it a reasonable second opinion alongside Indian Kanoon: if two independent searches surface the same authorities, your research note is stronger than if one chatbot produced a list from memory.

Reported pricing runs from around ₹12,999 to ₹41,999 a year, with monthly plans reported from around ₹1,399, plus a free trial and free student subscriptions reported at a small monthly credit allowance. As with the other commercial tools, treat these as reported figures and confirm them on the vendor's pricing page before paying. The AI features, including its AMICUS assistant, require a subscription; the free tier is for searching, not for AI analysis.

CaseMine is best used as a citation-checking habit rather than a magic answer machine. Run the case you plan to cite through its citation view, look at how later courts treated it, and read the passages where it was relied on, distinguished or negatively viewed. That one habit — checking subsequent treatment — prevents more errors than any drafting feature.

## General chatbots: powerful for drafting, dangerous for research

ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini all have free tiers in India and all work without a VPN, which is why they are tempting for legal work. Used correctly, they are genuinely useful. Give them a judgment you have already verified and ask for a summary, a chronology, or a comparison table. Give them your own facts and ask for a first draft of a letter, a notice, or a client update in plain language. Give them a dense clause and ask for a simpler explanation you can then check against the original. In each of these uses, the AI reasons over material you supplied, and you remain in control of the facts.

The dangerous use is the opposite: asking the chatbot to tell you the law from its own memory. Large language models predict plausible-sounding text; they do not retrieve verified records unless they are connected to one. That is how fake citations are born — a case name, a citation number and a paragraph that all look real but do not exist, or a real case quoted for a proposition it never decided. Indian courts have now dealt with this directly. In a 2026 Supreme Court matter concerning a customs penalty, the Court set aside a very large penalty after finding that the adjudicating authority had relied on non-existent or incorrectly cited precedents that appeared to have been generated by AI. The Andhra Pradesh High Court, in an October 2026 order, described an AI-written affidavit before it, warned advocates to be careful with the tool, and said plainly that AI may assist research and drafting but responsibility for verifying every authority and fact rests with the advocate. Commentaries on the Supreme Court's July 2026 ruling in Pooja Ramesh Singh v. Jammu and Kashmir Bank Ltd. describe the Court as adopting a zero-tolerance approach to unverified AI precedents and treating citation of such material without verification as professional misconduct. Whatever the precise wording of any single order, the direction is unmistakable: the advocate owns every citation, and "the AI said so" is no defence.

The practical workflow that follows from this is simple. Search in a real database yourself. Feed only verified material to the chatbot for analysis or drafting. Check every citation, party name, date and quoted paragraph against the original before it leaves your office. And never paste confidential client information — names, case strategy, settlement figures, privileged documents — into a free chatbot tier, where the provider's data-use terms may differ from a paid, enterprise or legal-specific product. If a matter is sensitive, use the tool's paid business tier with clear data terms, or do not use a general chatbot for it at all.

## Running the practice: client management, diary, billing and documents

Research and drafting get the attention, but most of a small firm's lost time is in administration: missed dates, unanswered clients, unbilled hours and documents scattered across email and chat. This is where practice management software earns its keep, and here the India question matters more than the brand name.

MyKase, built in India by Manupatra, is designed around Indian practice: matter management, court diary features, and billing that fits Indian practice. Sources comparing Indian legal software note it as the India-built option, in contrast to foreign tools. Because pricing is quote-based in the sources checked, take a demo with your own workflow: enter a real matter, check how a hearing date flows into reminders, and confirm that invoices carry GST details correctly before you commit.

Clio is the best-known global practice platform, with time tracking, billing, a client portal and the largest integration ecosystem in legal practice management. It is an excellent product for firms in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia. In India, however, comparison sources are clear about the gaps: no cause-list feed, no deep Indian court integration, invoicing built around foreign trust-accounting models rather than GST and SAC codes, prices quoted in dollars, and support hours set for other time zones. A large firm with staff to manage workarounds may still choose it for its ecosystem; a solo practitioner or small firm should weigh those gaps honestly against an India-built alternative.

Whatever you choose, judge it on four tests. First, does a court date entered once produce reminders, a diary entry and a client update without retyping? Second, can a client see the status of their matter and share documents through a portal, instead of chasing you on chat? Third, does billing record time, expenses and GST cleanly enough that an invoice needs no manual fixing? Fourth, can you export your matters and documents if you leave? A tool that fails the fourth test owns your practice; do not adopt it without an exit plan. ## Using AI in India: the rules that change the answer

Five India-specific points should shape every tool choice. First, the criminal codes changed. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam replaced the old codes in 2023, and any tool — or any old template — that still cites repealed sections without flagging the change is a liability. Check every criminal citation against the current code, and distrust any AI output that confidently cites the old section numbers.

Second, language matters. A large share of Indian litigation and client communication happens in Hindi and other Indian languages, and a tool that only works well in English will fail at the client-facing end. Prefer tools with genuine Hindi support — templates and interfaces written for Hindi, not machine-translated afterthoughts — and check the Hindi output as carefully as the English.

Third, confidentiality is a professional duty, not a privacy setting. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act framework and your obligations to clients both point the same way: know where a tool stores data, whether client material trains shared models, and whether a business or enterprise tier offers stronger terms than a free tier. If a vendor cannot answer those questions in writing, do not put client confidences into it.

Fourth, court infrastructure is your friend. The eCourts system, cause lists and the National Judicial Data Grid give you primary material for free, and a research tool that ignores them is weaker than one that sits alongside them. Build a habit of checking the cause list and the court website for your own matters rather than relying on any single vendor's diary feed.

Fifth, cost must be judged per matter, not per feature. A solo advocate doing a few matters a month may do perfectly well on Indian Kanoon plus a general chatbot used safely, spending nothing. A litigation chamber with daily filings may save many times a Manupatra or SCC subscription in a single avoided error. Price the tool against the work you actually do, and re-check the price before renewal, because vendors change tiers and credit allowances without notice.

## What to avoid

Avoid citing any authority you have not opened and read in a real database. Avoid pasting privileged or confidential client material into free chatbot tiers. Avoid tools that publish no price and no data terms — if a vendor will not say what it costs or where your data goes, that silence is information. Avoid large-firm products with seat minimums if you practise solo: reported figures for global legal AI products such as Harvey run into lakhs per user per year with minimum seat counts, which makes them irrelevant for most Indian solos however good they are. Avoid promising a client a result or a deadline based on a free tier you have not re-checked, because free limits change. Avoid letting an AI draft go to a client or a court without a line-by-line read, especially names, dates, amounts, limitation periods and section numbers. And avoid treating an AI summary as research: a summary is a map, not the territory, and the territory is the judgment itself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a lawyer in India use ChatGPT for legal work?**
Yes, for drafting help, summarising documents you supply, and explaining clauses in plain language — but not as a source of law. Never cite a case a chatbot produced unless you have opened that case in Indian Kanoon, Manupatra, SCC Online or the official report and confirmed it says what you claim.

**Which is the best free legal research tool in India?**
For free research, Indian Kanoon is the strongest starting point: search and reading are free, with 25 court copies a month on the free tier. For citation status and deeper coverage, a paid database such as Manupatra or SCC Online is still the professional standard on contested matters.

**Is Manupatra or SCC Online better for an Indian litigator?**
Both are established and widely relied on, and the honest answer is that it depends on your practice. Manupatra publishes its research plan prices, from around ₹14,250 to ₹55,460 a year plus GST, while SCC Online AI Pro pricing is not published on its own page and is reported only through comparison sources. Take both trials on your own live matters and choose on the results, not on marketing.

**Can AI draft a legal notice or agreement that can be filed?**
AI can produce a strong first draft from facts you supply, but a draft is not a filing. Check every party name, date, amount, limitation point and section number, confirm the current law including the new criminal codes where relevant, and have the final version reviewed before it is sent or filed. Consumer templates are a different, lower-stakes job; see the legal-documents article linked above.

**What happens if a lawyer cites a fake AI-generated case in court?**
Indian courts have treated this very seriously, including setting aside orders that rested on hallucinated authorities and warning that citing unverified AI precedents can amount to professional misconduct. The practical lesson is blunt: verify every citation in an authorised database before it reaches a judge, and keep a record of having done so.

**Do foreign practice tools like Clio work for Indian firms?**
They work for general matter, time and billing management, but comparison sources note real gaps in India: no cause-list feed, no deep Indian court integration, and invoicing not built around GST. An India-built tool such as MyKase may fit a small Indian firm better; test both on your own workflow before choosing.

**Is client data safe in free AI tools?**
Not necessarily, and you should not assume it is. Free tiers often carry different data-use terms from paid business tiers, and privileged material should never go into a tool whose terms you have not read. For sensitive matters, use a paid tier with clear written data terms, a legal-specific product, or no general chatbot at all.

**How much should a solo lawyer in India budget for legal AI?**
It can be zero to start: Indian Kanoon free plus careful use of a free chatbot tier covers a great deal. A paid research subscription, on published Manupatra figures, starts at around ₹14,250 a year plus GST, and AI add-ons cost more. Budget against the matters you actually handle, and re-check every price before paying or renewing.

## Final verdict

Start with Indian Kanoon free and a Manupatra or SCC trial, verify every citation, and keep client data out of free chatbots.

That is the whole discipline in one line. Free research covers most everyday questions. A paid database earns its fee on contested matters, where a missed overruling costs more than a year's subscription. Drafting AI earns its keep only when it works from facts and documents you verified yourself. And no tool, however expensive, transfers your responsibility to the court or the client. Use AI to work faster, check everything as if your reputation depends on it — because in this profession, it does.
