A dental clinic is a small business that runs on chairs, not just on clinical skill. An empty chair at 11 am is income lost for that hour, a patient who forgets a root-canal sitting breaks a treatment plan, and a pile of paper records, X-ray films, consent forms, and pending bills at the end of the day is what keeps many dentists working long after the last patient leaves. AI tools for dentists promise to fix all three problems at once: fill the chairs, read the X-rays, and write the records. Some of them genuinely do. Others are general clinic software with the word AI added to the brochure, or powerful American diagnostic tools priced for a large American practice and awkwardly sold into a one-chair clinic in Indore, Jaipur, or Lucknow.

This article is written for India on purpose: solo dentists, two-chair clinics, orthodontists, implant centres, and small chains, patients who message in Hindi, Hinglish, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, or English, a normal phone and laptop, and no assumption of a hospital IT team or a large monthly budget. It covers the four jobs the topic demands: appointments, X-rays, patient records, and reminders. It separates the tools that do each job well from the tools that merely claim to, and it states free limits and prices honestly, including where sources disagree or where a vendor does not publish a price at all.

The short answer is this: start with an India-built dental practice system such as [InviteAI](https://www.inviteai.in/) or [SmileBooks](https://smilebooks.in/) for appointments, dental charting, billing, and WhatsApp reminders, because they are priced in rupees, built around GST billing and WhatsApp, and offer a free trial without a credit card. Consider [epikdoc](https://pro.epikdoc.ai/) if you want voice-written chairside notes and AI-read radiographs in the same system and you are comfortable with a yearly plan. Treat X-ray AI such as [Pearl Second Opinion](https://hellopearl.com/products/second-opinion) and [Overjet](https://www.overjet.com/) as specialist diagnostic support, not as a first purchase: they are FDA-cleared in the United States for specific findings, their pricing is not publicly listed, and the dentist, not the software, remains responsible for every diagnosis. Use the free WhatsApp Business app for reminders if one person handles messages, and move to the official WhatsApp Business API only when you need automation or several staff answering from one number. Use a general chatbot such as ChatGPT or Gemini only for anonymised drafts, such as post-operative instruction sheets and patient-education handouts, and never upload a patient's name, phone number, or identifiable X-ray to one.

## InviteAI: the cheapest India-built starting point

InviteAI describes itself as a dental clinic operating system built exclusively for dental clinics in India, for solo dentists, hospitals, orthodontists, implant centres, and chains. Its feature list covers the daily work of a dental practice rather than a single clever trick: patient records with full history, allergies, dues, and treatment notes in one place, real-time appointment booking with automatic WhatsApp reminders, an FDI dental chart, AI-assisted prescriptions sent to the patient's WhatsApp, automatic GST invoicing with reminders for outstanding dues on day 3, 7, and 14, digital consent forms, post-operative instructions, and file and X-ray storage.

FDI charting deserves a word, because it is the feature that separates dental software from general clinic software. The FDI system numbers teeth by quadrant and position, so a chart entry means the same thing to every dentist who reads it. A general practice-management tool can store a note saying upper right molar, but a dental chart records exactly which tooth, which surface, and what was done, and that precision is what makes multi-sitting treatment plans, such as a root canal followed by a crown, trackable across visits. If you are comparing tools, check whether the chart is a real FDI chart or just a notes field with a tooth diagram pasted on top.

InviteAI's pricing is unusually clear for this market. The Basic plan is listed at ₹499 per month and includes unlimited patients, the FDI chart, appointments and scheduling, digital prescriptions with PDF, billing and invoices, six consent-form templates, file and X-ray storage, and post-operative instructions, but not WhatsApp automation or chairside AI. The AI + Pro plan is listed at ₹999 per month and adds WhatsApp AI automation, day 1, 3, 7, and month 6 follow-ups, a social media AI studio, chairside AI, AI lead scoring with a customer-relationship system, analytics with weekly AI insights, Google review automation, and drug safety warnings. Both plans carry a 14-day free trial with no credit card, a free setup call, and a GST invoice on request.

That pricing shape tells you who InviteAI is for. A solo dentist who mainly needs to go digital, meaning records, charting, appointments, billing, and prescriptions in one place, can start on Basic and add automation later. A clinic that loses patients between sittings, which is the classic dental problem, because a root canal, an implant, or an orthodontic plan spans months, will get more value from the AI + Pro plan's follow-up automation. One caution applies to the AI features: chairside AI in this plan is described as powered by Claude, a general large language model, and a general model writing a dental note still needs the dentist to read and correct every line before it becomes part of the record. The automation is real, but the responsibility stays with the dentist.

## SmileBooks: appointments, GST billing, and AI X-ray analysis in one Indian platform

SmileBooks is an AI-powered dental practice management platform built for Indian dental clinics, and its pitch covers the whole journey from patient intake to final billing: digital patient records with dental history, treatment plans, and medical alerts, AI-powered appointment scheduling with WhatsApp reminders and multi-chair management, GST-compliant invoicing with multiple payment modes, digital prescriptions with a drug database and dosage templates, AI-powered dental radiograph analysis for cavity detection and bone loss assessment, and a real-time analytics dashboard.

Its Starter plan is listed at ₹799 per month and includes unlimited patients, one doctor and one staff account, patient management, GST billing, digital prescriptions, appointment scheduling, 100 WhatsApp reminders per month, and cloud backup. The site also advertises a 14-day free trial with no credit card and free onboarding. Two numbers in that plan deserve attention before you buy. First, the 100 WhatsApp reminders per month: a clinic seeing ten patients a day and sending two reminders per appointment will use roughly 400 to 600 reminders a month, so the Starter allowance alone will not cover a busy practice, and you should ask what extra reminders cost before you depend on them. Second, one doctor and one staff account: a two-dentist clinic or a clinic with a receptionist and an assistant will need a higher plan, and the site should be checked for what that costs.

SmileBooks is best for a new or small clinic that wants one Indian system covering bookings, billing, prescriptions, and a first taste of AI X-ray analysis without paying American prices. It is less obviously the right pick if you need deep diagnostic AI on every radiograph, because its X-ray analysis is one feature inside a practice-management platform, whereas Pearl and Overjet below are dedicated diagnostic products with regulatory clearances and published validation. That distinction matters: a feature that highlights a possible cavity is useful, but it is not the same claim as a cleared diagnostic aid validated in a published study, and the two should not be compared as if they were.

## epikdoc: voice notes and AI-read radiographs for dentists who hate typing

epikdoc is an AI-powered clinic management system from India that makes a different promise from the two tools above: it answers calls, writes notes, and fills chairs, working on WhatsApp and inside general AI assistants. For dentists specifically, it offers chairside notes, AI-read radiographs, and multi-sitting treatment plans with invoices, all before the patient leaves the chair, plus voice-to-clinical-notes in real time, structured SOAP notes and prescriptions, WhatsApp appointment reminders, follow-ups, invoices, and lab reports, and a morning WhatsApp brief telling the doctor the day's schedule.

SOAP, for readers who have not met the term, means Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan: what the patient reports, what the dentist observes, the assessment, and the plan. It is the standard structure of a clinical note, and a system that produces it from spoken conversation saves the typing that usually happens after the patient leaves. epikdoc claims this saves doctors two or more hours a day, and that its voice system understands dental terminology without dictation training and works in the dentist's own language. Those claims are testable in one afternoon, and they should be tested: run it through real chairside conversations, including Hindi-English code-switching, and count how many corrections each note needs, especially for tooth numbers, drug names, and doses, where a fluent error looks most convincing.

epikdoc's pricing is yearly. The Starter plan is listed at ₹13,499 per year plus GST, which works out to about ₹1,125 per month, and includes up to 10 doctors and 20 staff accounts, 100 AI actions per month covering clinical notes and X-Ray IQ, with top-ups at ₹5 per action plus GST, plus WhatsApp reminders, prescriptions, reports, patient management, billing, appointment calendar, follow-up reminders, practice reports, treatment plans, patient timeline, data import, and 10 GB of secure cloud storage. The Business plan is listed at ₹22,999 per year plus GST, about ₹1,917 per month, with unlimited AI actions and 50 GB of storage. The site advertises a 30-day trial with no card required, and describes itself as DPDP-compliant and ABHA-integrated, meaning it claims to follow India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act and to connect with the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission health-record framework.

epikdoc is best for a dentist whose bottleneck is documentation time and who is comfortable committing to a yearly plan after a trial. Two cautions apply. First, the 100-action monthly limit on Starter is a real constraint: if every consultation generates a note and an X-ray analysis, a busy dentist will pass 100 actions quickly, and top-ups at ₹5 each add up, so count your monthly consultations before choosing Starter over Business. Second, DPDP-compliant and ABHA-integrated are claims to verify in writing, not labels to accept at face value: ask where consultation audio is processed, whether it is stored, for how long, whether it trains models, and how a patient's session can be deleted completely. A specific written answer is a good sign; a vague one is a reason to walk away.

## Pearl Second Opinion: dedicated AI for reading 2D X-rays

Pearl's Second Opinion is the best-known dedicated dental imaging AI, and it is a different kind of product from the practice systems above. It is FDA-cleared in the United States for real-time pathology detection on 2D dental radiographs, analysing bitewing, periapical, and panoramic images as they are captured, and highlighting up to 18 findings per image, including incipient and progressed caries, bone loss, calculus, periapical radiolucencies, defective restoration margins, and existing restorations such as crowns, fillings, bridges, root canals, and implants. Most images are processed in seconds, with over 90 percent loading in under a minute, and detections appear inside the dentist's existing imaging workflow, with native integrations for more than 40 imaging and practice-management systems.

Pearl's accuracy claims need honest handling, because sources disagree in a way that is itself instructive. Pearl's own product page describes independent third-party validation at 94 percent accuracy in detecting dental disease, while its FAQ describes over 95 percent accuracy and a published clinical study in which operator diagnostic accuracy improved from 82 percent to 98 percent with Pearl's support, with practices identifying 37 percent more disease on average. Those figures are not necessarily contradictory, since they may come from different studies, different finding sets, or different ways of counting, but they are not the same number, and a dentist should not quote either one to a patient as a promise. What matters clinically is narrower: Pearl is a concurrent-read aid, meaning the dentist reads the image with the AI's highlights visible, and the diagnostic decision remains the dentist's. It helps catch early decay and bone loss that a tired eye might miss, and its colour-coded overlays help explain findings to patients, which is genuinely valuable for case acceptance, because a patient who can see the dark area on their own X-ray understands a filling recommendation far better than a patient who is only told about it.

Pearl's pricing is not publicly listed, which is common for enterprise diagnostic software but inconvenient for a small clinic comparing options. Treat that as a reason to ask for a written quote, a trial on your own radiographs, and a clear answer on whether your imaging system is supported, because an AI that does not integrate with your sensor or panoramic unit will add manual export work to every image. Also note the regulatory point: FDA clearance is an American authorisation, not an Indian one. It is strong evidence about the product's validation, but before relying on it for patient care in India, check what Indian professional guidance and any applicable Indian regulation say, and do not present an FDA clearance to a patient as if it were an Indian approval.

## Overjet and Diagnocat: two more X-ray AI options, with different strengths

Overjet is another FDA-cleared dental AI, and comparing it with Pearl and Diagnocat shows why the choice depends on what images you actually take. Overjet holds a series of FDA clearances covering caries detection, bone level measurement, calculus detection, periapical radiolucency detection, charting assistance, and image enhancement on bitewing, periapical, and panoramic radiographs, with paediatric caries detection included in some clearances, and it added CBCT segmentation and measurement in December 2025. It integrates with common practice systems such as Dentrix, Eaglesoft, and Open Dental. Its pricing is not published, and the honest route is a demo and a written quote.

Diagnocat takes a different angle. It is strongest on CBCT, the three-dimensional scans used in implant planning, orthodontics, and oral surgery, with United States clearances for detecting periapical radiolucency on CBCT scans and for 3D viewing and segmentation, and it lists compatibility with CBCT units from manufacturers such as Dentsply Sirona, Vatech, Planmeca, and Carestream. Unlike Overjet, it sells self-serve plans at listed prices: from €499 per year for a basic subscription, with CBCT reports from €590 for six months or €990 per year. A comparison of the two products notes that no United States clearance covers Diagnocat's 2D reports, while Overjet's clearances do cover 2D findings, which is a real difference if most of your images are bitewings and periapicals rather than CBCT scans.

The practical rule for a small Indian clinic is simple. If most of your images are 2D bitewings and periapicals, Overjet or Pearl fits the work, and Diagnocat's CBCT strength is not what you need. If CBCT reporting is your main job, Diagnocat's listed self-serve pricing is an advantage, because you can budget without a sales call. In both cases, trial the tool on your own images, including images from your own sensor or panoramic unit, and check whether it handles Indian clinical realities such as heavily restored dentitions and images taken on older equipment, because a demo on clean American images proves nothing about your practice.

CareStack is included here so that a dentist who meets it in a search or a sales call can recognise it for what it is, a strong product for a different market, rather than assuming the highest price means the best fit for a one-chair clinic.

## WhatsApp reminders: the cheapest way to fill chairs

If a dental clinic can only afford one automation, appointment reminders are usually the one that pays for itself, because empty chairs are lost income and broken multi-sitting plans are lost treatments. In India, the channel matters as much as the message. SMS reminders get filtered into spam folders, email goes unread, and WhatsApp gets opened. That is why clinics that move reminders to WhatsApp typically report fewer no-shows, and why the tooling around WhatsApp deserves attention.

The simplest version costs nothing: the free WhatsApp Business app, with a business profile, saved quick replies, and manual or semi-manual reminders sent by the receptionist. It suits a clinic seeing a modest number of patients a day, where one person can send the day's reminders in one sitting. The scalable version uses the official WhatsApp Business API through a provider, with automated reminders 24 hours and 2 hours before the appointment, two-way confirmation where the patient replies Confirm or Cancel, and templates in Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, or the patient's preferred language. The API needs approved templates for messages sent outside the 24-hour customer service window, and Meta charges per delivered template message by category and country, with patient-to-clinic messages free and a limited number of free service messages per month per business number. Provider fees are separate from Meta's charges, so when comparing quotes, separate the two, or you will compare unlike with unlike.

Two rules are non-negotiable. First, use the official API or the official app, not an unofficial automation tool, because an unofficial tool risks getting the clinic's number banned, and a banned number is worse than no reminders at all. Second, only message people who gave you their number and agreed to receive messages, and keep health details out of reminders: a reminder needs the date, time, and clinic name, not the reason for the visit. A message saying your root canal appointment is tomorrow tells anyone who glimpses the patient's phone something private; a message saying your appointment at Sharma Dental Care is tomorrow at 10 am does the job without it.

Follow-up is the quieter half of this tool, and for dentists it matters more than for most clinics, because dental treatment is rarely one visit. The same WhatsApp channel can carry post-operative instructions, medicine reminders, and recall messages, such as a six-month cleaning reminder or a reminder to return for the crown after a root canal. InviteAI automates exactly this pattern, with follow-ups on day 1, 3, 7, and month 6 on its AI + Pro plan, and even without a paid tool, a simple recall list, reviewed once a week by the receptionist, will recover patients who would otherwise drift away.

## Practo and general chatbots: discovery and drafts, with fences

Patients find dentists through Practo, and a listing there is a discovery tool rather than a clinical tool. Practo's dentist pages show verified profiles, experience, consultation fees, and patient stories, and patients can book appointments with minimum wait time. For a new clinic, a complete Practo profile with accurate fees, timings, and services is a cheap form of visibility, and it pairs naturally with any booking system, because the patient who finds you still needs a reminder to actually arrive. Check Practo's current terms for clinics before relying on it, because listing and booking terms change, and a channel you do not control should never be your only source of patients.

General chatbots deserve a careful place in this article, because many dentists already use them every week, and the honest guidance is not to ban them but to fence them properly. Used well, they save real time on writing that is not patient-specific: post-operative instruction sheets, patient-education handouts explaining a root canal or an implant in plain language, referral-letter templates, and social media posts about oral hygiene. Used carelessly, they create the single most common privacy mistake in clinic AI use: uploading identifiable patient information, or an X-ray that can be linked to a patient, into a consumer tool under consumer terms that were never written for healthcare.

Under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, a dentist who pastes a patient's name, diagnosis, or contact details into a commercial chatbot has almost certainly processed personal data through a third-party system without the patient's informed, specific consent. The fix is a habit, not a purchase: draft prompts with anonymised scenarios, for example post-operative instructions for an adult patient after a routine extraction with no complications, never with a name, phone number, or any detail that identifies a real person. Keep AI prompts fully anonymised and you avoid the sharpest privacy exposure entirely. Every dose, guideline, and reference in a chatbot draft must also be checked against a source you trust before it reaches a patient, because chatbots hallucinate confidently and in fluent language, and the dentist who signs the handout owns every word in it.

## How a dental clinic in India should actually use AI

Start from your bottleneck, not from a feature list. A clinic with empty chairs needs reminders first. A clinic drowning in evening paperwork needs a voice-notes tool first. A clinic with paper registers needs a proper dental system with an FDI chart first. A clinic that already runs smoothly but misses early decay on X-rays needs diagnostic AI first. Buying all four in one week guarantees that none of them gets set up properly, and a half-set-up system is worse than a paper register your staff actually understand.

For most small dental clinics, a sensible sequence looks like this. First, fix bookings and reminders, because they pay back immediately in fewer empty chairs and fewer phone calls, and the free WhatsApp Business app or the cheapest tier of an Indian dental system is enough to start. Second, move records from paper or scattered files into one dental system, and choose that system partly on its charting, its GST billing, and its data-export terms, since records outlive any single vendor. Third, trial a voice-notes or X-ray AI tool for two weeks with real patients and real images, and measure corrections per note and findings you actually acted on before paying for a year. Fourth, use general chatbots only for anonymised drafts, and write that rule down for every staff member who touches patient communication.

Language deserves the same seriousness as price. A tool that only works well in English will fail in a clinic where patients speak Hindi, Hinglish, or a regional language, no matter how good its English demo looks. Test every tool in the languages your patients actually use, including code-switching mid-sentence, because that is how real chairside conversations sound. Similarly, test on a normal phone on a normal mobile connection, not only on a fast office laptop, because many Indian clinics run on exactly that phone, and a system that needs a desktop and a fast connection will not survive a busy morning.

Billing and payments are part of the same workflow, even though they are not strictly AI. A dental system that produces clean GST bills, tracks outstanding payments and partial payments, supports UPI-based collection, and reminds patients about dues will save more staff time than a cleverer AI feature that nobody opens. When comparing tools, ask how billing, prescriptions, lab work, and multi-sitting treatment plans connect, because a clinic that has to re-enter the same information in three places has not actually automated anything.

Staff training is the step most clinics skip, and it is the step that decides whether the tool survives its first month. One person in the clinic should own the tool, know how to add a doctor, change a template, and export data, and every receptionist should know what never goes into a public chatbot and never goes into a WhatsApp broadcast. A one-page written rule, pinned near the reception desk, beats a long policy document nobody reads.

## What to avoid

Avoid uploading patient names, phone numbers, addresses, prescriptions, reports, consent-form photographs, or identifiable X-rays into any free online tool, chatbot, translator, or document summariser that is not your clinical system. This is the most common and most avoidable mistake, and it applies even when the tool feels private, because a chat window is not a medical record, and an X-ray uploaded to a free tool is data you can no longer control.

Avoid letting any AI tool make or communicate a diagnosis on its own. AI can draft, suggest, transcribe, highlight, and summarise, but the diagnosis, the treatment plan, and the advice that reaches the patient must be the dentist's, reviewed and signed by the dentist. Be especially careful with tooth numbers, drug names, doses, and measurements, because those are exactly the details where a fluent AI error looks most convincing.

Avoid buying a year of an expensive platform before a real trial. A demo with the vendor's scripted patients and clean sample X-rays proves nothing about your accent, your specialty, or your patient mix. Insist on a trial with your own consultations and your own images, and measure the time you actually save after corrections and the findings you actually acted on, not the time or accuracy the vendor claims.

Avoid tools that will not explain, in writing, where patient data goes, how long it is kept, whether it trains models, and how you export or delete it. Avoid unofficial WhatsApp automation that risks a ban on your clinic's number. Avoid publishing AI-generated patient testimonials, synthetic patient stories, or promotional claims you have not verified, because professional guidance treats promotional AI content strictly and the dentist remains responsible for content published under their name.

Avoid assuming a free tier or a trial price will stay the same. Free limits, message allowances, and AI-action quotas change, and a clinic that builds its whole reminder system on a free allowance it outgrows in two months will face a forced upgrade at the worst moment. Budget for the paid tier you will actually need at your real patient volume, not the free tier that looked generous on day one.

Avoid a tool that locks your records in. Before signing, confirm that patients, appointments, treatment plans, X-rays, billing, and records export in a usable format, and that you can get a full data handover if you leave. Your clinic's history is not a vendor's asset, and a system that makes leaving hard is a long-term cost even if the monthly fee looks low.

Avoid choosing an American-priced platform for an Indian cash-and-UPI practice without checking the fit first. Insurance-claims machinery, dollar pricing, and integrations with American practice systems do not help a clinic whose patients pay by UPI and whose billing must be GST-compliant. The best tool is the one that fits the clinic you actually run, not the one with the longest feature list.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which AI tool should a solo dentist in India start with?**
Start with the problem that costs you the most time or money. If empty chairs are the problem, start with an Indian dental system such as InviteAI or SmileBooks plus WhatsApp reminders. If evening paperwork is the problem, trial a voice-notes tool such as epikdoc for its trial period. If missed early decay on X-rays is the problem, trial a diagnostic AI such as Pearl on your own images. Do not buy everything at once; one working tool beats three half-installed ones.

**Are AI X-ray tools accurate enough for a dental clinic?**
They can be a useful second pair of eyes, but accuracy depends on your images, your sensor or panoramic unit, and how carefully you review the output. Pearl describes independent validation at 94 percent accuracy on one page and over 95 percent on another, which shows why a single headline number should not be quoted to patients. Trial the tool on your own radiographs, check whether its highlights match your clinical judgement, and remember that the diagnostic decision is always the dentist's, never the software's.

**Can I use ChatGPT for patient-related work?**
You can use it for anonymised drafts, such as general post-operative instruction sheets, patient-education handouts, and administrative writing, provided no identifiable patient information or identifiable X-ray goes into the prompt and you review every word before use. You should not upload patient names, phone numbers, diagnoses, reports, or X-rays that can be linked to a patient into a public chatbot, and you should not let it make or communicate a diagnosis.

**What is an FDI chart, and why does it matter?**
The FDI system numbers teeth by quadrant and position, so a chart entry means the same thing to every dentist who reads it. It matters because dental treatment spans multiple sittings, and a real chart records exactly which tooth, which surface, and what was done, while a plain notes field cannot. When comparing dental software, check whether the chart is a real FDI chart or just a notes field with a diagram.

**How much should a small dental clinic budget for AI tools?**
It depends on patient volume, staff numbers, and message volume. Published snapshots in this article range from the free WhatsApp Business app at the low end, through ₹499 to ₹999 per month for InviteAI and ₹799 per month for SmileBooks, to about ₹1,125 per month for epikdoc Starter on a yearly plan, to much higher figures for American platforms such as CareStack at $829 per month and above. Budget for your real message volume and your real number of doctors, and re-check official pricing before paying, because prices and limits change.

**Do AI tools work in Hindi and regional languages?**
Some do, and some only claim to. India-built tools such as epikdoc explicitly claim to work in the dentist's own language, and WhatsApp templates can be written in Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, and other languages. Test any tool in the exact languages and code-switching your patients and staff use before paying, because a demo in clean English proves nothing about a real chairside conversation.

**Will AI replace the dentist or the receptionist?**
No, and any vendor promising that should be treated with suspicion. AI replaces typing, reminding, and re-entering information. It does not replace clinical judgement, examination, counselling, or the human conversation that makes a patient trust a clinic enough to return for the second, third, and fourth sitting of a treatment plan. The clinics that benefit most use AI to give dentists and staff more time with patients, not fewer people in the building.

**Is my clinic's patient data safe with an AI tool?**
It is safe only to the extent the vendor's contract and practices make it safe. Ask where data is processed and stored, how long audio, transcripts, and images are kept, whether data trains models, who inside the vendor can access it, and how you delete it. Prefer tools with role-based access rather than shared logins, and an audit trail of record changes. If the vendor cannot answer in writing, do not send patient data through the tool.

**What should I check before paying for any dental AI tool?**
Check the official pricing page, the free limit, what counts against message or AI-action allowances, the trial length, data-export and deletion terms, language accuracy in your own consultations, and, for X-ray AI, whether your imaging system is supported and what the regulatory position is. Run a trial with real patients and real volume, and measure time saved after corrections and findings actually acted on. Prices and limits change, so the official pricing and help page is the final authority, not this article and not a reseller.

## Final verdict

Start with InviteAI or SmileBooks for bookings and reminders, and trial X-ray AI on your own images first. Never upload identifiable patient X-rays to a free tool.

That sequence is deliberate. Bookings and reminders fix the clinic's front door and pay for themselves in fewer empty chairs and fewer broken treatment plans. A proper dental system fixes the clinic's memory, and its FDI chart, GST billing, and export terms matter more than any single clever feature. Voice notes fix the dentist's evening, but only if they survive a real trial in your languages and your specialty. X-ray AI fixes a real clinical problem, missed early findings, but only if it integrates with your imaging system and only as a second pair of eyes under the dentist's judgement. Everything else, from patient-education drafts to recall messages, is useful only inside the same rule: the dentist reviews it, the patient consented to how their data is handled, and no identifiable patient detail ever enters a tool that was not built and contracted for clinical use. Buy the tool that solves today's bottleneck, trial it honestly, and let the time and the chairs it genuinely fills decide whether it stays.
