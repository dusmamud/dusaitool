A parent's real question about AI tools is not which tool is smartest. It is which tool is safe to hand to a child, works on a normal Indian phone, costs nothing or almost nothing, and actually teaches something. Most AI apps are built for adults. They assume an account, a payment method, and a user who can judge whether the answer is wrong. A child cannot do any of that, so the rules for kids' tools are stricter: no ads aimed at children, no open chat with strangers, no surprise payment, and a parent who can see and limit what happens.

The short answer for India is this: start with [Khan Academy Kids](https://www.khanacademy.org/kids), which is completely free with no ads, and [Read Along](https://readalong.google/), Google's free reading app that works in Hindi and English and even works offline. Add [Duolingo ABC](https://abc.duolingo.com/) for early reading practice, [Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/) when your child is old enough to build things instead of only watching them, and [Google Family Link](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?hl=en_US&id=com.google.android.apps.kids.familylink) on every device before you hand it over. [Lingokids](https://lingokids.com/) is useful but mostly paid, so try its free content first and check the price at checkout before subscribing. AI bedtime stories are fun, but a parent should preview every story before bedtime, because an AI story can contain a mistake or a scary line that nobody checked.

This article is written for India on purpose: Hindi and English content, low-cost phones, patchy internet in many homes, and no assumption that you have a US account or a paid plan. It also carries a warning that matters more for kids than for any other topic: never type your child's full name, photo, school name, or address into an AI tool, and never let a young child chat freely with an open AI chatbot. The tools below are picked because they limit those risks by design, not because they promise the most features.

## Khan Academy Kids: the first app to install, and it is completely free

[Khan Academy Kids](https://www.khanacademy.org/kids) is the safest starting point in this whole list. It is made by Khan Academy, a nonprofit, together with early-learning experts from the Stanford Graduate School of Education. The app covers reading games, maths games, phonics lessons, and interactive storybooks for children aged 2 to 8, with characters like Kodi the Bear. Its own store listing says it is 100% free with no ads and no subscriptions, and its pricing page says the same: free for families and teachers.

One honest disagreement between sources: the Play Store listing says the app has over 5,000 educational games and activities, while Khan Academy's own blog says more than 4,000 learning activities and over 400 books. The exact count differs because the two pages count activities and books differently, but the important fact does not change: the library is large, and all of it is free. The app also works offline after you download books and games, which matters on a slow connection or a long car trip. For older children who have outgrown the Kids app, the same organisation's [Khan Academy India](https://india.khanacademy.org/) site covers school subjects, which is covered later in this article.

A practical rule for parents: let the child play in the Kids app without an account struggle, and sit with them for the first week. You will quickly see which games they enjoy and where they get stuck, and that tells you more than any progress chart.

## Read Along by Google: reading aloud in Hindi and English, even offline

[Read Along](https://readalong.google/) is Google's reading app, and it is the best fit in this list for Indian homes. A child reads aloud, and a reading buddy on screen gives pronunciation help in real time when the child gets stuck. The app is available at no charge as a mobile app and also online, and it is designed to run well on low bandwidth and offline. That combination — free, offline, low data — is exactly what a home with one shared phone needs.

Language is where Read Along beats most kids' apps. Its help page lists Hindi, English, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Gujarati, Bangla, and Urdu among its supported languages, so a child can practise reading in their home language and in English in the same app. Teachers can also use Read Along inside Google Classroom, and its stories can be generated with Gemini — but Google itself says those Gemini-generated stories are a writing aid that educators should review, customise, and approve before giving them to students. The same caution applies at home: if a story was made by AI, read it once yourself first.

If your child is learning English, this article on [AI tools for learning English in India](/blog/ai-tools-for-learning-english-india/) covers speaking and pronunciation tools for older learners as well.

## Duolingo ABC: free phonics for early readers

[Duolingo ABC](https://abc.duolingo.com/) is Duolingo's reading app for young children, and its homepage states the key fact plainly: it is 100% free. It teaches reading through play, with a phonics curriculum based on recommendations by the National Reading Panel. Children learn letter sounds and decoding in bite-sized lessons, practise writing lowercase and capital letters, and learn sight words — common tricky words like "one" and "was" that cannot be sounded out letter by letter.

Duolingo ABC works best as a short daily habit, not a long session. Ten to fifteen minutes of phonics games, followed by a real book, beats an hour of tapping. It pairs naturally with Read Along: Duolingo ABC teaches the sounds, and Read Along gives the child real books to practise those sounds in. Neither app asks you to pay to unlock the basics, which is why both are in the free-first list in this article.

## Lingokids: good songs and games, but check the price before you pay

[Lingokids](https://lingokids.com/) is a popular early-learning app for children aged 2 to 8, with songs, games, and activities built around English learning. It has free content, which is worth trying, but its full version, Lingokids Plus, is paid. According to Lingokids' own help centre, Plus starts with a 7-day free trial, and after that the website price is $13.49 a month or $161.88 a year in US dollars. The same help page warns that the price differs if you subscribe through the App Store, Google Play, Amazon, or Huawei, and that it varies by country because of local taxes and currency conversion.

That warning matters in India: the price you see at checkout in rupees is the price that applies to you, not the dollar price quoted on the website. So the honest advice is simple. Let your child try the free content first. If they love it and ask for more, check the price shown in the app at checkout, set a reminder before the 7-day trial ends, and only then decide. Do not let a trial quietly turn into a monthly payment, and do not buy a yearly plan in the first month. A free trial is a test, not a commitment.

## Khan Academy India: free school learning for Grade 1 to 12

When your child outgrows the Kids app, [Khan Academy India](https://india.khanacademy.org/) is the next step, and it is also 100% free. Its homepage says it offers free content for Grade 1 to 12, aligned to the India curriculum, with maths foundation courses for Classes 6 to 10 and content in Hinglish as well as English. That India alignment is the point: the lessons follow the syllabus your child actually studies, instead of a US grade system you have to translate in your head.

This is the tool for homework help with a parent nearby. If your child is stuck on a maths concept, find the matching lesson, watch it together, and then let the child try the practice questions. ## Scratch: free coding for children aged 8 to 16

[Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/) is a project of the Lifelong Kindergarten Group at the MIT Media Lab, and its about page says it plainly: Scratch is provided free of charge. It lets children program their own interactive stories, games, and animations by snapping blocks together instead of typing code, and share their creations with others in the online community. It is designed especially for ages 8 to 16, though people of all ages use it.

Scratch is the best answer to the complaint that children only consume screens. A child who makes a game in Scratch has to think about the story, the rules, and what happens when something goes wrong — that is real learning, and it is free. One safety note: Scratch has an online community where children can share projects and see other people's projects. For a younger child, keep sharing off at first, or only share with a parent's help, and talk about the rule that applies everywhere online: never share your real name, school, photo, or address. For families thinking about how teachers use these tools at school, [AI tools for teachers in India](/blog/ai-tools-for-teachers-india/) covers classroom tools and their free limits.

## AI bedtime stories: fun, but preview every story first

AI bedtime story generators are the newest category in this list, and they are genuinely delightful when used well. As an example, [KidsStoryteller.ai](https://kidsstoryteller.ai/ai-bedtime-story-generator) offers over 120 original bedtime stories for ages 3 to 10, with narrated voices, and says its stories are free to start with no credit card and an MP3 download included. A parent can pick a story, choose a voice, and play it at bedtime, or download it for offline use.

Here is the honest limit: the page says "free to start" but does not clearly state how many stories are free or where the free part ends. That is not a reason to avoid it, but it is a reason not to promise your child unlimited stories. Try the free stories first, and check the site's pricing before you pay for anything. More important than price is previewing: AI stories are generated, not edited by a human, so a story can contain a factual mistake, a confusing line, or something too scary for a small child. Read or listen to a story once yourself before bedtime. The five minutes you spend previewing is the safety check no app can replace.

## Google Family Link: the free control every parent should set up first

[Google Family Link](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?hl=en_US&id=com.google.android.apps.kids.familylink) is Google's free parental controls app, and it is the tool that makes all the others safe to use. With Family Link, a parent can set daily screen-time limits, set school time and downtime schedules, set individual app time limits, block apps, require approval before a child installs a new app, and manage content filters on Google services like Chrome, Play, YouTube, and Search. You can also see a child's device location when the device is on and connected, and get notifications about where they are.

Two honest limits from Google's own pages. First, Family Link's tools vary depending on the child's device, and screen-time limits work on Android 7 and above and on Chromebooks. Second, and more important, Google says clearly that Family Link does not make the internet safe: it cannot judge the content on the internet, it only gives parents the chance to decide how their child spends time on a device. That is why Family Link works best together with the choices in this article — free, ad-free learning apps first, and open chatbots and random video apps kept out. [YouTube Kids](https://support.google.com/youtubekids/answer/10495678?hl=en-IN), for example, is managed through Family Link, where a parent can choose a content level setting and decide whether the child can use search inside the app. For the wider question of keeping children safe online, including passwords, VPNs, and breach checking, see [AI privacy and safety free tools in India](/blog/ai-privacy-safety-free-tools-india/).

## Using kids AI tools in India: language, payment, and data

Language comes first. A tool that only works in English will fail a child who thinks in Hindi, Tamil, or Telugu. Read Along supports Hindi and several other Indian languages, Khan Academy India offers Hinglish content, and Khan Academy Kids and Duolingo ABC are English-first but use pictures, sounds, and games that a young child can follow with a parent nearby. If a tool does not offer your home language, use it together with the child for the first weeks instead of leaving the child alone with it.

Payment comes second. Every paid tool in this list shows its price in dollars on its website or varies the price by app store and country. Never trust a dollar price as the Indian price. Check the price at checkout in rupees, check whether a trial converts automatically, and set a reminder before any trial ends. The free tools in this list — Khan Academy Kids, Read Along, Duolingo ABC, Khan Academy India, Scratch, and Family Link — need no payment at all, which is why they are the core recommendation.

Data comes third, and it is the strictest rule. A child's data deserves more care than an adult's. Use official apps from the Play Store or App Store, not copied APK files from random websites. Do not enter a child's full name, photo, school, or address into any AI tool, including story generators and chatbots. Prefer tools that state they do not show ads to children and do not sell data. And keep the device itself in a shared room for young children, so screen time is visible, not hidden in a bedroom with the door closed.

## What to avoid

Avoid open AI chatbots for young children. A chatbot will answer any question confidently, including questions about health, relationships, or scary topics, and it can invent facts without warning. If an older child uses a chatbot for homework, the rule should be: the chatbot explains, the child writes, and a parent or teacher checks the facts. Never let a child paste homework into a chatbot and submit the answer as their own work without understanding it.

Avoid apps that mix learning with shopping. If an app shows ads, offers in-app purchases on every screen, or locks basic lessons behind a payment wall after a few days, delete it and go back to the free list in this article. A child cannot tell an ad from a lesson, so an ad-free environment is a baseline, not a bonus. Avoid also any "AI tutor" that asks for a child's photo or voice recording without explaining why, and avoid tools that promise exam results or admission. No app can promise that, and any tool that does is selling hope, not learning.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best completely free learning app for kids in India?** Khan Academy Kids is the strongest answer: 100% free, no ads, no subscriptions, for ages 2 to 8, with reading, maths, phonics, and stories. Read Along is the second free essential, especially for Hindi reading practice, because it works offline.

**Can my child learn Hindi with these tools?** Yes, best with Read Along, which supports Hindi along with Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Gujarati, Bangla, and Urdu. Khan Academy India also offers Hinglish content for school subjects. Khan Academy Kids and Duolingo ABC are mainly English, so use them together with Hindi books and conversation at home.

**Are AI bedtime stories safe for small children?** They can be, if a parent previews each story first. AI stories are generated, not human-edited, so check for mistakes or scary content before bedtime. Never enter your child's name, photo, or school into a story generator.

**How much screen time is right for a child?** There is no single number that fits every family, and no app can decide it for you. Use Family Link to set a daily limit and a downtime schedule, keep learning apps unlimited if you want, and keep the device in a shared room. Short, regular sessions with a parent nearby beat long unsupervised sessions.

**Should I pay for Lingokids or stick to free apps?** Try the free content first. If your child loves it, check the price at checkout in rupees, remember the 7-day trial converts to a paid plan, and set a reminder before the trial ends. Most families do not need a paid kids app in the first month, because Khan Academy Kids, Read Along, and Duolingo ABC already cover reading and early maths for free.

**Is Scratch safe for my child to use online?** Scratch itself is free and made by MIT for ages 8 to 16, and it teaches real coding. Its online community is the part that needs supervision: keep sharing off for younger children, or share only with a parent's help, and never share a real name, school, photo, or address.

## The final verdict, and a checklist before you pay

Start with Khan Academy Kids and Read Along: both free, with Hindi reading on Read Along. Add Family Link before you hand over any device. That trio covers reading, maths, and safety without spending anything. Add Duolingo ABC for phonics practice, Khan Academy India for school homework, and Scratch when your child is ready to build games instead of only playing them. Try Lingokids free content before paying, and preview every AI bedtime story yourself.

Before you pay for anything, run this checklist. First, has your child used the free version for at least two weeks and still asked for more? Second, have you checked the price at checkout in rupees, not dollars? Third, does the trial convert automatically, and have you set a reminder before it ends? Fourth, does the app show ads to children or ask for your child's photo or full name? If any answer worries you, stay with the free tools. They are not a compromise — for most Indian families, they are the complete answer.
