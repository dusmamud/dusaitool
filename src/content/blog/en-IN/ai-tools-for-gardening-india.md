Gardening in India usually starts with a simple question, not with a shopping list. What is this plant growing in the pot, why are its leaves turning yellow, and when should it be watered again? A plant bought from a nursery often comes with no name tag, a cutting from a neighbour comes with no instructions, and a plant found on a walk comes with no warning about whether it is safe for children or pets. AI gardening tools answer the first question from a photo, and the better ones also handle the second and third: they diagnose visible problems, and they remind you to water, feed, and repot at the right time.

The direct answer for India is this: **start free with Google Lens for instant identification and Pl@ntNet for a second opinion, and add Seek by iNaturalist if children are involved.** All three are free, all three work in India without a VPN, and none of them requires a subscription to identify a plant. If you want care reminders for many plants, **Planta** is the strongest free starting point, because its basic schedule is free and its watering reminders use local weather data. **PictureThis** is the best all-in-one paid option, with disease diagnosis and a large species database, but check its price inside the app before paying, because sources disagree on what it costs. **PlantSnap** and **Blossom** are useful alternatives for identification and care guides, but neither beats the free combination above for a beginner on a budget.

One warning applies to every tool in this article, and it matters more here than in most tool categories: **never eat, touch, or give medicine based only on an app's identification.** Apps identify from a photo, and a photo can mislead them. A vendor claim of 98 percent accuracy is a marketing claim, not a guarantee for your plant, in your light, in your photo. If a plant might be toxic, or if you are unsure whether something is edible, confirm with a local nursery, a botanist, or a reliable reference before acting. The apps are excellent for naming and care, and they are not a substitute for expert advice on safety.

This article is written for India on purpose: balcony and terrace gardeners in flats, people with a few pots and a busy schedule, beginners who cannot tell a money plant from a pothos, and families growing tulsi, curry leaves, mint, chillies, tomatoes, hibiscus, and marigold. Prices and limits change often, so **re-check the official pricing and help page inside the app before paying or promising a deadline.** Nothing here is a paid recommendation, and no tool paid to appear in this list.

## Google Lens: the free identification tool already on your phone

Google Lens is the fastest free way to identify a plant in India, because most Android phones already have it. It is built into the Camera app on some phones, into the Google app, and into Google Assistant, and Google's own India page lists identifying plants and animals as one of its uses. There is no subscription for identification, no separate download on many phones, and no account beyond the Google account most phones already use.

Using it is simple: point the camera at the plant, tap the Lens icon, and look at the matches. Lens shows visually similar images and pages, and from those you can usually read the plant's common name and botanical name. It works best on a clear photo of a leaf, a flower, or the whole plant in good light. It works worst on a blurry photo, a plant shot from far away, or a plant with no flower or distinctive leaf in the frame.

Lens has two honest limits. First, it is a general visual search tool, not a gardening app, so it gives no watering schedule, no disease diagnosis, and no care reminders. Second, its answer is a set of matches, not a single confident identification, so you sometimes have to judge between two or three similar plants yourself. That is exactly why this article pairs it with Pl@ntNet: when Lens and Pl@ntNet agree, you can be reasonably confident; when they disagree, treat the plant as unidentified and ask a nursery or a community before acting.

## Pl@ntNet: the free second opinion from a science project

Pl@ntNet, written as Pl@ntNet identify, is a citizen-science project that identifies plants from photos. Its own statistics page reports 76 floras, more than 87,000 species, and more than 9.6 million accounts, with identifications built from observations submitted by users and checked by the community. Identification is free, and the project exists for research and education rather than for selling subscriptions.

For an Indian gardener, Pl@ntNet has three strengths. It is free, so it costs nothing to get a second opinion on every plant Lens names. It asks you to photograph different parts of the plant — leaf, flower, fruit, bark — and it gives results per part, which teaches you what to photograph for a better identification. And it shows which species other people have recorded near you, which helps with common Indian garden plants as well as wild plants seen on a walk.

Its limits are the limits of any community project. Rare or very local plants may have few observations, and a plant that is common in India but poorly photographed may return a less confident match. The app is also more educational than decorative: it does not send watering reminders or manage a plant collection the way Planta does. Use it for what it is best at — a free, careful second identification — and use a care app for reminders.

## Seek by iNaturalist: free, private, and good for children

Seek by iNaturalist is the family-friendly option in this list. It uses image recognition to identify plants, animals, and fungi, and it turns identification into badges and monthly challenges, which makes it genuinely enjoyable for children. Its official page states the two facts that matter most for parents: **no registration is involved, and no user data is collected**, and your precise location is never stored or sent to iNaturalist, even though the app asks for location permission to suggest species from your general area.

Seek is free, and it works without an account, which also makes it a good choice for a shared family phone or a school activity. Its identification is broader than a gardening app's: it will name a bird, a mushroom, and a weed with equal enthusiasm, and it is less focused on care instructions for a potted plant. That is a fair trade for a free, private tool. Use Seek to explore and learn, especially with children, and use a dedicated care app when the question is not "what is this?" but "what does this plant need this week?"

## PictureThis: the strongest all-in-one, but check the price first

PictureThis is the best-known plant identification app, and its App Store page describes why: it claims to identify more than 400,000 plant species, offers automatic disease diagnosis from a photo, gives care instructions including watering schedules and light conditions, warns about toxic plants, identifies weeds, tracks watering, and measures light with a light meter. For a gardener with one or two problem plants, the disease diagnosis alone can be worth more than a generic search, because it gives a step-by-step treatment plan rather than just a name.

The free part is real: the app is listed as free with in-app purchases, and identification is available without paying. The paid part is where sources disagree, and this article states that disagreement plainly. The App Store page lists PictureThis Premium as a subscription but does not give a single clear price in the listing this article checked, while user reviews report around 30 dollars a year and mention a 7-day free trial. Prices also vary by country, plan, and offer. **Do not trust any single price quoted here or elsewhere: open the app, read the premium screen, and check whether a free trial will auto-renew before you start it.** If the price in your app is higher than you expected, the free combination of Lens and Pl@ntNet covers identification, and Planta covers reminders, without paying anything.

PictureThis also has a weakness shared by every identification app: its accuracy claim of over 98 percent is the vendor's own claim, measured on its own data, not an independent test on Indian balcony plants. Treat its disease diagnosis as a strong suggestion, not a prescription, and if a plant keeps getting worse, take a sample or a photo to a nursery rather than trusting a second automated diagnosis.

## Planta: the best free care reminders for many plants

Planta is the care app in this list, and its strength is not identification but routine. Its official site promises an individual care schedule and reminders for each plant, with step-by-step guides, identification, and a light meter, and it reports more than 7 million plant lovers using it. Its help centre states plainly that the app is free to download and use, and that you will not be charged simply for downloading it or creating an account. Premium is a separate subscription, offered on monthly, 3-month, and 12-month plans inside the app.

What makes Planta's reminders better than a phone alarm is that they are not fixed dates. Its smart algorithms use more than 30 parameters, along with local climate and weather data, to tell you when it is actually time to water, and it also reminds you to fertilise, mist, repot, and clean. That matters in India, where a watering schedule that works in a cool January fails in a hot May, and where monsoon humidity changes everything. A fixed "water every three days" rule over-waters in the rains and under-waters in summer; a weather-aware reminder adapts.

One disagreement to state plainly: Planta's official help pages do not list a price, while a third-party review reports Premium from 7.99 dollars a month on the App Store. Treat the third-party figure as an indication only, and check the price inside your app before subscribing. For most beginners, the free schedule is enough for a handful of pots, and Premium becomes worth considering only when you have many plants, or when you want the full step-by-step guides for every plant.

## PlantSnap: a huge database, with prices that vary

PlantSnap identifies more than 600,000 plants, including flowers, trees, succulents, and cacti, and it reports more than 50 million users across more than 200 countries. Its App Store page describes instant identification from a photo, detailed growing and care instructions, a personal library of discoveries, and a community where users share photos and tips. For a gardener who likes collecting and comparing, the library and community are genuinely pleasant features.

PlantSnap is free to download with in-app purchases, and its App Store listing shows several premium prices: a monthly subscription at 2.99 dollars, a yearly subscription at 21.99 dollars, a lifetime access option at 29.99 dollars, and another yearly subscription at 32.99 dollars. Those different yearly prices are not a mistake in this article; they reflect different plans, offers, or regions shown in the listing. **Check the price shown in your own app, in rupees, before paying, and remember that a lifetime plan only makes sense if you will use the app for years.** For pure identification, the free tier plus Pl@ntNet as a second opinion is enough, and PlantSnap's paid plans are best seen as paying for an ad-free library and community, not for the identification itself.

## Blossom: identification plus a care guide

Blossom is a plant care guide app with leaf and flower identification, listed as free with in-app purchases on the App Store, where it holds a 4.6 rating from about 69,000 ratings. It sits in the same category as PictureThis and PlantSnap: identify a plant, read its care guide, and follow its advice. It is a reasonable alternative if you try PictureThis or PlantSnap and dislike their interface or their prices, and its care guides are written for beginners rather than for botanists.

Blossom's limits are the same as its rivals'. The free tier is enough for occasional identification, the full guide and extra features sit behind premium, and the exact premium price is shown inside the app rather than on a single public pricing page. There is no reason to pay for two identification apps at once: pick one paid app if you want one, or use the free combination in this article and keep your money for pots, soil, and plants.

## Using AI tools in India: language, payment, and no VPN needed

All seven tools in this article work in India without a VPN. They are available on Google Play and the App Store in India, and premium payments go through Google Play or Apple, where Google Play supports UPI and other Indian payment methods. You do not need a foreign account, a foreign card, or any workaround to use them.

Language is the real India limit, not availability. Most of these apps work in English and give plant names in English and Latin, while many Indian gardeners know plants by Hindi or regional names: tulsi, curry patta, pudina, sadabahar, gudhal, genda. An app that names your plant "Ocimum tenuiflorum" has correctly identified tulsi, but you still need to connect the two names yourself. A practical habit is to save the English and botanical name the app gives, and then search that name together with the Hindi name you know, so that care advice written for Indian conditions is easier to find. Balcony and terrace gardening has its own rules that no app replaces. Pots need drainage holes, because a pot without drainage drowns roots in monsoon rain. Water thoroughly until water runs from the drainage holes, then wait until the top layer of soil is dry before watering again, and check with a finger: push a finger about 2.5 centimetres into the soil, and water only if it feels dry. In peak summer, a terrace pot can dry out in a day, while the same pot in monsoon may not need water for days, which is why a weather-aware reminder like Planta's is more useful than a fixed schedule. A simple moisture meter, sold for roughly 200 to 500 rupees in garden shops and online, is a cheap backup if you are unsure. ## What to avoid

**Do not eat or touch a plant based only on an app's answer.** This is the most important rule in this article. Identification apps make mistakes, especially with similar-looking species, and the difference between an edible plant and a toxic lookalike can be small. If a child or pet may come into contact with the plant, confirm the identification with a second source before deciding it is safe.

**Do not trust a disease diagnosis blindly.** An app can name a likely problem from a yellow leaf, but yellow leaves have many causes: over-watering, under-watering, low light, nutrient deficiency, and pests can all look similar in a photo. If the plant does not improve after the app's suggested treatment, or if many plants are affected at once, ask a nursery or an experienced gardener in person.

**Do not start a free trial without checking auto-renewal.** Most premium trials renew automatically unless you cancel before the trial ends, and the renewal charge appears without a second warning. If you start a trial, set a reminder to decide before it ends, and cancel in the Play Store or App Store subscription settings if you do not want to continue.

**Do not water on a schedule alone.** An app's reminder is a prompt, not a measurement. Always check the soil with a finger first, and skip watering if the soil is still moist, especially during monsoon. Over-watering kills more balcony plants in India than under-watering, because pots hold water and roots rot in standing water.

**Do not pay for two identification apps.** Identification is a commodity now: Lens, Pl@ntNet, and Seek all do it free. Pay only for something the free tools do not give you, such as Planta's smart reminders for many plants, or one all-in-one app's disease diagnosis. If you are sharing your garden online, the [AI tools for social media in India](/blog/ai-tools-for-social-media-india/) cover captions, scheduling, and posting, which is a separate job from plant care.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which is the best free plant identification app in India?** Google Lens for instant identification, because it is already on most Android phones and costs nothing, with Pl@ntNet as a free second opinion. If both agree, the identification is reasonably reliable. If they disagree, treat the plant as unidentified.

**Can these apps identify Indian plants like tulsi, curry leaves, and hibiscus?** Yes, common garden plants are well covered, because they are widely photographed and widely grown. The apps usually return English and botanical names, so you may need to match the botanical name to the Hindi or regional name yourself.

**Are plant identification apps accurate enough to trust?** Accurate enough for naming and general care, not accurate enough for safety decisions. Vendor accuracy claims are measured on the vendor's own data. Never decide that a plant is safe to eat or touch based only on an app.

**Do I need to pay for watering reminders?** Not for a few pots: a phone alarm plus the finger test works. Planta's free schedule covers basic reminders, and its paid plans make sense mainly when you have many plants or want weather-aware scheduling for all of them.

**Which app is best for balcony gardening in India?** Planta for reminders, because its watering advice uses local weather data, which matters across Indian summer and monsoon. Pair it with Google Lens or Pl@ntNet for identification, and remember that no app replaces checking drainage, sunlight, and soil yourself.

**Can children use these apps safely?** Seek by iNaturalist is the safest choice for children, because it needs no registration and collects no user data, and it turns identification into badges and challenges. Other apps need an account or a subscription screen, so set them up yourself before handing the phone to a child.

## The final verdict, and a checklist before you pay

Start free, and pay only for a specific job. Identify plants with Google Lens and confirm with Pl@ntNet, explore with Seek if children are involved, and use Planta's free schedule for reminders. Consider PictureThis if you want disease diagnosis in one app, and PlantSnap or Blossom if you prefer their libraries or guides. Before paying for anything, check the price inside your own app, confirm whether a free trial auto-renews, and cancel the trial if you do not want it.

Before you pay, run this checklist: have you tried the free tier for a week? Does the paid feature do something the free tools do not? Is the price shown in rupees in your app, and does it match what you expected? Have you set a reminder before the trial ends? And most importantly, have you checked the soil with your finger today? The best gardening tool in India is still a gardener who looks at their plants every day, and these apps are at their best when they support that habit rather than replacing it.
