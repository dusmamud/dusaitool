A cloud kitchen in India is a restaurant without tables. There is no dining room to fill, no waiter to take orders, and no customer walking in to ask what is good today. Everything arrives through a phone screen: an order on Swiggy, an order on Zomato, a WhatsApp message from a regular customer, and a menu that has to sell food nobody can smell or taste before buying. That makes a cloud kitchen a software business as much as a food business. The kitchen that wins is rarely the one with the longest menu. It is the one whose orders land in one place, whose menu reads clearly on a small phone, whose prices survive delivery commission, and whose regular customers come back without paying commission again.

The short answer for India is this: use [UrbanPiper](https://www.urbanpiper.com/hub) to pull Swiggy, Zomato and other online orders into one dashboard, because juggling separate tablets for each app is where missed and delayed orders begin. Use [Petpooja](https://www.petpooja.com/poss/pricing) when billing, kitchen display and online ordering need one paid system, because it is built for Indian restaurants with a central kitchen module and dozens of reports. Sell through the Swiggy Partner and Zomato Restaurant Partner apps, because that is where delivery demand already lives, but price for their commission before you list, not after. Use [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) free for menu descriptions, dish names and cost drafts, and Canva free for menu cards and festival posters, because those two cover almost all the words and pictures a small kitchen needs. Use [WhatsApp Business](https://whatsappbusiness.com/products/business-app-features/) free for repeat orders and offers to your own customers, because a direct order has no aggregator commission. Track every rupee in Google Sheets until the kitchen is big enough to need paid software for that job too.

This article is written for India on purpose: Hindi, Hinglish and English customers, UPI payments, FSSAI and GST realities, a normal phone and laptop, and no assumption that you have a paid plan, an agency, or a US account. It also carries a warning that matters more than any tool recommendation: prices and free limits change often, so re-check the official pricing or help page of any tool before you pay for it or promise a deadline around it. Where sources disagreed during the checks for this article, that disagreement is stated openly rather than smoothed over. This article is general information only, not professional financial, tax or legal advice, and official sources and a qualified professional must be checked before acting on licences, tax or pricing decisions.

## UrbanPiper: one dashboard for every delivery order

UrbanPiper's Hub is built for exactly the problem cloud kitchens face first: orders arriving on several apps at once. Its own description says it integrates online sales channels such as Zomato, Swiggy, UberEats and Amazon Food into an existing restaurant POS system, and offers online menu management, order tracking, stock management and reporting. More than 40,000 businesses use it, according to the same page, and it is web based, so it needs no extra hardware beyond the devices a kitchen already has. For a cloud kitchen, the practical benefit is simple: staff watch one screen, accept orders faster, and spend less time retyping the same order from a tablet into a billing system.

The second benefit matters just as much, and it is menu management. Change a price, mark a dish out of stock, or switch off a slow item once in UrbanPiper, and the change can flow to the connected channels instead of being updated separately on Swiggy, then Zomato, then your own list. That single point of control prevents the classic cloud kitchen embarrassment: a customer orders a dish that finished an hour ago, the order is cancelled, and the kitchen's rating drops for a mistake a person did not even make. UrbanPiper's help pages also describe the onboarding honestly: the outlet must already exist on the aggregator, the menu and taxes must be ready, and activation involves a mapping step with the aggregator's team, with the outlet expected to go live within about 48 hours of a correct request. That is a planning detail, not a surprise to discover on launch day.

UrbanPiper is a paid integration, and its price depends on your setup, so get a quote for your actual order volume and number of outlets before budgeting. Do not buy it on day one if you have only one channel and ten orders a day; a single tablet and a spreadsheet can carry that load. Buy it when missed orders, delayed acceptance or menu mismatches across apps start costing you ratings and refunds. That is the point where one dashboard pays for itself. ## Petpooja: billing, kitchen display and reports in one paid system

Petpooja is an Indian point-of-sale system, and its pitch fits a cloud kitchen that has outgrown a notebook and a calculator. Its pricing page lists plans from Basic through Core, Growth and Scale, with features that include cloud billing, an inventory module, more than 80 reports, an in-built customer system, unlimited terminals and user rights, a central kitchen module, multi-terminal billing and a kitchen display system. It also claims more than 10,000 customers and says a large share of online orders on Zomato and Swiggy are processed through it. The central kitchen module is the feature to notice: a cloud kitchen often runs several brands from one kitchen, and a system that keeps their billing, stock and reports separate while the cooking stays shared is worth more than a prettier bill format.

The honest way to judge Petpooja is against the jobs it replaces. If your kitchen takes orders on two apps, bills walk-in or phone customers separately, tracks stock on paper, and discovers at month end that it does not know its best-selling dish, a POS with 80-plus reports earns its fee by answering questions you are currently guessing at. Which dish makes money after commission, which hour is dead, which item is always out of stock on Sundays: those are report questions, and they decide the menu more reliably than any founder's favourite dish. Petpooja's own pricing note says local pricing may vary by region and currency, so treat any price you read second-hand as stale and ask for the current quote for your city and plan.

Do not buy the biggest plan first. Start with the plan that covers billing, inventory and online orders, and upgrade only when a second outlet, a central kitchen or a loyalty programme creates a job the smaller plan cannot do. A POS is a monthly cost that continues in slow months, so the test before paying is blunt: will this system save staff time or recover lost orders every week, in amounts larger than its fee? If the answer is not yet, Google Sheets and the aggregator apps carry you further for free.

## Swiggy and Zomato partner apps: where the customers already are

No honest cloud kitchen guide can pretend otherwise: in most Indian cities, Swiggy and Zomato bring the customers. Listing on them is not optional for a new delivery-only brand, because customers search there first and trust the ratings, delivery time and refund process they already know. The partner apps are how a kitchen manages that presence: menus, prices, availability, preparation time, offers and order status, all from a phone. Learn them properly before launch, because a wrong preparation time or a dish left live when stock is over is a rating problem, not a software problem.

The cost of these channels is the number every cloud kitchen founder must do before pricing a menu. Industry reporting puts Swiggy and Zomato commissions roughly in the range of 15 to 30 percent of every order, plus GST on the commission, with the exact figure varying by city, brand and contract, and with some founders negotiating lower introductory rates tied to order volume. Take a simple example from that same reporting: a biryani sold at 150 rupees, with 80 rupees of raw material, 10 rupees of packaging and a 20 percent commission of 30 rupees, leaves only about 24 to 25 rupees before rent, salaries and electricity. That example is an illustration, not a promise about your kitchen, but it shows why pricing from food cost alone fails. Price from contribution margin: what is left after raw material, packaging, commission and GST on commission, for every single dish. Any dish that is negative after that maths should be repriced, resized or removed, however popular it looks.

Treat aggregator ads the same way. Paid placement can lift a new listing, but paying for clicks before ratings, photos and preparation times are stable is paying to show strangers a kitchen that is not ready. Fix the menu, the packaging and the on-time rate first, then spend on visibility, and measure every rupee of ad spend against orders it actually produced.

## ChatGPT: menu descriptions, dish ideas and cost drafts

ChatGPT is the first free AI tool to try because a cloud kitchen needs words constantly: dish descriptions for Swiggy and Zomato, a shorter name that fits a phone screen, a reply to a review, a festival combo offer, and a WhatsApp broadcast to regular customers. Give it your ingredient list and ask for a 20-word description, then fix the price, spice level, portion and veg or non-veg mark yourself. Ask it to draft ten combo names from your five best dishes, then pick one and discard the rest. Ask it to turn a long recipe costing sheet into a per-plate cost table, then check every number against your actual purchase bills, because it cannot know what you paid for paneer this week.

One honest disagreement surfaced during the checks for this article, and it is worth stating plainly. News reports on the ChatGPT free tier disagreed on the exact message limit, with one reporting 10 messages per hour and another 10 messages within a five-hour window, before switching to a smaller model, and both agreeing that free users get only one advanced thinking message a day. OpenAI's own help page says the free tier's default model and available limits can change over time. The safe conclusion is that the free tier is enough for daily menu writing, but it is not a foundation to promise deadlines on. Do the writing in batches, keep your own copies of every final menu text, and never build a launch plan that assumes a particular free limit will still exist next month.

Never paste customer phone numbers, addresses or order details into any AI chat tool. A menu draft is fine; customer data is not. And never let AI invent a dish you do not serve, a portion you do not give, or an ingredient that is not in the food. A customer who receives something different from the description will leave the review that every future customer reads first.

## Canva: menu cards, posters and offer graphics without a designer

Most cloud kitchens cannot afford a designer for every festival, combo and price change, and that is where Canva earns its place. Its free tier covers menu cards, rate cards, offer posters and social posts from templates, which is exactly the set of pictures a delivery-only brand needs. The practical way to use it is to build one template and reuse it: your brand name, phone number and ordering link on every card, veg and non-veg marks large and clear, prices in a font big enough to read on a phone screenshot, and the offer, dates and price as the biggest text on any poster. Customers decide in two seconds whether to read further, so the offer must not hide behind decoration.

Use Canva for layout, text and posters, and use real photos of your own dishes for the menu itself. A generated picture of a perfect butter chicken will disappoint a customer whose real plate looks different, and on delivery apps that disappointment becomes a one-star review with a photo attached. The same rule applies to festival creatives: a clean poster with your real dish photo and a readable offer beats a dramatic generated image every time. Check Canva's pricing page before budgeting, because premium photos, elements and brand features are paid, and what is free today may move behind a paid plan tomorrow. For wider design help, the [small business tools guide](/blog/ai-tools-for-small-business-india/) covers the same build-once, reuse-often approach.

## WhatsApp Business: repeat orders without paying commission again

WhatsApp Business is the cheapest growth tool a cloud kitchen owns, because it turns a first-time aggregator customer into a direct customer. The free app offers a catalogue to showcase dishes, broadcast messages to customers, quick-reply buttons such as order and call, and support for up to 10 linked devices or agents, according to its official features page. The workflow is simple: put a small card in every delivery box with your WhatsApp number and a first-direct-order offer, save every customer who messages you, and send one useful broadcast a week, such as a new dish, a weekend combo or a price-off window. A direct order through WhatsApp or your own link carries no aggregator commission, so even a small shift of repeat orders to this channel changes monthly profit more than most new dishes would.

The discipline matters more than the tool. Broadcasts must be wanted, short and infrequent, because a customer who blocks your number is lost, not just annoyed. Never buy customer number lists, never message people who did not contact you, and answer direct orders fast, because a customer who waits an hour on WhatsApp will simply reopen Swiggy. Keep a simple rule for the team: every WhatsApp order gets a confirmation with the items, total, preparation time and delivery arrangement within minutes, and every complaint gets a human reply, not a copied template. That reliability is what converts a discount hunter into a weekly regular.

## Google Sheets: costing, stock and the daily numbers, free

Google Sheets will not impress anyone, and that is its virtue. It is free with a Google account, it works on a phone, and it forces the one habit that decides whether a cloud kitchen survives: writing the numbers down every day. Build four sheets before launch. One sheet lists every dish with raw material cost, packaging cost, aggregator price, commission estimate and the margin left after all of them. One sheet tracks daily orders by channel, so you can see whether Swiggy, Zomato or WhatsApp is actually growing. One sheet tracks stock, so the kitchen knows what runs out every Sunday instead of discovering it mid-service. One sheet records every refund, cancellation and complaint with its reason, because that list is the cheapest quality audit a kitchen will ever run.

Sheets does not automate anything, so it needs a daily ten-minute routine, ideally at closing time, by the same person each day. The moment the sheets are a week out of date, they stop being a management tool and become fiction. When the kitchen grows past roughly fifty orders a day across channels, or when stock and billing mistakes start costing more than a POS subscription, that is the honest signal to move costing and billing into Petpooja or a similar paid system, and keep Sheets for experiments and new-brand planning. Until then, free and updated beats paid and ignored.

## Using these tools in India: licences, costs and the launch order

A delivery-only kitchen still needs licences, and starting them late is the most common avoidable delay. Industry guides list the usual set: an FSSAI licence, which is mandatory for all food businesses, with the type depending on turnover and whether you operate in one or several states; GST registration, required once turnover crosses the threshold, with cloud kitchens reported as taxed at 5 percent without input tax credit; Shops and Establishments registration; a trade or health licence from the municipal authority; and in some states a fire NOC and pollution control consent. Those are reported requirements, not legal advice for your city, so confirm the exact list with your municipal authority and a chartered accountant before signing a lease or buying equipment. Home-based starting is possible in some cases, but it still requires complying with FSSAI rules, because selling food commercially is a licensed activity wherever the cooking happens.

Costs vary sharply by city, and every number here should be read as a reported estimate to verify locally, not as a quote. Industry reporting puts a small commercial kitchen's rent roughly at 15,000 to 30,000 rupees a month outside metros, higher in metros; basic kitchen equipment at roughly 2 to 4 lakh rupees and a professional setup at 6 to 9 lakh; licences and registrations at roughly 15,000 to 40,000 rupees; the tech stack of tablets, POS, printers and internet at roughly 20,000 to 50,000 rupees; and a lean team of one cook, one helper and one packer at roughly 50,000 rupees a month. Packaging alone is reported at roughly 5 to 10 rupees per order, which sounds small until it is multiplied by a thousand orders. Shared kitchens are an alternative where the infrastructure already exists, usually at a higher running charge, and they suit a founder testing one brand before committing to a lease.

A sane launch order for a first cloud kitchen looks like this. First, licences and one brand with no more than fifteen dishes, because a short menu is easier to cost, cook consistently and photograph well. Second, listings on Swiggy and Zomato with real photos, honest preparation times and prices built from contribution margin. Third, WhatsApp Business with a catalogue and a card in every box, so repeat customers have a direct route from week one. Fourth, UrbanPiper or a similar aggregator tool when order volume across channels makes separate tablets a source of errors. Fifth, a paid POS when reports and stock control become a weekly need rather than a monthly curiosity. Tools bought in that order solve problems you actually have; tools bought in reverse order create monthly bills for problems you have not met yet.

## What to avoid

Avoid a fifty-dish launch menu. Every extra dish adds stock, prep time, packaging and a photo, and it dilutes the few dishes that could have become famous. Avoid pricing from food cost alone, because commission, packaging and GST on commission are real costs that arrive whether or not the dish is popular. Avoid AI food photos on delivery listings, because the gap between a generated dish and the delivered plate is a one-star review waiting to happen. Avoid paying for aggregator ads before ratings, photos and on-time delivery are stable, because ads amplify whatever customers will find, good or bad. Avoid pasting customer data into AI tools, and avoid building any deadline on a free AI limit, since free tiers change without notice. Avoid running separate tablets per app once orders grow, because missed and late orders cost ratings that take months to rebuild. And avoid treating any price, commission or free limit in this article as permanent: re-check the official page before paying, signing or promising.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which single tool should a new cloud kitchen buy first?**
None, if the kitchen is still under about twenty orders a day. Start with the free Swiggy and Zomato partner apps, ChatGPT free for menu writing, Canva free for menu cards, WhatsApp Business for repeat customers, and Google Sheets for costing. Buy UrbanPiper when juggling channels causes missed orders, and buy a POS like Petpooja when billing, stock and reports need one system. Buying software before the problem exists is the easiest way to burn a thin margin.

**Do Swiggy and Zomato commissions really take 15 to 30 percent?**
That range is what industry reporting describes, plus GST on the commission, with the exact figure varying by city, brand and contract. Your signed partner terms are the only figure that matters for your kitchen, so read them before pricing and re-check them when they change. Price every dish from what remains after raw material, packaging, commission and GST on commission, not from food cost alone.

**Can a cloud kitchen run on free tools alone?**
Yes, at small scale. Free tools cover menu writing, menu design, repeat-customer messaging and costing sheets. What free tools cannot do is pull several delivery channels into one dashboard, run a kitchen display system, or produce reliable stock and sales reports automatically. Those are the jobs that justify paid tools, and only once order volume makes manual work the bottleneck.

**Do I need an FSSAI licence for a delivery-only kitchen?**
Yes, according to industry guides, an FSSAI licence is mandatory for all food businesses, including delivery-only ones, with the type depending on turnover and operating area. Confirm the exact licence, plus GST, Shops and Establishments and municipal requirements, with the official authorities and a qualified professional for your city, because requirements vary by state and local body.

**Should I use AI-generated photos on Swiggy and Zomato?**
No. Use real photos of your own dishes, shot in daylight with a clean plate and honest colours. AI can help with layout and posters, but a generated dish that looks better than the delivered plate produces complaints and bad reviews. Customers forgive a plain photo; they do not forgive a dish that does not match its picture.

**How many dishes should a new cloud kitchen launch with?**
Fifteen or fewer is a sensible ceiling. A short menu is cheaper to stock, faster to cook consistently, easier to photograph well, and simpler to cost honestly. Add dishes only when a dish earns its place with steady orders and a healthy margin after commission, and remove dishes that sell rarely or lose money after packaging and commission.

**Is WhatsApp ordering better than Swiggy and Zomato?**
It is better for repeat customers and for margin, because a direct order has no aggregator commission, but it is worse for finding new customers, because nobody discovers a kitchen by browsing WhatsApp. Use both: aggregators to be discovered, WhatsApp to keep the customers you have already won. A card in every delivery box is the cheapest bridge between the two.

**What is the biggest cost mistake new cloud kitchens make?**
Pricing from food cost and ignoring commission, packaging and GST on commission. A dish that looks profitable at 150 rupees can leave almost nothing after those three deductions, before rent and salaries. Build a costing sheet for every dish, update it when ingredient prices change, and reprice or remove any dish that is negative after all deductions.

## Final verdict

Start with one order system, ChatGPT and Canva free for menus, and WhatsApp for repeat orders. Pay for a POS only when order volume proves it.

That verdict is deliberately boring, because cloud kitchens fail on boring things: a missed order, a wrong price, a dish photographed better than it tastes, and a month of commission nobody budgeted for. Get those four things right with free tools and one good order system, and the paid tools you add later will have a healthy kitchen to help.
