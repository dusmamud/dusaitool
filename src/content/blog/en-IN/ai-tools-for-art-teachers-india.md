An art teacher in India does far more than teach drawing. The same teacher plans lessons for different age groups, judges and displays student work, keeps portfolios for admissions and exhibitions, answers parents, and often runs weekend workshops on a tight budget. AI tools can help with all of that preparation and presentation, but they cannot replace the core work of an art class, which is looking, drawing, making mistakes, and trying again by hand.

The direct answer is this: start with Canva for Education for lesson slides, posters, worksheets, and workshop handouts, because it is listed as 100 percent free for eligible K-12 educators after verification, with most premium features included. Add Adobe Express for Education for classroom-safe image generation and simple video and web-page projects, because it is free for K-12 teachers and students in India, with no paywalls, ads, or popups in the education version. Use Behance for public student and teacher portfolios, and Google Sites for a simple class or workshop site that needs no coding. Use ChatGPT, Ideogram, or Leonardo only as idea and reference tools, never as a substitute for student artwork, and check their free limits before you plan a whole term around them. Midjourney is not a free classroom tool, because its main website and Discord routes currently offer no free trial, and only a limited trial exists in its separate niji journey mobile app. Everything in this article is general information only, not professional advice, and prices and free limits change, so re-check the official pricing and help page of any tool before you pay or promise a deadline to students or parents.

This article is written for India on purpose: school art teachers, private coaching and hobby-class teachers, and workshop instructors working with a normal phone and laptop, mixed Hindi, Hinglish, and English classrooms, and students who may share one device at home. It covers three jobs separately, because a tool that is good for lesson planning is not automatically good for portfolios, and a tool that is good for portfolios is not automatically good for running a workshop. It also carries a warning that matters more in art than in many subjects. AI images look finished, and finished-looking work can hide the learning. If a student submits an AI image as their own drawing, the teacher has learned nothing about that student's observation, control, or imagination. The rule for the whole article is simple: AI may prepare, explain, and present, but the artwork that is assessed must be made by the student.

If you read nothing else, remember three rules. First, verify early, because Canva for Education verification can take days, and a workshop poster cannot wait for verification on the morning of the workshop. ## Canva for Education: the lesson and workshop workhorse

Canva for Education is the most useful starting point for most art teachers in India, not because it is the most artistic tool, but because it covers the largest number of everyday jobs. Lesson slides, colour-theory charts, step-by-step technique sheets, exhibition posters, certificates, fee receipts for a private workshop, and social media posts for a batch can all be made in one place, with drag-and-drop editing and thousands of education templates. Students can be invited to collaborate with a school email, assignments can be shared and tracked from a dashboard, and quick polls and quizzes can check understanding during a class.

The free offer is strong, but it has eligibility rules that private tutors should read carefully. Canva Education is free for eligible K-12 teachers, students, school districts, and institutions, and it is available all over the world and is not restricted to any country. It is not currently available for higher education teachers and institutions, and it is not available for private, volunteer, or online tutors, parents, or organisations providing extracurricular activities outside school. That last point matters for art teachers, because many art classes in India are private hobby classes, not school classes. A school art teacher can usually apply with a verified education email or by uploading proof of employment, with review within about 48 hours for document uploads, while a private tutor who is not employed by a K-12 institution should not promise students a Canva Education account. Private tutors can still use the ordinary free Canva plan, but that plan does not include the same premium features, and the difference should be checked on the official pricing page before a workshop is priced.

For a school art teacher, the practical workflow is straightforward. Build a reusable template set once: one lesson slide template, one worksheet template, one poster template, and one certificate template, all in the same colours and fonts. After that, each new lesson is a copy and a small edit, not a fresh design. For a workshop, make the poster, the registration form link, the materials list, and the certificate before admissions open, and reuse the same set for the next batch with only dates and fees changed. That reuse is where the time saving actually happens. A tool does not save time if every poster is designed from zero.

## Adobe Express for Education: classroom-safe images and simple projects

Adobe Express for Education is the second tool worth setting up, especially for teachers who want AI image features inside a classroom-safe environment. The education version offers the same creative tools as the commercial version, plus education-specific privacy controls, single sign-on through a school ID, premium creative assets and student-friendly stock images, safe search for age-appropriate image results, and no paywalls, ads, or popups. Adobe states that it is free for K-12 teachers and students in India and worldwide, and that its teacher training, certifications, and badges are also free. Schools can deploy it through an admin, including integration with Google Classroom and other learning systems.

For art teaching, Adobe Express is useful in a different way from Canva. Its Generate Image and Generative Fill features let a teacher create reference images, for example a still-life arrangement in a particular light, a colour palette variation, or a poster background, without copying images from the internet. Its text effects, animation of characters, video templates, and simple web pages let older students turn a project into a presentation, a short video, or a small exhibition page. That range makes it a good tool for project-based art classes, where the final work is not only a drawing on paper but also a presentation of how the student thought about it.

The honest limit is access. Adobe Express for Education is a K-12 school offer, deployed through schools and districts, so a private workshop teacher may not get the education version at all. The ordinary Adobe Express free plan is different: it gives basic templates, design tools, and 5 GB of cloud storage, while premium features such as background removal and bulk resize need a premium plan. Do not confuse the two plans when you plan a budget. If you are a school teacher, ask your school admin about deployment early in the term. If you are a private teacher, test the ordinary free plan first and decide whether the paid features are worth it for your batch size.

## ChatGPT, Ideogram, and Leonardo: idea tools, not art teachers

ChatGPT is best used by an art teacher as a writing and planning assistant, not as a drawing teacher. It can draft a six-week course outline for beginners, turn a long artist biography into a short classroom note, suggest critique questions for a portfolio review, write a polite parent message about a workshop, or explain a technique in simple Hindi or English for a handout. Its image creation can produce reference images and poster drafts, and its paid plans offer more image creation, but the free plan limits are not fixed in a way a teacher can safely build a term plan around. Treat free image generation as a bonus for the teacher's own preparation, not as a guaranteed classroom resource, and always re-check the official pricing page before promising image work to a batch.

Ideogram is more specialised, and that specialisation is useful for art teachers. It handles text inside images better than many image tools, which matters for posters, exhibition titles, book covers, and calligraphy-style lettering ideas. Its free plan gives 30 credits each week, but only for accounts that sign in with Google, Apple, or Microsoft. Accounts created with an email and password do not get free credits. Free generations are public, which means a teacher should not generate anything private, personal, or student-related on a free Ideogram account. For a classroom, the safe use is teacher-made reference material: a poster layout idea, a title treatment, or a composition example that the teacher then discusses and the students redraw or reinterpret by hand.

Leonardo is useful for older students and for teacher preparation, with a clear and honest free limit. The free plan gives 150 Fast Tokens a day, basic quality settings, one personal collection, and public creations, with model access limited to Auto only. Paid plans add monthly tokens, private creations, more models, and enhanced quality. For an art class, Leonardo is best used to explore how a composition, a lighting setup, or a colour scheme could look, and then to set a hand-drawn task based on that exploration. It is not a good tool for young children, because free creations are public, and it is not a good tool for assessed work, because the student has not drawn the image. Use it for inspiration and discussion, and keep the assessed drawing on paper or in a drawing app.

Midjourney deserves a separate warning, because it is often described online as free, and that description is now misleading. Midjourney's own free-trial documentation says there is no free trial on the main website or Discord, and a limited trial is available only on the separate niji journey mobile app for eligible new users, with 20 generation attempts. Sources that describe Midjourney as free for everyone are confusing account creation and browsing, which are generally possible, with free image generation, which is not. A teacher who plans a workshop around free Midjourney images will be stuck on the day of the class. If you want Midjourney, budget for a subscription, and check the official pricing page first. For most Indian classrooms, the free tools above cover the same teaching purpose without that cost.

## Krita: the free drawing tool that respects the craft

Krita is the most important tool in this article for the actual making of art on a computer, because it is a real drawing tool, not an image generator. It is free and open source software, licensed under the GNU GPL, with multiple brush types, layers, drawing assistants, stabilisers, and animation tools, plus brushes, patterns, textures, and plugins shared by its community. There is no subscription, no credit system, and no daily token limit. A student who learns Krita learns drawing, layer control, brush control, and patience, which are transferable skills. A student who only learns to write prompts learns to describe art, not to make it.

Krita does have practical limits. It runs on a computer, not as a light phone app, so it suits a school computer lab, a home laptop, or a workshop with desktops better than a phone-only batch. It also needs practice time. The first two classes should be slow and guided: opening a file, choosing a brush, using layers, saving work, and exporting an image for a portfolio. Teachers who rush this stage will get frustrated students and messy files. The payoff is real, though, because a Krita portfolio is genuinely the student's work, and it can be printed, exhibited, and submitted for admissions without any question about who made it. For teachers who want a free digital art route that keeps the craft honest, Krita should come before any image generator in the course order.

## Behance and Google Sites: portfolios that people actually open

A portfolio is not a folder of images. It is a short, organised argument that a student can draw, think, and finish work. For admissions, internships, freelance clients, and exhibition selection in India, the two most practical starting points are Behance and Google Sites, and they serve different purposes. Behance is a free portfolio platform with strong community exposure, and it is widely used in India and commonly accepted on job platforms such as Naukri and LinkedIn. Its limits are limited layout control and the fact that the teacher or student does not fully control hosting. Google Sites and Notion are fully free, need no technical setup, and are popular for a first student portfolio, with limited design customisation but a simple, structured presentation. Adobe Portfolio is a third option, but it is included free with a Creative Cloud subscription, not as a standalone free product, so it only makes sense if the teacher or student already pays for Creative Cloud.

For a school art course, the best portfolio structure is simple and repeatable. Each student keeps six to ten finished pieces, not fifty. Each piece gets a title, the medium, the size, the time taken, and two or three lines about the idea and the problem the student solved. Process photos or scans come before the finished piece: a thumbnail sketch, a colour test, and a mid-stage photo. That process section is what separates a real portfolio from a gallery of finished images, and it is also what protects the student if anyone asks whether the work is really theirs. Teachers should set a rule from the first week: every portfolio piece must include its process, and AI-assisted reference images, if any were used, must be labelled as reference, not as the student's work.

For a workshop batch, the portfolio job is different. The teacher needs a batch showcase that attracts the next batch, without exposing children's names, photos, or personal details. A Behance project for the workshop, with first names only or no names at all, and with parent or guardian permission for any student under 18, is a safer choice than posting student work on a personal social media account, where comments and reshares cannot be controlled. Google Sites works well for a course page that stays up after the workshop ends, with the syllabus, the materials list, the batch photos with permission, and a link to admissions for the next batch. Keep the site updated, because an outdated course page with last year's dates does more damage than no page at all.

## Google Classroom and Padlet: running the class and the exhibition

Google Classroom is the least glamorous tool in this article, and for many teachers it is the most useful. It is a free blended learning platform to manage, distribute, and grade assignments, with announcements, classwork, grading, and a class calendar. Teachers can add students by link or email, organise classwork by topic, set assignments with due dates, add rubrics, give private comments, and return work to students. Class materials are filed into Google Drive folders automatically, which matters for art, because photos and scans of student work otherwise get lost in chat groups. Classroom contains no ads and does not use student content for advertising, and it is already used in Indian teacher training materials, including NCERT-linked workshop guides. For a school art teacher, Classroom is the place where the course lives. Canva and Adobe Express make the materials, Krita makes the art, and Classroom collects, dates, and returns it.

Padlet is the exhibition tool. A Padlet board is a digital wall where students can post images, short videos, and audio notes, and where visitors can look and comment. The free plan allows 3 padlets, 20 MB file uploads, 2 minute video recordings, and 5 minute audio recordings, for one user account. That limit shapes how it should be used. Do not create a new Padlet for every class every week, or the free limit will be gone in a month. Create one exhibition board per term or per batch, archive it as a PDF or screenshots at the end, and then reuse the slot. Padlet is excellent for a critique session, where students post work and classmates write one thing they like and one suggestion, and for a workshop showcase, where parents can see the whole batch in one place without joining a chat group. It is not a replacement for a portfolio, because a Padlet board is a wall, not a curated argument, and its free limit means it should be treated as a temporary exhibition, not a permanent archive.

## India-use section: courses, portfolios, and workshops in real conditions

An art course in India often runs in conditions that tool websites do not imagine. Internet may be slow, students may share a phone, a school computer lab may have old machines, and parents may pay in cash or by UPI rather than by card. Plan for those conditions from the start. Keep lesson files small, so they open on a slow connection. Keep a printable version of every important worksheet, because a class should not stop when the internet stops. If students will use Krita, check the lab computers before the term starts, and keep a paper alternative for students without a computer at home. If students will use a phone tool, test it on an ordinary Android phone, not only on a teacher's newer phone, and check how much mobile data a video or image task will use.

For courses, the strongest structure is a short, honest syllabus that parents can read. A six-week beginner course, for example, could cover line and observation in week one, shading and value in week two, colour mixing in week three, composition in week four, a medium such as watercolour or poster colour in week five, and a finished portfolio piece in week six. AI tools fit into that syllabus in small, labelled places: Canva for the weekly handout, Adobe Express for a reference image in week three, and a portfolio template in week six. They do not fit as a separate week called AI art, unless the course is honestly advertised as a digital art course, because parents who paid for drawing lessons will rightly ask why their child spent a week writing prompts.

For portfolios, the India-specific point is admissions and work. Many entry-level design roles and internships in India expect a PDF portfolio or a Behance link, not a custom website, and a custom website becomes more valuable later, for freelancers and experienced professionals. Students should therefore be taught to export a clean PDF from their portfolio first, then publish the same work on Behance, and only then think about a custom site. The PDF is what gets attached to an application, the Behance link is what gets opened in an interview, and the website is what builds a long-term brand. Teaching that order saves students from spending a month on a website and having nothing to send for an internship deadline.

For workshops, the practical checklist is longer than the tool list. Decide the age group, the maximum batch size, the materials students must bring, and the materials included in the fee, before you design a poster. Make the poster in Canva, collect registrations through a form, confirm seats by message, and send a materials list and a venue note at least two days before the workshop. During the workshop, take process photos with permission, and use Padlet or a simple Google Sites page for the showcase afterwards. After the workshop, send certificates, ask for feedback, and save the poster, the form, the attendance list, and the showcase link in one folder, so the next workshop is a copy, not a fresh start. AI tools help with the poster and the certificate, but they do not run the workshop. The teacher's planning, the materials, and the time given to each student do that.

Language matters too. Many art students in India learn best in a mix of Hindi and English, with technical terms in English and explanations in Hindi. AI tools can draft a handout in Hindi, but the teacher must check it, because AI Hindi can mix formal and informal terms, mistranslate a technique name, or use a word that sounds right but means something else in an art context. A short glossary on the first handout, with terms such as value, composition, perspective, and wash written in both Hindi and English, prevents confusion for the whole term. ## What to avoid

Avoid letting AI images enter assessed work without a label. If a portfolio piece, an exhibition entry, or an admissions submission contains AI-generated or AI-heavily-edited work presented as the student's own drawing, the student risks rejection, embarrassment, and a loss of trust that is hard to rebuild. Teach students to label honestly: drawn by me, drawn by me from an AI reference, or generated by AI for discussion only. That honesty is a professional skill, not a punishment.

Avoid pasting student names, photos, school names, or personal details into free AI tools. Free plans often make generations public, and even paid plans should not receive children's personal data without a school agreement and parent or guardian permission. Keep student work inside Classroom, Drive, or a school system for assessment, and publish only work that has permission, with names limited to what parents agreed to.

Avoid building a whole course around a single free limit. A free plan that gives 30 credits a week, 150 tokens a day, or 3 exhibition boards will run out at the worst moment, usually just before an exhibition or a deadline. Plan the core course so it works with paper, pencils, and Krita alone, and treat AI tools as an extra layer that can disappear without breaking the course. If a tool becomes paid or changes its limit mid-term, the course should still finish on time.

Avoid copying another artist's style or a famous character and presenting it as original work, and avoid using AI to recreate a living artist's work for sale or exhibition. Style imitation for classroom study, with credit and discussion, is a normal part of learning. Selling or exhibiting that imitation as original work is not learning, and it can create legal and professional problems. When in doubt, teach from observation, from still life, from nature, and from the student's own imagination, and credit every reference clearly.

Avoid the trap of the finished-looking image. An AI image can look complete in ten seconds, while a student's drawing takes an hour and still looks unfinished. That speed gap can discourage beginners, who compare their slow, honest work with a fast, polished image and conclude that they are bad at art. The teacher's job is to name that gap in the first class: speed is not skill, polish is not understanding, and the slow drawing is the learning. Keep AI images in the reference and discussion part of the lesson, and keep the student's own drawing at the centre of assessment.

Avoid paying for a tool before testing it with one real batch. Free trials and free plans exist so a teacher can test with real students, real internet, and real time pressure. Test Canva templates with one class, test a Padlet board with one critique session, and test a Krita lesson in the actual lab before buying a plan for a whole school or workshop series. Prices and limits change, so re-check the official pricing and help page before paying, and keep a record of what was promised, so a changed limit does not become a surprise in the middle of a term.

## FAQ

**Which AI tool is best for an art teacher in India?**
Canva for Education is the best starting point for most school art teachers, because it covers lesson slides, posters, worksheets, and certificates in one free education offer after verification. Adobe Express for Education is the best second tool for classroom-safe images and simple video and web projects. For actual drawing, Krita is better than any image generator, because it teaches drawing rather than describing it.

**Can a private art tutor use Canva for Education for free?**
Not under the current eligibility rules, because Canva Education is for eligible K-12 teachers, students, schools, and districts, and it excludes private, volunteer, or online tutors, parents, and extracurricular organisations outside school. A private tutor can use the ordinary free Canva plan, but should check its limits on the official pricing page, and should not promise students an education account that the tutor cannot provide.

**Is Midjourney free for classroom use?**
No, not on its main website or Discord, where there is currently no free trial. The only limited free trial documented by Midjourney is in its separate niji journey mobile app for eligible new users, with 20 generation attempts. For most classrooms, Ideogram, Leonardo, and Adobe Express cover the same teaching purpose without a subscription, and their free limits should still be re-checked before a lesson is planned.

**How should AI be used in a student portfolio?**
AI should appear only as labelled reference or discussion material, never as the student's own assessed drawing. A strong portfolio shows six to ten finished pieces, each with its process: a thumbnail sketch, a colour test, and a mid-stage photo, plus a short note about the idea and the problem solved. That process section proves the work is the student's, and it is what admissions tutors and clients actually want to see.

**What is the cheapest way to teach digital art in India?**
Start with Krita on a computer, which is free and open source, plus Canva or Adobe Express for handouts and presentations, plus Behance or Google Sites for portfolios. That combination has no subscription cost for the core drawing and portfolio work. The real cost is time: teacher practice time, lab setup time, and the time to build reusable templates, and that time should be planned into the term, not squeezed into the first week.

**How can a teacher run an online art exhibition for free?**
Use one Padlet board per term or batch, within the free plan limit of 3 padlets, and archive it as screenshots or a PDF at the end. Publish a longer-lasting showcase on Behance or Google Sites, with parent or guardian permission for students under 18, and with names limited to what was agreed. Do not post children's full names, school names, or photos on a public account without that permission.

**Should young children use AI image generators in art class?**
No, not as a drawing substitute, and not on free plans where generations are public. Young children learn art through observation, touch, colour mixing, and making mistakes on paper. AI image tools are better kept for teacher preparation and, for older students, for supervised discussion about composition, lighting, and reference. The assessed work for young children should always be their own drawing, painting, or craft.

**Do AI tools work without a VPN in India?**
The tools in this article, including Canva, Adobe Express, ChatGPT, Ideogram, Leonardo, Krita, Behance, Google Sites, Google Classroom, and Padlet, are generally available in India through their normal websites and apps without a VPN. Availability can still vary by school network, device, or account type, so test each tool on the actual school or workshop network before a lesson depends on it, and keep a paper backup for the day the network fails.

**What should a teacher check before paying for an AI art tool?**
Check the official pricing page, the free limit, whether generations are public or private, whether the plan covers one teacher or a whole batch, and whether student data is covered by a school agreement. Test the tool with one real class first, keep a record of the price and limit on the day you paid, and plan the course so it still works if the limit changes. Never promise parents a tool, a deadline, or a certificate that depends on a free limit you have not tested.

**Can AI replace an art teacher?**
No. AI can prepare handouts, suggest ideas, and make reference images, but it cannot watch a student's hand, correct a grip, notice that a student is frustrated, or judge when to praise effort and when to push for more care. Art teaching is observation, feedback, and encouragement in person, and a tool that generates a finished image in seconds teaches none of those skills. The best use of AI in art class is to give the teacher more time for the human work, not less.

## Final verdict

Start with Canva for Education for lessons and Behance for portfolios; keep AI as a sketch aid, and never let it replace student drawing.

That verdict is deliberately narrow. A school art teacher who sets up Canva for Education, Adobe Express for Education, Google Classroom, and Krita, and who publishes student work on Behance or Google Sites with permission, has a complete, low-cost system for courses, portfolios, and workshops. A private tutor who cannot access the education offers can still run the same system with ordinary free Canva, Krita, Google Classroom, and Behance, and should budget honestly for any paid feature rather than promising it for free. In both cases, the art itself stays human: drawn, painted, or made by the student, with process shown, references credited, and AI kept in its proper place as a preparation and discussion aid. Prices and free limits will change, so re-check the official pricing and help pages before paying or promising a deadline, and keep the course working even if a free tool disappears. That is the honest, India-ready way to use AI in art teaching, and it protects both the teacher's time and the student's learning.
