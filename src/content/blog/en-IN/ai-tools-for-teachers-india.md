A teacher's scarcest resource is not ideas. It is **prep time**. A lesson plan, a rubric, a quiz, a leveled reading passage, and a parent email, for five classes, every week, take hours that could go to teaching, to feedback, or to rest. AI tools help with that prep, but they help in different ways, and the tool that plans a lesson is not the tool that makes it visual, and neither tool should see student names, work, or scores without a school agreement.

The short answer for India is this: use [MagicSchool](https://www.magicschool.ai/) free for lesson plans, rubrics, quizzes, and classroom writing, knowing its free **history keeps only your last five generations**. Use [Canva for Education](https://www.canva.com/education/) for slides, posters, and handouts, because it is listed as 100 percent free for K-12 educators after verification, with premium Pro features included. Use [Gemini](https://edu.google.com/ai/gemini-for-education/) and [Gemini Notebook](https://workspace.google.com/products/gemini-notebook/) free if your school already uses Google Workspace for Education, with your administrator managing access. Use [Diffit](https://web.diffit.me/) Basic free for leveled reading passages, knowing it keeps only the last 90 days of content. Everything else is a sampler, not a classroom tool, until your school has a data agreement for it.

This article is written for India on purpose: CBSE, NCERT, state board, and private school teachers, Hindi, Hinglish, and English classrooms, a normal phone and laptop, and no assumption that you have a US teacher account, a paid plan, or a district agreement. It also carries a warning that matters for teachers more than for any other group in this series: a free teacher account does not settle student data. Only a tool your school or district has signed an agreement for should ever see **student names, work, or scores**. Never paste student work into a free chatbot to grade it, even if the tool is free and convenient, because that work becomes data you cannot control.

## MagicSchool: the widest free planning tool, with a five-item memory

MagicSchool gives free users access to its 80 plus teacher tools, and for a teacher generating a few resources a week the free plan is genuinely workable. Lesson plans, rubrics, IEP-adjacent supports, behaviour intervention ideas, and text leveling: the breadth is the pitch. Its free plan is described as Forever Free, with no numeric cap published, while Plus is 8.33 dollars per user a month billed annually or 12.99 dollars billed monthly, and adds unlimited generations, unlimited output history, unlimited output editing, and 50 plus student tools. The comparison table marks unlimited generations as unavailable on free and gives no threshold, so a planning-heavy week may run into an invisible cap.

What breaks first on MagicSchool free is your output history. The free tier keeps only your last five generations. Generate a lesson plan Monday, three exit tickets Tuesday, and a parent email Wednesday, and Monday's plan is gone. In practice, teachers learn this rule exactly once, the hard way, before they learn to copy everything into a doc the moment it generates. *Raina*, the chatbot, is also rationed on free. MagicSchool's privacy policy lists, among the things it does not do, using student data to train, fine-tune, or improve AI models, and says student data is processed under a student data privacy agreement with the school. Whether your school has that agreement is a question for whoever signs your school's contracts, not something a teacher account settles.

Use MagicSchool in a specific way:

1. Generate one resource at a time.
2. Copy it into a Google Doc or a Word file immediately.
3. Edit it against your syllabus before you use it.

Outputs are solid first drafts, not finished products, and they must be read against the standard before they reach a student. A lesson plan that covers a topic in general may miss your board's specific wording, a rubric that looks fair may not match your school's marking scheme, and a quiz that looks good may test a topic your class has not covered yet. MagicSchool saves prep time, but it does not replace your knowledge of your class, your syllabus, and your students.

## Canva for Education: visuals free, verification is the delay

Canva for Education is the quiet outlier: verified K-12 teachers get the premium product free, including AI features like *Magic Write*, *Magic Design*, and image tools, with no generation meter found in normal classroom use in one test. A week of warm-up slides, two posters, and a set of vocabulary cards were built without touching a limit. The AI is a design assistant more than a lesson brain, but making a lesson readable and printable is half the job of teaching materials. Canva says it is 100 percent free, with no hidden fees, paywalls, or trial periods, for K-12 educators, and its FAQ says all premium Canva Pro features are included in Canva Education, with its feature list naming Magic Write 2.0.

What breaks first is nothing significant once you are verified. The friction is the verification itself, which can take a few days if your school domain is not recognised automatically, and up to seven days in busy periods if instant verification fails and you upload a document. Start verification before the lesson you need it for, not the night before. Canva says it never uses student data to train AI models, but student work uploaded to a design tool is still student data, so do not upload student names, photos, or work into Canva unless your school's policy allows it.

Canva for Education will not plan instruction. It makes what you already planned look like you had a prep period. Use it after MagicSchool, not instead of it: plan the lesson in MagicSchool, copy it into your own doc, edit it, and then turn it into [slides, posters, or handouts](/blog/free-ai-presentation-makers-india/) in Canva. For Indian classrooms, Canva is especially useful for visual aids in Hindi and English, for vocabulary cards with images, and for posters that students can read from the back of a classroom. Keep text large, keep one idea per slide, and check Hindi spelling at full size, because a small spelling error on a poster is visible to every student.

## Gemini, Diffit, and Brisk: Google schools, leveled reading, and feedback

Gemini and Gemini Notebook, renamed from *NotebookLM* in July 2026, are included free in all Google Workspace for Education editions, with your administrator managing access. Google says data is not human reviewed or used to train AI models for these editions. Gemini is for drafting, summarizing, and lesson ideas, while Gemini Notebook is for study guides, quizzes, and summaries built from sources you upload, like your textbook chapter, your class slides, or your teacher notes. For schools already on Google, Gemini is the simplest general assistant, because it lives inside the Workspace your school already uses, and Notebook is the best tool for [source-based guides](/blog/ai-tools-for-study-notes-india/), because it cites the passage behind every claim.

Diffit turns one text into leveled passages, vocabulary, and questions, which is invaluable for a mixed-ability classroom where students read at different levels. Diffit Basic is free, but it keeps content from the last 90 days only, with PDF, print, and share, and no Google, Canva, or Microsoft export. That means you must download or print leveled passages within 90 days, or they will disappear from your Diffit account. Diffit's directory lists its FERPA, COPPA, and GDPR fields as unknown, which is not an audit, and it does not settle student data for your school. Use Diffit for passages and questions you create yourself, not for student work, and download everything you want to keep.

[Brisk](https://www.briskteaching.com/) works inside Google Docs, Slides, and Chrome, giving feedback on student writing and helping teachers create materials without leaving Google tools. Its free For Teachers plan gives no numeric cap on its page, and its privacy notice does not say whether student data trains models, which is unverified. That unverified status matters: Brisk is convenient because it lives inside Docs, but convenience does not settle data. Do not use Brisk on student work unless your school has checked its data terms and signed an agreement. For teacher-created materials, like turning a Doc into a quiz or a handout, Brisk free is worth testing, because it saves the step of copying between tools.

## Using teacher AI in India: syllabus, language, and student data

Indian classrooms follow a syllabus, and AI tools do not automatically know yours. NCERT, CBSE, state board, and private school syllabi differ in wording, depth, and question pattern. An AI lesson plan that is correct in general can still be wrong for your class, because it uses a different definition, a different example, or a different question type. Always read every output against the standard before it reaches a student, and edit it to your board's exact wording. For [Hindi classrooms](/blog/ai-translation-tools-hindi-english-india/), check spelling and technical terms carefully, because AI Hindi can mix formal and informal terms in the same lesson plan. For [English classrooms](/blog/ai-tools-for-learning-english-india/), learn the exact technical terms your board uses, because an examiner marks the term, not just the idea.

Language for teachers is also about reading levels. A class has students who read at different levels, and Diffit helps by leveling the same text for different readers, but leveling is not dumbing down. A leveled passage should keep the same ideas and the same key terms, with simpler sentences and clearer vocabulary, not with the ideas removed. Check leveled passages yourself, because an AI leveler can remove a key concept while simplifying a sentence, and a student who reads the simplified passage will miss that concept in the exam. For Hinglish classrooms, where students speak Hinglish but write in English or Hindi, use AI to draft explanations in Hinglish first, then write the formal answer in the exam language, so students understand first and write correctly second.

[Student data](/blog/ai-privacy-safety-free-tools-india/) is the most important rule in this article, and it is simple: only a tool your school has signed an agreement for should ever see student names, work, or scores. A free teacher account, a privacy page, and a vendor claim do not replace that agreement. Never paste student essays into a free chatbot to grade them, never upload a class photo with student names into a design tool without school permission, and never use a student-facing AI tool without checking your school's policy first. For teacher-created materials, like lesson plans, rubrics, and handouts, free tools are safe and useful. For student work, the tool must be school-approved, or the work must stay offline.

## What to avoid

- Do not trust an AI lesson plan, rubric, or quiz without reading it against your syllabus, because outputs are first drafts, not finished products.
- Do not rely on MagicSchool free history, because it keeps only five generations, and copy every output into your own doc immediately.
- Do not start Canva for Education verification the night before you need it, because it can take up to seven days.
- Do not paste student names, work, or scores into any free tool without a school agreement, and do not use a tool whose data training status is unverified on student work.
- Do not plan around US-only teacher offers in India, and do not treat SchoolAI's five-launch trial as a classroom tool.
- Do not pay for five teacher tools at once, and do not use AI to grade student work without checking your school's policy and the AI's reasoning, because AI grading can be inconsistent and unfair, especially for creative or Hindi answers.

A final trap is using AI to avoid teaching. Beginners often generate a lesson plan, a quiz, and a handout, and then read them to the class without understanding them deeply themselves. Students notice when a teacher does not understand the material, and they learn less, even if the handout looks professional. Use AI to save prep time, then use that saved time to understand the lesson better, to prepare examples from your students' lives, and to give feedback that only you can give. AI saves time. Teaching still needs you.
