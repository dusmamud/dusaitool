If you need a voice for a YouTube video, a podcast, a presentation, or simply to listen to a long article while you travel, you do not need to pay on day one. You do need to know the difference between three very different jobs: **listening to text yourself**, **downloading audio to use in a video**, and **selling that audio as part of paid work**. Free voice tools treat those three jobs very differently, and confusing them is the most common mistake in this category.

The short answer for India is this: use ElevenLabs free for the most natural-sounding voice, and use TTSMaker, an India-made tool, for long Hindi or English text that you want to download as an MP3 without paying. Everything else is useful for a specific job, not for everyday voice work. If you need a voice for a monetised video or a client project, free tools will probably not give you the licence you need, and that is the point at which paying becomes justified.

This article is written for India on purpose: normal broadband and mobile data, Hindi and English scripts, Indian accents and code-mixed speech, and no assumption that you have a paid plan, a studio microphone, or a developer account. It also carries a warning that matters more for voice than for images or video: do not clone a real person's voice without permission, and do not use an AI voice to pretend that a real person said something they did not say. That is not a free-tool trick. It is a trust and safety problem, and it can cause real harm.

If you read nothing else, remember three rules:

1. **Minutes and characters are not the same.** Ten minutes of audio is roughly 1,500 words at normal speaking speed, while 20,000 characters is roughly 3,000 words. Compare tools in the same unit before you decide which is more generous.
2. **Listening is not publishing.** A tool that lets you listen for free does not necessarily let you download an MP3 or use that audio in a video.
3. **Commercial use is a separate licence.** ElevenLabs free, Murf free, and Fish Audio free all restrict commercial use, even when the voice sounds excellent.

## ElevenLabs: the best voice, with a clear commercial boundary

[*ElevenLabs*](https://elevenlabs.io/) is the tool to try first when voice quality matters most. Its voices pause, stress words, and change tone in a way that sounds close to a real person, and its most expressive model can read audio tags like whispers, laughs, or sarcasm in your script and change delivery to match. It supports audiobooks and podcast narration in more than 70 languages, voiceovers for games and ads, dubbed videos, and conversational voices for apps.

The free plan gives you about 10,000 credits a month, which is about 10 minutes of audio, plus access to text-to-speech, speech-to-text, sound effects, voice design, and music, with up to three projects in Studio in the October 2026 checks. That is enough to test voices, to narrate a short video, or to make a demo for a client. It is not enough for a full audiobook, a daily podcast, or a long course.

The free restrictions are the important part. Free audio comes with **no commercial licence**, so you cannot use it in anything that makes money, including a monetised YouTube video, a paid course, a client voiceover, or an ad. You can share it non-commercially, but you must credit ElevenLabs when you do. Voice cloning is not included on the free plan. Commercial rights and instant voice cloning start at the Starter plan, listed at 6 dollars a month for 30,000 credits in the October 2026 checks. If your work is non-commercial practice, ElevenLabs free is excellent. If your work is paid, budget for Starter or use a tool whose free licence actually covers your use.

For Hindi content, ElevenLabs is worth testing with a short Hindi paragraph before you commit to a long script. Hindi voices have improved greatly, but pronunciation of names, English technical terms mixed into Hindi, and numbers can still sound unnatural. Listen at full speed, not just at the start, and fix pronunciation by spelling a difficult word phonetically or by splitting a long sentence into two shorter ones. A voice that sounds perfect for ten seconds can still stumble on a name in minute three.

## TTSMaker: the India-made workhorse for long text and MP3 download

[*TTSMaker*](https://ttsmaker.in/), at ttsmaker.in, is a free text-to-speech tool made in India, and it deserves attention for a simple reason: it lets you download MP3 audio from free voices without a complicated signup. You paste text, pick one of 318 free voices in 74 languages, listen, and download the MP3. Without an account, it converts up to 500 characters at a time and 2,000 characters a day. With a free account, opened with one tap in Telegram, it raises that to 20,000 characters at a time and 200,000 characters a day, according to its own page checked in October 2026. That free-account allowance is far larger than ElevenLabs free for long text, because it uses Microsoft neural voices rather than premium ElevenLabs voices.

There are three practical details to know:

- **Telegram-only login.** Telegram is the only way to log in for the free account. There is no email or password login. If you do not use Telegram, you are limited to the no-account allowance.
- **Use the exact domain.** ttsmaker.in is an independent service and is not connected to ttsmaker.com or to any other site with a similar name. Use the exact domain, and do not enter payment details on a lookalike site.
- **Premium voices use paid credits.** Premium voices on the same site use paid credits, with packs listed in rupees, for example 299 rupees for 1,000,000 credits in the Mini Credit check, valid for 30 days. New accounts also get welcome credits to try premium voices. Do not confuse a welcome credit with a permanent free premium voice.

For Indian users, TTSMaker is especially useful for three jobs: converting a long Hindi article into audio for listening on a commute, making a simple voiceover for a presentation when you do not want to record your own voice, and testing how a script sounds before you record it yourself. The Microsoft voices are clear and reliable, though they sound less expressive than ElevenLabs for drama or storytelling. For a factual explainer, a news-style narration, or a study note, clarity matters more than drama, and TTSMaker free is often the right choice.

## Murf: a professional editor you can test, but not publish from for free

[*Murf*](https://murf.ai/) is built around a full voiceover editor, not just a voice. You write or paste a script, select a voice from a library of more than 200 voices, and adjust pitch, speed, and emphasis before exporting. It has a pronunciation library to fix how the AI says specific names, brands, or technical terms, and it integrates with Canva, PowerPoint, and Google Slides. For training videos, product explainers, and e-learning courses, that editor is genuinely useful.

The free plan, however, is the most limited on this list for publishing. You get **10 minutes of voice generation in total, not per month**. You **cannot download** anything you create on free, and there is no commercial licence. In practice, Murf free lets you hear how your script sounds and test the editor, but you cannot use the audio anywhere else. To download audio with commercial rights, you need the Creator plan, listed at 29 dollars a month, or 19 dollars a month billed annually, in the October 2026 checks. Voice cloning is only on the Enterprise plan.

Use Murf free the way it is designed: as a test drive. Write a real script, test three voices, fix two pronunciations, and decide whether Murf's editor saves you enough time to justify paying later. Do not write a full course on Murf free and assume you will download it later. You will not be able to, and you will have to rewrite or regenerate the whole project in another tool.

## Speechify and NaturalReader: for listening, not for publishing

[*Speechify*](https://speechify.com/) and [*NaturalReader*](https://www.naturalreaders.com/) solve a different problem: listening to text yourself, rather than publishing audio for others. Speechify offers unlimited listening with basic voices, but no download for publishing. NaturalReader offers unlimited use of free system voices, 20,000 characters a day for listening with Lite voices, and 4,000 shared characters a day for listening with Plus and cloned voices, but no MP3 conversion on free and no OCR on free, according to its help page.

For students, these tools are excellent. You can listen to a PDF, a web article, or your own draft while you walk, cook, or travel, and you can catch errors in your own writing by hearing them read aloud. For accessibility, listening tools are also essential: they let readers with low vision or reading difficulties access the same material as everyone else. For creators, however, listening tools are not a substitute for a voiceover tool. If you need an MP3 to put in a video, use ElevenLabs or TTSMaker, not Speechify or NaturalReader free.

A practical study method is to listen to your own notes at slightly faster speed, then write a one-paragraph summary from memory without looking at the notes. That method tests understanding far better than re-reading the same page five times. Listening tools make that method easy, and they do not require a paid plan for personal study.

## Google Cloud TTS and developer tools: huge free volume, but not a studio

[*Google Cloud Text-to-Speech*](https://cloud.google.com/text-to-speech) gives developers up to 4 million free Standard characters a month, then charges 4 dollars per million Standard characters, with Neural2 at 16 dollars and Chirp 3 HD at 30 dollars per million in the October 2026 checks. [*Amazon Polly*](https://aws.amazon.com/polly/) charges 4 dollars per million Standard characters, 16 dollars for Neural, and 30 dollars for Generative, with free tier allowances for new accounts. Those numbers are enormous compared to creator tools, because they are priced for apps, call centres, and automated pipelines, not for a single creator making one video.

The catch is setup. Google Cloud TTS and Polly are APIs, not polished studios. You need a developer account, API keys, and code or a third-party app to turn text into audio and download it. If you are building an app, an accessibility product, or an automated narration pipeline, the free volume is genuinely useful. If you are a student who just wants one MP3 for a presentation, the setup time is not worth it. Use TTSMaker or ElevenLabs instead, and leave cloud TTS for developers.

[*Fish Audio*](https://fish.audio/) and [*Descript*](https://www.descript.com/) sit between creator tools and developer tools. Fish Audio offers a large voice library at about 7 minutes a month on free, for personal use only. Descript offers 60 media minutes a month and 100 one-time AI credits for podcasts and video editing, with terms to check for publishing. [*Fliki*](https://fliki.ai/) offers 3 credits a month on free for script-to-video, with 720p exports, a watermark, and no commercial rights. All three are worth a test for a specific workflow, but none replaces ElevenLabs for quality or TTSMaker for long free downloads.

## Hindi voices, accents, and code-mixed speech

Hindi voice quality has improved, but it still needs a human check, especially for three cases: names, numbers, and English words mixed into Hindi. A voice may pronounce a Hindi sentence perfectly and then mispronounce an English brand name, a technical term, or a date. A voice may also read a number as a year when you meant a quantity, or read an abbreviation letter by letter when you meant a word.

Three fixes help in almost every tool:

1. **Write numbers in words.** Write numbers in words for important lines, or split a long number into smaller groups.
2. **Spell difficult names phonetically.** Spell a difficult name phonetically in Hindi, or add a pronunciation note if the tool supports it, as Murf does.
3. **Listen to the full audio.** Listen to the full audio, not just the first ten seconds, because errors often appear in the middle of a long script. For YouTube, also check the audio on a phone speaker, not just on headphones, because many viewers will hear it on a phone in a noisy place.

Code-mixed Hindi-English, or Hinglish, is common in India, and most tools handle it better than they did two years ago, but not perfectly. A sentence that mixes Hindi and English in the same clause can change accent mid-sentence in some voices. If that happens, try splitting the sentence into a Hindi part and an English part, or choose a voice that is explicitly listed for Hindi rather than a generic multilingual voice. For a professional video, record the Hinglish lines yourself if the AI voice keeps switching accent, because a human voice handles code-mixing naturally in a way AI voices still struggle with.

## Three workflows that actually work

A [YouTube voiceover workflow](/blog/ai-tools-for-youtube-creators-india/) starts with a script, not with a voice. Write the script, read it aloud yourself once, and fix any sentence that is hard to say. Then generate the voice in ElevenLabs for quality or TTSMaker for length, download the MP3, and edit it in CapCut, Canva, or Descript with visuals and [captions](/blog/free-ai-video-editing-captions-india/). Do not generate a voiceover for a script you have not read aloud yourself, because a script that is hard for you to read will also be hard for an AI voice to read naturally.

A [podcast workflow](/blog/ai-tools-for-podcast-audio-india/) is similar, but with one extra rule: consistency. Use the same voice for every episode of a series, and save the voice settings, speed, and pitch you used. Listeners notice when a voice changes every episode, and they trust a consistent voice more. If you use ElevenLabs free, remember that 10 minutes a month is not a podcast season. Plan one short episode a month on free, or budget for Starter when the podcast becomes regular.

A [study and accessibility workflow](/blog/ai-tools-for-study-notes-india/) is the simplest. Use Speechify or NaturalReader to listen to your notes, use TTSMaker to download a long article as MP3 for a commute, and use ElevenLabs for a short, high-quality narration of a presentation. Do not pay for a voice tool for study alone, because listening tools are free for personal use and TTSMaker free covers long downloads. Pay only when you need downloads with commercial rights or a specific premium voice for paid work.

In all three workflows, the rule for paying is the same. Do not pay in month one. Use the free tier for two weeks, write down where it actually stops you, and pay only for the one limit that stops you repeatedly. If ElevenLabs free runs out at 10 minutes every month, that is a reason to consider Starter. If Murf's no-download rule is the problem, that is a reason to pay for Creator or to switch to TTSMaker. If you never hit a limit, you do not need to pay yet.

## What to avoid

Do not do the following:

- **Do not clone a real person's voice** without their clear permission, and do not use an AI voice to make it sound like a real person, a politician, a celebrity, or a teacher said something they did not say. That is misleading, and it can harm the person and your audience.
- **Do not use a free voice for a monetised video or a client project** when the free licence forbids commercial use. ElevenLabs free, Murf free, and Fish Audio free all forbid or restrict commercial use, and a client who discovers that later will not trust you again.
- **Do not trust a tool that calls itself free but asks for a card on signup.** That is a trial, not a free plan.
- **Do not enter payment details on a lookalike site**, especially for TTSMaker, where the .in site and the .com site are different services.
- **Do not generate a full audiobook on a free plan and assume you will download it later**, especially on Murf, where free has no download at all.
- **Do not pay for five voice tools at once.** Pay for one tool, for one reason, after free use has proven that reason.

A final trap is overusing expressive tags. ElevenLabs can whisper, laugh, or speak sarcastically when you add tags, but overusing those tags makes narration sound like a performance, not like information. Use expressive tags sparingly, for one or two lines that genuinely need them, and keep the rest of the narration natural and steady.

## Frequently asked questions

Can I use these tools in India without a VPN? Yes, for the major tools in this article. ElevenLabs, TTSMaker, Murf, Speechify, NaturalReader, and Google Cloud TTS all work in India through a normal browser with a standard account. If a smaller tool does not load, check whether it is down or region-limited before assuming you need a VPN, and do not enter payment details on a site you cannot verify.

Which free tool is best for YouTube voiceover? ElevenLabs free for quality, if your video is short and non-commercial, and TTSMaker free for longer voiceovers that you need to download as MP3. For monetised YouTube, neither free licence is sufficient on its own. Budget for ElevenLabs Starter or another commercial licence before you monetise, and do not monetise a video that uses free non-commercial audio.

Can I download MP3 audio for free? Yes, from ElevenLabs free and from TTSMaker free, with different limits. ElevenLabs free gives about 10 minutes a month with download. TTSMaker free gives long MP3 downloads from Microsoft voices, with a much larger character allowance on a free account. Murf free does not allow downloads, and Speechify and NaturalReader free are for listening, not for MP3 publishing.

Are Hindi voices good enough for professional work? For factual narration, yes, with a human check. For drama, storytelling, or code-mixed Hinglish, test carefully and listen to the full audio. Fix names, numbers, and English terms by spelling them phonetically or splitting sentences, and record yourself if the AI voice keeps switching accent mid-sentence.

What should I do when ElevenLabs free runs out mid-project? Stop generating variations, pick the best audio you already have, and finish it in an editor. If the project must continue today, switch to TTSMaker for the remaining text, or split the project across two months. Do not pay in a panic for the first upgrade button you see. Compare the paid price, the new allowance, and whether it fixes minutes, downloads, or commercial use, because those are three different problems.

Should a student pay for a voice tool? Not to start, and not for study alone. Listening tools are free for personal use, and TTSMaker free covers long downloads. Pay only when you need a premium voice or a commercial licence for paid work, and then pay for one tool, not five.

## The final verdict, and a checklist before you pay

Start free, and start with the job, not the tool. If the job is listening, use Speechify or NaturalReader. If the job is a short, high-quality voiceover, use ElevenLabs free. If the job is a long MP3 download, use TTSMaker free. If the job is testing a professional editor, use Murf free, knowing you cannot download from it. If the job is building an app, use Google Cloud TTS free volume, knowing you need developer setup.

Before you pay for any voice tool, answer these questions in writing:

- Which exact limit stopped you, and on which date?
- Did it stop minutes, characters, downloads, or commercial use, because each has a different fix?
- Did you check the official pricing page today?
- Does the paid plan fix that exact limit, or does it only give you more of something you did not need?

If you cannot answer those questions, you are not ready to pay yet, and another two weeks on free allowances will give you a clearer answer than any review article can.

Free AI voice tools in India in 2026 are genuinely useful, but only when you treat minutes, characters, downloads, and licences as four separate limits. Use free tools to learn, to test voices, and to prove what you actually need. Pay later, for one tool, for one proven reason, and never use a voice to impersonate a real person. That is how a student saves money, how a creator stays professional, and how a listener gets clear audio without paying for five voice subscriptions at once.
