The short answer for an NGO in India is this: you do not need an expensive AI platform to start. You need a small, honest stack. Use a free AI chatbot such as ChatGPT or Gemini to draft fundraising appeals, donor thank-you notes and volunteer messages, and then edit every word yourself before it goes out. Use Canva Nonprofits, if your organisation is eligible, to design campaign posters, social posts and impact-report layouts without hiring a designer for every single post. Use an Indian fundraising platform such as Give.do or Ketto to collect donations online, because donors in India already trust those pages, and because building your own payment page from scratch creates compliance and trust problems you do not need. Add a proper volunteer or donor system, such as Sevastack or Zoho for Nonprofits, only when spreadsheets and WhatsApp groups start losing track of people, money or hours. That is the whole stack. Everything else in this article is detail, limits and warnings around those four choices.

This article is written for small and medium NGOs in India: a team of five to fifty people, a mix of staff and volunteers, donors who give through UPI, bank transfer or a fundraising page, and funders who ask for a proper impact report at the end of a project. It assumes a normal phone and laptop, patchy internet in the field, and no budget for a large software purchase. It also assumes something important: AI can draft, summarise and organise, but it cannot verify a beneficiary story, audit your accounts or replace a human decision about who receives help. If a tool promises to do any of those things automatically, treat that promise with suspicion.

Prices, free limits and eligibility rules change often, and different sources sometimes disagree about the exact current figure. Before you pay for anything, or before you promise a donor or a funder a deadline that depends on a tool, re-check the official pricing or help page of that tool. The figures in this article were verified against official or reputable sources before writing, but they are a starting point for your own check, not a substitute for it. This article is general information only, not professional advice on law, tax, accounting or fundraising compliance. For questions about registration, foreign funding rules, tax exemption or donor-data law, check official sources and speak to a qualified professional.

If you read nothing else, remember three rules. First, the donor-facing words matter more than the tool: a sincere, specific appeal written with AI help and then corrected by someone who actually visited the field will raise more than a polished but vague appeal generated in one click. Second, the free limit that bites first is rarely the one you expect: for email it is the contact count, for transcription it is the monthly minutes, and for volunteer tools it is the volunteer count. Third, never put a beneficiary's full name, photograph, medical detail or address into a public AI chatbot without consent and without checking your organisation's own privacy policy, because what you paste into a chatbot may be stored or used in ways you cannot fully control.

## ChatGPT: the drafting desk for appeals and donor letters

ChatGPT is the most useful general starting point for a small NGO because its free plan covers everyday text chats in India without a VPN, and because it is good at the exact jobs NGOs repeat every week: turning rough field notes into a readable appeal, shortening a long project update into a donor email, writing five versions of a social-media caption, and drafting a polite follow-up to a donor who has not replied. Give it structure, not just a topic. For example, paste your rough notes and ask for a 150-word appeal with a clear opening, one concrete fact, one human detail with consent, and one call to action. Then rewrite the result in your own voice, remove anything the chatbot invented, and check every number against your own records. Chatbots invent plausible-sounding facts, and a wrong number in a donor appeal is worse than a plain appeal, because it damages trust.

The free plan is genuinely usable for everyday text, but it is not unlimited in every dimension. Separate caps apply to file uploads, image generation, voice and data analysis, and the in-app usage screen shows when each one resets. That matters when a whole team shares one free account during a campaign week: the text chats may keep working while image generation stops. Do not build a campaign timeline that depends on unlimited image generation from a free plan. Also, do not use the free chatbot as your record system. Keep the final, approved version of every appeal, letter and report in your own documents, not only in a chat history that can be lost or renamed.

## Gemini: the summariser inside Google Workspace

Gemini is the natural second chatbot for NGOs that already live in Google Docs, Gmail and Drive, because it sits close to those tools and because its free plan in India costs nothing with a Google account. Its strengths for NGOs are summarising long meeting notes, turning a field-visit report into a short update, drafting replies in Gmail, and helping a small team turn a pile of notes into a structured impact-report outline. The free plan works on a compute budget rather than a simple message count: each prompt uses part of a budget based on the model, the features used and the length of the chat, the budget refreshes every five hours, and there is also a weekly cap. In practice this means short, focused chats last longer than one very long chat, and starting a new chat for a new topic is good practice.

For an NGO, Gemini's most practical use is the boring one: summarising. A two-hour community meeting, a set of volunteer feedback forms, or a long funder guideline document can be turned into a one-page summary that a busy coordinator can actually read. But the same warning applies as with every chatbot: the summary is a draft. Check names, dates, places and numbers against the original notes before the summary goes to a donor or a funder. If the original notes are in Hindi, Hinglish or another Indian language, test the tool on a small sample first, because translation and summarisation quality varies by language and by topic, and a mistranslated beneficiary quote is a serious error, not a small typo.

## Canva Nonprofits: design without a designer for every post

Most small NGOs do not have a design problem; they have a volume problem. Every campaign needs a poster, every festival needs a social post, every project needs a report cover, and hiring a designer for each one is unaffordable. Canva Nonprofits addresses exactly that problem by giving eligible charitable organisations free access to Canva's premium tools. The official eligibility page states the core conditions clearly: the organisation must be officially registered as a charitable nonprofit in its country, must operate independently from government affiliation and direct government appropriations, and must not operate for commercial profit. Meeting those criteria does not guarantee eligibility, because participation is subject to review, business policies and Canva's discretion, and applicants need proof of registration plus governing documents showing their structure and charitable purpose.

For an NGO that qualifies, the practical value is large. Campaign posters, donor thank-you cards, volunteer certificates, social-media carousels and impact-report layouts can all be made from templates and then customised with the NGO's own colours, logo, photographs and words. That consistency matters: donors recognise a campaign faster when every post looks like it comes from the same organisation. Two cautions apply. First, if your organisation is a school, a government body, a political organisation or a grantmaking foundation, the same page lists you as not eligible for this particular programme, and you should look at Canva's education or government programmes instead rather than applying and waiting. Second, templates are a starting point, not a finished campaign. Replace stock photographs with your own consented photographs wherever possible, because donors can tell the difference between a generic image and a real project photograph, and because using a stock image to imply a specific project outcome would be misleading.

## Google for Nonprofits: the back office most NGOs already need

Google for Nonprofits is less visible than a fundraising platform, but for many Indian NGOs it is the most valuable programme on this list, because it covers the everyday back office: professional email on your own domain, shared documents, storage, forms for volunteer applications, and video calls for remote teams. India is on the list of countries where the programme operates, and eligibility requires registration as a charitable organisation plus verification by Google's validation partner Goodstack, along with agreement to the programme's additional terms. Government entities, hospitals and healthcare organisations, and schools and universities are not eligible as such, although their charitable arms or foundations may be.

The practical sequence for a small NGO is to apply for Google for Nonprofits first and sort out the chatbot and design tools second, because a proper domain email address, shared drives with sensible permissions, and a form-based volunteer intake process prevent more chaos than any AI feature. Set up shared drives per project, not one giant drive for everything. Give volunteers access to only the folders they need. Keep beneficiary data in restricted folders, not in a drive shared with everyone. And remember that a Google for Nonprofits account does not automatically make your data practices compliant: you still need your own consent forms, retention rules and access controls, and you still need to check what the current programme includes in India, because not every product is available in every country.

## Zoho for Nonprofits: donor and volunteer records in one place

When an NGO grows past the spreadsheet stage, the problem is usually not writing appeals but remembering people: who donated, when, how much, through which campaign, whether they were thanked, and whether they gave again. Zoho for Nonprofits is built for that stage. Zoho offers nonprofit organisations a one-time credit of 5,000 US dollars to use across Zoho products, with Zoho covering up to half of subscription costs on each purchase, and the credits can be used for new purchases, upgrades, additional users and renewals. Applying requires a Zoho account, registration documents, and, if applying through TechSoup, a validation token. Zoho states that verification takes seven to ten business days, so do not plan a campaign launch around receiving the credits the same week you apply.

The honest question to ask before adopting any CRM, Zoho included, is whether your team will actually maintain it. A CRM with outdated donor records is worse than a spreadsheet that one person updates religiously, because the CRM creates a false sense of order. Start with one workflow only: record every donation and every thank-you in the CRM for one month, and see whether the team keeps it up. Only then add volunteer records, campaign tracking and reporting. Also note that a few Zoho apps are not covered by the programme, so check the programme details for the specific app you need before assuming the credit applies. And keep your donor data clean from the start: one record per donor, consistent spellings of names, and no duplicate entries created by importing the same list twice.

## Give.do and Ketto: fundraising where Indian donors already are

Fundraising platforms solve a trust problem that AI cannot solve. A donor who has never heard of your NGO is far more likely to give through a familiar platform page, with visible verification, regular updates and a receipt, than through a link to an unfamiliar payment page. Give.do, formerly GiveIndia, describes itself as a platform for verified nonprofits, with thousands of vetted NGOs across causes such as education, health, elderly care, disability, environment and women's empowerment, and it conducts due diligence and periodic verification visits. That verification is part of the value: it signals to a donor that someone has checked the organisation. Before launching, read the platform's current terms on fees, payout timing, donor-data access and update requirements, because those terms decide how much of each donation reaches you and how quickly.

Ketto works differently: it is a crowdfunding platform where anyone can start a fundraiser for free on the Bronze plan, with personalised or automated story creation depending on the plan. Its published pricing page lists a 5 percent platform fee on the Silver plan and a 7 percent platform fee on the Gold plan, with higher-touch support on the premium tiers. For an NGO, the choice between a verified-NGO platform and a general crowdfunding platform depends on the campaign. A verified-NGO page suits ongoing programme fundraising and repeat donors. A crowdfunding campaign suits a specific, urgent, time-bound goal with a clear story, such as medical support, a school building repair or flood relief supplies. In both cases, AI helps with the story draft, but the story itself must be true, specific and updated: donors give again when they see progress updates, photographs with consent, and honest reporting when a goal changes.

## Mailchimp: donor newsletters without spamming your list

Email remains the cheapest way to keep donors informed, and Mailchimp is the most widely known tool for it. Its free plan covers up to 250 contacts and 500 sends a month, with a daily send limit of 250, one audience and one seat. That is enough for a small NGO starting a monthly newsletter, but it is a hard ceiling: once the list grows past 250 contacts, sending stops until you upgrade or reduce the list. Mailchimp also offers a 15 percent discount to verified nonprofit organisations and charities, which is worth applying for before you pay for a larger plan. The practical discipline matters more than the discount. Send one useful update a month rather than four vague appeals. Segment the list so that a donor to an education project does not receive urgent appeals for an unrelated health project unless they asked for them. And always keep your own backup of the donor list, because a mailing platform account can be suspended, and your donor relationships should not live only inside one vendor's system.

For writing those newsletters, pair Mailchimp with the chatbot workflow from earlier in this article: draft in ChatGPT or Gemini, edit for accuracy and tone, then paste the final version into Mailchimp. Do not let an AI tool send email directly to donors without a human review step. A wrong name, a wrong amount or a wrong project detail in a mass email reaches hundreds of people at once and cannot be recalled. ## Sevastack: volunteer management built for Indian NGOs

Volunteer management is where many NGOs lose the most time, because volunteers arrive through WhatsApp, a Google Form, a friend's referral and a college noticeboard, and nobody has one list. Sevastack is a volunteer-management platform built for Indian nonprofits, and it addresses that specific mess: it ranks your own volunteer list against a project's requirements using AI, returning the top five matches scored out of 100 with a reason drawn from skills, availability and past activities, and it automatically emails a QR-verifiable certificate when an activity is marked complete. Volunteers log in through an OTP portal without a password, browse activities, log their own hours, download certificates and claim out-of-pocket costs, while the NGO collects ID proof and agreements through the portal instead of a chat group.

The free plan covers up to 25 volunteers, with 100 on Starter and 1,000 on Growth, and AI matching is available from the Growth plan upwards, so a very small NGO can start free but should not promise AI matching to its team until it checks which plan it is on. The deeper value is not the AI ranking but the hours record: when a funder asks how many volunteer hours went into a project, or when a volunteer asks for a certificate for a college or job application, the answer is already in the system instead of being reconstructed from chat history. As with any volunteer system, keep the human decision in the loop. An AI ranking is a shortlist, not a selection: the coordinator still checks availability, safety, language and local knowledge before assigning someone to a field activity, especially where children, patients or vulnerable people are involved.

## Otter.ai: turning field interviews into usable notes

Impact reports fail most often not at the writing stage but at the notes stage: a field worker records a long interview, nobody transcribes it, and the report ends up based on memory. Otter.ai's free Basic plan transcribes up to 300 minutes a month, with a limit of 30 minutes per conversation and up to three imported audio or video files per account. That is enough for a small NGO to transcribe a handful of short interviews each month, or to transcribe meeting recordings and then edit the transcript into report material. Longer interviews need to be split, and the import limit means the free plan cannot become a permanent archive for hundreds of recordings.

Two rules matter here. First, record only with consent, and tell the person being recorded how the transcript will be used and stored. A transcript of a beneficiary interview is sensitive material even when the person is willing to be recorded, and it should not be pasted into a public chatbot or shared outside the team. Second, treat the transcript as a draft: automated transcription makes mistakes with Indian names, place names and mixed Hindi-English speech, so a human must correct the transcript before any quote appears in a report. Used that way, transcription saves hours of typing and makes impact reports more honest, because they can quote what people actually said rather than what someone remembered weeks later.

## Using these tools in India: language, payments and field reality

India-specific reality shapes which tools work and which only look good in a demo. Language comes first. Most donors read English, but many volunteers, field staff and community members work in Hindi or a regional language. Test every AI tool in the language you actually use before committing a campaign to it. Draft in Hindi, summarise in Hinglish, and check whether the tool handles names and places correctly. A tool that writes fluent English but garbles Hindi names will create more editing work than it saves. For translation and language help, the guide to [AI translation tools for Hindi and English in India](/blog/ai-translation-tools-hindi-english-india/) is a useful companion, and the poster guide at [free AI poster makers in India](/blog/free-ai-poster-makers-india/) helps when a campaign needs print material for a local event.

Payments come second. Indian donors expect UPI, and fundraising platforms already support the payment methods donors trust. If you build your own donation page, you take on payment-gateway fees, reconciliation, refund handling and receipt generation yourself, and you must still prove to donors that the page belongs to a real, registered organisation. For most small NGOs, the platform route is cheaper in time even when the platform fee looks high, because staff time spent chasing payment failures is time not spent on programmes. Field reality comes third. Tools that assume constant high-speed internet fail in rural field visits, so prefer tools with good mobile apps, offline drafting in a notes app, and a workflow where field notes are written offline and uploaded later. Finally, keep a single source of truth for numbers: one spreadsheet or CRM record for donations, one for volunteer hours, and one for project outcomes. AI tools should read from that source, not invent a second version of it.

## What to avoid

Avoid pasting beneficiary data, donor phone numbers, bank details or children's information into a public AI chatbot. If a tool needs that data to work, it is the wrong tool for that job, or it needs a proper data-processing agreement and access controls first. Avoid publishing AI-generated beneficiary stories without verification: a moving story that never happened is a breach of donor trust and is unfair to the real people your NGO serves. Avoid buying a paid plan during a campaign week because a free limit blocked you once; wait until the same limit blocks you three times, because a one-off block is often a sign of a badly planned week, not a real capacity need. Avoid collecting volunteer or donor data you do not need, because every field you collect is a field you must protect. Avoid letting one person hold all the passwords: use shared drives, proper roles and a password manager so that a staff departure does not lock the NGO out of its own accounts. Avoid promising a funder a specific AI-generated output, such as a fully automated impact report, before you have tested the tool on your own data. And avoid copying another NGO's appeal, photographs or impact claims, even with AI paraphrasing, because that is still misrepresentation and donors notice it faster than you think.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which free AI tool should a small NGO in India start with?**
Start with ChatGPT free for drafting and Canva free for design, and apply for Canva Nonprofits and Google for Nonprofits if you are an eligible registered nonprofit. Those four cover writing, design and the back office without any payment, and you can add a fundraising platform and a volunteer system once you know which bottleneck actually hurts.

**Do these AI tools work in India without a VPN?**
Yes. ChatGPT, Gemini, Canva, Google Workspace, Zoho, Give.do, Ketto, Mailchimp, Sevastack and Otter.ai all work in India on a normal internet connection without a VPN. If a tool asks you to use a VPN, or if a payment page refuses an Indian card, treat that as a warning sign and check the tool's official India availability before relying on it.

**Can AI write a fundraising appeal that donors will trust?**
AI can draft an appeal, but trust comes from specifics only your NGO can supply: a real project, a real place, a real number, a consented photograph or quote, and a clear statement of how donations will be used. Edit every AI draft, remove invented details, and have someone who knows the project read the final version before it is published.

**Is Canva Nonprofits really free for Indian NGOs?**
Canva Nonprofits gives eligible charitable organisations free access to premium tools, and India is among the countries where nonprofits can apply, subject to registration proof, governing documents and Canva's review. Meeting the criteria does not guarantee approval, so apply early and do not plan a campaign launch around an approval that has not arrived yet.

**Should a small NGO pay for a CRM or keep using spreadsheets?**
Keep using spreadsheets until they demonstrably fail: duplicate donor records, missed thank-you notes, or volunteer hours that cannot be reconstructed at report time. At that point, a tool such as Zoho for Nonprofits or Sevastack earns its cost, because the failure it prevents is a lost donor or a failed funder report, not a minor inconvenience.

**How should an NGO handle donor and beneficiary data when using AI tools?**
Collect only what you need, keep sensitive data in restricted folders with limited access, never paste personal details into a public chatbot, and record consent for photographs, interviews and quotes. Check India's data-protection requirements and your own privacy policy, and ask a qualified professional if you are unsure, because this article is general information, not legal advice.

**Can AI tools prepare an impact report for a funder?**
AI tools can summarise notes, structure an outline and draft sections, but the numbers, outcomes and quotes must come from your own verified records. A funder report built on AI-invented figures will fail verification and damage the relationship, so treat every AI draft as a starting point that a human verifies line by line.

**What is the cheapest way to collect donations online in India?**
For most small NGOs, a verified-NGO platform such as Give.do or a crowdfunding campaign on Ketto is cheaper in staff time than building a custom payment page, even after platform fees. Compare the current fee, payout timing and donor-data access on each platform before launching, because those terms change and they decide how much of each donation reaches your programmes.

## Final verdict

Start free: ChatGPT for drafts, Canva Nonprofits for design, and Give.do or Ketto for fundraising. Add Zoho or Sevastack when spreadsheets break.
