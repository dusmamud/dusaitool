A small manufacturer in India does not lose money only on the shop floor. Money is lost when raw material runs out in the middle of a production run, when a finished batch fails a buyer inspection, when the same item is promised to two customers because stock was counted in two different notebooks, and when an order sits unread in a WhatsApp chat while the customer buys from someone else. Artificial intelligence will not fix a broken factory on its own, but the right tools can remove a large part of this daily guessing: what to make, how much raw material it needs, what is in stock, what is about to run out, and which finished pieces need a second look before dispatch.

The short answer is this: if you make and sell physical goods in India with a small team, start with Vyapar or Zoho Inventory for stock, production and orders, use TallyPrime if GST accounting and job costing are your main pain, and add a free Google Sheets plus Gemini forecast for demand planning before you pay for any expensive planning software. Use ChatGPT or Gemini photo checks only as a second pair of eyes for visible defects, never as the final quality decision. Tools like Katana and the India-built ShivAI suite are worth looking at once your catalogue or your order volume outgrows a basic billing app. Every tool in this article works in India without a VPN, on an ordinary phone or computer, and every price or free limit below comes with a warning that applies to all of them: prices and limits change, so re-check the official pricing and help page before you pay or promise a customer a deadline based on a tool.

If you only have time to set up one thing this week, set up correct stock first. Forecasting, scheduling and AI quality checks are all useless if the opening stock number they start from is wrong. Count physical stock once, enter it carefully, and only then switch on alerts and forecasts.

## 1. Vyapar: production, stock and GST billing for small units

Vyapar is an Indian billing and inventory app built for shops and small manufacturers, and its manufacturing features match the way a small unit actually works. You define the recipe for each finished product once, as a bill of materials: which raw materials, in what quantity, plus related expenses. After that, every production run automatically deducts the raw material from stock, adds the finished goods to stock, and updates costs. The app also gives low-stock alerts for both raw materials and finished goods, tracks expiring items, and produces manufacturing and consumption reports that show what was produced, at what cost, and what raw material was consumed.

For a small manufacturer, the practical value is simple. A spice-packing unit in Indore, a garment stitching unit in Tiruppur, or a small furniture workshop in Jodhpur no longer needs to remember recipes in a notebook or recalculate raw material after every batch. The recipe is entered once, and the arithmetic is done every time. Vyapar also handles GST-compliant invoicing, wholesale and customer rates, payment terms, and accounting reports, so a sale, a purchase and a production run all land in the same books rather than in three separate registers.

Vyapar offers a free trial of its full features, reported as seven days, and then paid plans. One third-party listing reported a Silver plan at around ₹308 per month on a longer purchase, but that figure should be treated as indicative only: check Vyapar's official pricing page before buying, because plan names and prices change. Vyapar works on phone and desktop, works in India without a VPN, and is a sensible first step if your main problems are production recording, stock accuracy and GST bills. ## 2. Zoho Inventory: one stock count across every sales channel

Zoho Inventory is a cloud-based inventory platform from Zoho, an Indian company, and it solves a different problem: overselling. If you sell the same product on Amazon, Flipkart, Shopify and your own store, a sale on one channel must reduce the stock shown on all the others, immediately. Zoho Inventory syncs stock in real time across those channels, and supports barcode scanning, batch tracking and automated order management. It also connects with Zoho Books, so a sales order can flow into a GST invoice without retyping.

Its free plan is genuinely useful for a small seller: it supports 50 orders a month, one user and one location, including composite items, dropshipment and backordering. Paid plans were reported in 2026 at around ₹999 per month billed annually for 500 orders a month, ₹2,299 for 3,000 orders, and higher tiers beyond that, excluding GST. Those figures come from a third-party pricing guide rather than Zoho's own page, so they may already be out of date, and you must confirm them on Zoho's official pricing page before budgeting. Zoho Inventory also integrates with Indian shipping partners such as Delhivery, Blue Dart, Shiprocket and XpressBees, which matters because dispatch is part of order handling, not an afterthought.

Choose Zoho Inventory over Vyapar when most of your orders come from online channels and the overselling problem is costing you cancellations and bad ratings. Choose Vyapar when your main work is production inside your own unit and most sales are offline or wholesale. Some units use both in different roles, but a small team should avoid running two stock systems at once: pick one as the single source of truth for stock, and let the other, if used at all, feed into it.

## 3. TallyPrime: GST, costing, job work and payroll in one system

TallyPrime is not marketed as an AI tool, and it would be dishonest to call it one. It belongs in this article because for many small Indian manufacturers it is the system that every AI or planning tool must read from or write to, and because getting production costing right inside Tally removes more errors than a flashy new dashboard. TallyPrime supports a bill of materials, manufacturing journals that convert raw material into finished goods, additional manufacturing costs such as electricity or labour added to the true cost of a product, co-products and scrap handling, job work, e-way bills, GST reconciliation and payroll.

A concrete example shows why the bill of materials matters. Suppose one wooden table needs one table top, four steel legs, ten screws and 200 millilitres of varnish, with about five percent wastage. Once that recipe is set in Tally, a manufacturing journal for ten tables deducts the right quantities automatically, instead of someone multiplying by hand and forgetting the wastage. TallyPrime also helps with GST: it can download portal data and compare book values with portal values, which highlights input tax credit mismatches before filing. TallyPrime is paid software sold through licences and partners, and its exact price depends on the edition and the partner, so get a quote from Tally Solutions or an authorised partner rather than trusting a single figure in any article, including this one.

Use TallyPrime as your base when your accountant already works in Tally, when your buyers need proper GST documentation, or when you need believable product costing rather than a rough guess. Planning tools such as ShivAI, covered below, are designed to read Tally data rather than replace it, which is a good sign: it means your Tally records become more valuable, not redundant.

## 4. Katana: visual production planning when SKUs multiply

Katana is a manufacturing and inventory platform built for small and mid-sized makers. It combines inventory management with visual production planning: you can see what needs to be made, what raw material is available, what is missing, and which orders are waiting, on one planning screen. It supports integrations with online sales channels and accounting tools, and offers an AI assistant and knowledge base for support.

Katana's free tier is a real, usable starting point for a very small catalogue: it covers up to 30 SKUs, with unlimited users, integrations and inventory locations, and access to all Katana features, with no card required for the free tier. Beyond that, Katana's paid Advantage plan uses custom pricing set after a scoping conversation, so there is no fixed published price to quote honestly here. That is a disadvantage for budgeting, but it is also honest: a unit with 200 SKUs and three warehouses genuinely needs a different setup from a unit with 25 SKUs.

Katana is priced in dollars, so a small Indian unit should check the current rupee cost, taxes and payment method before committing, and should test the free tier with real data first. It fits when your catalogue has outgrown a simple billing app, when you run production in batches against sales orders, and when you need to answer the question "can we promise this order by Friday?" from data rather than from memory. If you have fewer than 30 SKUs and simple production, Vyapar or Zoho Inventory will be simpler and cheaper.

## 5. Google Sheets plus Gemini: a free demand forecast from your own sales

The most useful AI technique for a small manufacturer is often the least glamorous: forecasting demand from your own past sales, so you order raw material before a festival rush instead of after it. You can build a workable version for free with Google Sheets and the Gemini API, and this matters because dedicated forecasting tools often cost hundreds of dollars a month, which is out of reach for a small unit.

The method is straightforward. Export at least 90 days of daily sales per SKU from your billing or inventory system; 180 days is better and a full year is ideal because it captures festival seasonality such as Diwali, Eid, Pongal and wedding-season demand. Put the sales in one tab and a forecast table in another, with columns for SKU, current stock, average daily sales, predicted daily sales for the next 30 days, predicted 30-day demand, days of stock remaining and whether a reorder is needed. A Gemini API key, available free from Google's AI developer site, can then analyse the sales summary for each SKU and return a 30-day demand estimate, considering trend, day-of-week patterns and seasonality. One guide to this method reported a free-tier allowance of 15 requests per minute and 1,500 requests per day, which is more than enough for a daily forecast of 100 SKUs, but free-tier limits change, so check Google's current limits before relying on them.

This approach has limits you should respect. A forecast is a probability, not a promise: a sudden bulk order, a supplier delay, a transport strike or a festival date shifting can all break it. Keep a safety stock for your top-selling SKUs, review the forecast weekly rather than monthly, and never let a spreadsheet order raw material without a human approving the purchase order. Used that way, a free forecast beats gut feeling by a useful margin, and it costs nothing to test for a month. ## 6. ChatGPT and Gemini photo checks: a second pair of eyes, not a final judge

Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT and Gemini can look at a photo of a finished product and comment on visible problems: a scratch, a dent, a stain, uneven stitching, a misprinted label or a wrong colour. For a small unit without a dedicated quality team, this is genuinely useful as a screening step. A worker can photograph a batch sample, ask the AI to list anything that looks wrong, and then put the flagged pieces aside for a human inspector. Google has even published a manufacturing example in which a camera feed over a line is streamed to Gemini's live API to read product codes, detect and classify visual defects, measure them and raise alerts, with results written to a database for later analysis.

But the honest limits are serious, and they come from independent testing, not from marketing pages. In one published test on industrial inspection images, GPT-4o and Gemini models correctly answered "defect or no defect" only about 73 percent and 66 percent of the time respectively, well below specialised inspection systems, and analysis took one to ten seconds per image through an API, which is too slow for a fast-moving line. Lighting, angle, dust, glare and camera quality all change the result. That means photo AI can help where products vary a lot and where a human reviews every flagged item, but it cannot replace a human inspector for safety-critical goods, for export buyers with strict acceptance criteria, or for defects that are internal, structural or measurable only with instruments.

Practical rules for using photo checks well: photograph under the same light every time, against a plain background, from the same distance; write a short checklist of what counts as a defect for that product, with sizes where possible, and paste it into the prompt; keep the AI's verdict and the human's final decision in the same record, so you can see over time where the AI is reliable and where it is not; and never ship a batch on the strength of an AI "looks fine" alone. For food, medicine, electrical safety or children's products, follow the applicable Indian standards and buyer requirements first, and treat AI as an extra screen only.

## 7. ShivAI: India-built AI planning that sits on top of Tally

ShivAI is a desktop software suite built for Indian manufacturers and distributors, and it is worth knowing about precisely because it does not ask you to throw away your existing system. It reads data from Tally, Zoho Books, Busy, Marg or Excel, and then adds the planning layer: production scheduling, material planning through the bill of materials, demand forecasting, inventory planning, and cash, credit and bank-limit planning. Its manufacturing planner covers multi-level bills of materials, time-phased netting, capacity checks, lead times, yields and safety stock measured from your own history, plus a comparison of plan versus actual with reasons for misses. Its scheduler offers multiple scheduling methods, including AI optimisation options, and can re-schedule when a machine breaks down mid-shift.

ShivAI's published pricing at the time of checking was a 30-day paid pilot at ₹5,900 including GST, with installation and training, and a single-location manufacturing planner plan at ₹2,950 per month including GST, or ₹29,500 per year with two months free, with extra users at ₹590 per month. Prices include 18 percent GST. As with every price in this article, confirm these figures on ShivAI's official site before paying, because plans change. Data stays on your own computer, which some factory owners prefer over a purely cloud system, and the tool works alongside Tally rather than replacing it.

ShivAI fits a unit that already keeps decent Tally records, that plans production weekly, and that wants to answer questions such as what to make this week, what to order from each supplier, and which stock will run out or expire. It is probably too much, too soon, for a unit that has not yet fixed basic stock counting. Fix the base first, then add planning.

## 8. WhatsApp Business: where small manufacturers actually receive orders

A large share of orders for small Indian manufacturers still arrives on WhatsApp: a photo from a buyer, a quantity, a rate question, a "please confirm" message. WhatsApp Business, the free business version of WhatsApp, gives you a business profile, a product catalogue, quick replies for repeated questions, labels to mark orders as new, confirmed, in production or dispatched, and automated greeting and away messages. Used properly, it stops orders from disappearing into a personal chat history.

The workflow that works is boring and effective. Every confirmed order gets a label, a catalogue entry or a written confirmation message with quantity, rate, delivery date and advance, and a daily habit of moving labelled chats through production and dispatch stages. Combined with Vyapar or Zoho Inventory, WhatsApp becomes the front door and the inventory system becomes the record; neither should be the only record. WhatsApp Business is free to download and use, though messaging and business charges can apply for some business messaging features, so check WhatsApp's current business terms if you plan to send bulk messages. ## Using these tools in India: a realistic rollout

A small manufacturer in India faces conditions that a generic tool guide often ignores, so plan for them from the start. First, connectivity: many workshops have patchy internet, so prefer tools with a mobile app and offline billing or stock entry where possible, and sync when the connection returns, rather than a tool that stops completely without internet. Second, language: your workers may be more comfortable in Hindi, Tamil, Marathi, Bengali or another Indian language than in English, so choose tools with local-language support or simple screens, and write your product names and checklists in the language your team actually reads. Third, festivals and seasons drive demand more than any monthly average: build your forecast around Diwali, Eid, Pongal, Raksha Bandhan, wedding season and local harvest or school cycles, and keep the previous year's festival sales as a separate reference, not buried inside a yearly average.

Fourth, GST and buyer documentation are part of production, not paperwork after production. A GST invoice, an e-way bill where required, a batch or lot number for traceability, and a delivery challan should come out of the same system that recorded production, so the numbers match when a buyer or an auditor asks. Fifth, start small and in order. A sensible four-week rollout is: week one, count stock and enter correct bills of materials; week two, run all new orders and production through one system and switch on low-stock alerts; week three, add a simple weekly forecast for your top ten SKUs; week four, add photo quality screening for one product line and review where it helped and where it misled you. Do not buy three tools in week one. One working system beats three half-configured ones.

Costs deserve the same honesty. Free tiers are real, but they end: Zoho Inventory's free plan covers only 50 orders a month, Katana's free tier covers only 30 SKUs, and a Gemini free tier can change without notice. Budget for the paid tier you will actually need at your real order volume, not the free tier you start on, and include GST at 18 percent where it applies. Also budget for training time: a tool your storekeeper will not use is more expensive than a slightly costlier tool he will use daily. Finally, keep your data exportable. Before you commit to any tool, check that you can export your items, stock, customers and orders to Excel or CSV. If a tool locks your own records inside itself, that is a reason to be cautious, however good its demo looks.

## What to avoid

Avoid buying an expensive all-in-one ERP before you have fixed stock counting, because an ERP will faithfully automate your existing errors at a higher price. Avoid letting any AI tool place orders, promise delivery dates or approve dispatches without a human sign-off, especially during your first three months with it. Avoid using a photo AI as the only quality check for goods where a failure could harm someone or cost you a major buyer: use it as a screen, and keep a trained human as the judge. Avoid tools that need a VPN to work in India, tools with no Indian payment option, and tools whose support only answers in a time zone twelve hours away when your production problem is happening now. Avoid entering the same stock in two places, because two stock numbers will disagree, and nobody will know which one is true. And avoid sharing your factory's pricing, buyer list or product designs with a public AI chatbot in a way you would not share with a stranger: use business or enterprise settings where available, keep sensitive prompts generic, and never paste a buyer's confidential drawing or a customer's personal data into a free public tool without checking its data policy.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which is the best AI tool for a small manufacturer in India?**
There is no single best tool, because the answer depends on your bottleneck. If production recording and stock are the problem, start with Vyapar. If online overselling is the problem, start with Zoho Inventory. If GST and costing are the problem, start with TallyPrime. Add forecasting and photo checks only after stock is accurate.

**Can a small factory use AI without buying expensive machines or cameras?**
Yes. Most of the value for a small unit comes from software, not hardware: stock and production records, demand forecasting from past sales, and photo checks with an ordinary phone camera. Specialised vision cameras and sensors are for larger lines, and you should only consider them after the software basics are working and paying for themselves.

**Is Zoho Inventory really free for small sellers?**
Zoho Inventory has a free plan covering 50 orders a month, one user and one location. That suits a genuinely small seller. Once you cross 50 orders a month, you need a paid plan, so treat the free plan as a trial of your real workflow, not a permanent solution at higher volumes. Confirm the current limits on Zoho's official pricing page.

**Can ChatGPT or Gemini replace a quality inspector?**
No. Independent testing found general AI models correct on defect-or-no-defect questions only around two-thirds to three-quarters of the time on industrial images, and they are sensitive to lighting and camera angle. Use them to flag pieces for human review, especially for visible surface defects, and keep a human inspector as the final decision-maker.

**Do these tools work with GST billing in India?**
Vyapar and TallyPrime are built for GST billing and related Indian compliance, and Zoho Inventory connects with Zoho Books for GST invoicing. Katana and ShivAI handle production and planning and are normally used alongside an Indian accounting system, not instead of one. Always confirm the current GST features with the vendor, because tax rules and software features both change.

**How much should a small manufacturer budget for these tools?**
A realistic starting budget is a few hundred to a few thousand rupees a month, plus GST where applicable, once you outgrow free tiers: for example, reported Zoho Inventory paid plans from around ₹999 a month billed annually, and ShivAI's manufacturing planner from ₹2,950 a month including GST at the time of checking. Add training time and, if you choose Katana, a dollar-priced custom plan. Re-check official pricing before committing, because these figures change.

**What should I do before paying for any manufacturing tool?**
Run a two-week trial with your real data: enter ten real products with correct bills of materials, record ten real production runs, enter your real opening stock, and process ten real orders end to end, including a GST invoice and a dispatch. If the tool survives two real weeks, including a stock count that matches the shelf, it is worth paying for. If it only looks good in a demo, it is not.

## Final verdict

Start with Vyapar or Zoho Inventory for stock and orders, add Sheets plus Gemini forecasting, and keep a human checking every quality call.
