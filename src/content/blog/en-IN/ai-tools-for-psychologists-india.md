A psychologist's working day is not only the fifty minutes in the session. It is the booking message at 9 pm, the rescheduled appointment, the intake form that never came back, the invoice that is two weeks late, and the progress note that still has to be written at 10 pm after the last client has left. AI tools promise to take some of that admin away. Some of them do, and genuinely well. But in therapy, the admin is not neutral paperwork. A scheduling tool holds a client's name and phone number. A notes tool holds what a client said in their most private hour. A client-management system holds the whole story of a practice. So the question for a psychologist in India is never only "which tool is fastest?" It is "which tool is fast, honest about its limits, and safe enough for the trust a client places in me?"

The short answer is this: start simple. Use [Calendly](https://calendly.com/pricing) or Google Calendar appointment scheduling for bookings, keep your clinical notes in a private system you control, and add an AI notes tool such as Mentalyc or Upheal only after you have written client consent, checked where the data goes, and committed to reading and signing every note yourself. If you want one all-in-one practice system, [SimplePractice](https://www.simplepractice.com/pricing/) and TherapyNotes are the established names, but both are built for the United States, bill in US dollars, and carry insurance features that mean little in Indian private practice. Nothing here replaces your professional judgement, your ethics code, or legal advice. Prices and free limits change often, so before you pay for anything, or promise a client a deadline or a feature, re-check the official pricing or help page linked in each section.

This article is general information only, not professional, legal, or medical advice. Mental health practice in India sits under real rules — the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act), and, for clinical psychologists, Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) registration and guidance — and those rules, plus your own ethics code and any guidance from your institution or supervisor, must be checked with official and professional sources before you change how you handle client data. If you are a client reading this, these tools support a therapist's work; they do not make a therapist good, and no AI tool can replace a qualified professional. If you are in distress, call Tele MANAS, India's free national mental health helpline, on **14416**, any time of day or night.

## SimplePractice: the all-in-one practice system

[SimplePractice](https://www.simplepractice.com/pricing/) is the best-known practice management system for therapists, and its appeal is that almost everything lives in one place: scheduling, a client portal, intake forms, notes, secure messaging, and billing. For a solo psychologist, the Starter plan is listed at $49 a month, after a 50 percent discount for the first three months, and a free trial runs to 30 days if you need more time. Higher plans add group appointments, premium phone support, and advanced analytics, starting at $74 a practitioner for groups, with custom pricing for groups of six or more.

The honest India check is this. SimplePractice works as a website from India on a normal internet connection, with no VPN needed, but it is a US product. Its insurance claims, superbills, and eligibility checks are built for the American system and will not map onto Indian practice, where most therapy is paid directly by the client. You will be billed in US dollars, so the monthly cost in rupees moves with the exchange rate, and support hours and payment processing are set up for US users. That does not make it a bad tool. If you want one system for booking, forms, notes, and reminders, and you are happy to ignore the insurance half of it, it is a serious option. But do not buy it for features you will never use in India, and do not assume that because it is HIPAA-compliant in the United States, it automatically satisfies Indian law. HIPAA is American law. India's DPDP Act is a different law, with its own consent and security duties, and compliance with one is not compliance with the other.

## TherapyNotes: notes, scheduling and a client portal

TherapyNotes is the other established all-in-one name, and it covers a similar spread: unlimited clients, appointments, and notes on every plan, a client portal, secure messaging, file storage, backups, and basic telehealth. Its standard Solo subscription is $69 a month for a single user, and every plan starts with a 30-day free trial. Group pricing is $89 a month for the first clinician and $60 a month per additional clinician, with unlimited non-clinical users such as schedulers and billers. Optional extras are charged separately: appointment reminders by text or voice cost 14 cents each, while email reminders are free, and credit card processing is charged per transaction.

Two warnings matter before you pay. First, TherapyNotes itself notes that updated subscription pricing takes effect on 1 December 2026, so the figures in this article may already be out of date by the time you read them. Re-check its pricing page before subscribing. Second, the same India caveat applies as with SimplePractice: this is a US system, billed in dollars, with insurance claims and remittance features built for American practice. Its telehealth, portal, and notes tools are usable from India, but its billing machinery is not an Indian billing solution. If your practice is small and mostly direct-pay, compare its $69 a month honestly against a combination of a free booking tool and a notes tool, which may cost far less and do everything you actually need.

## Mentalyc: AI progress notes that build on the last session

Mentalyc is different from the two systems above because it does only one job: clinical documentation. It drafts progress notes, treatment plans, and progress summaries from a session, using templates such as SOAP and DAP on every plan, with BIRP, GIRP, and EMDR notes and a template builder on higher plans. Its comparison of itself with Upheal reports that Mentalyc is used by more than 30,000 clinicians, has been built only for clinical documentation since 2021, and is HIPAA-compliant with a signed business associate agreement on every account. Plans start from $14.99 a month billed yearly, or $19.99 month to month, each with a note limit ranging from 40 to 330 notes, and a 14-day full Pro trial covers 15 notes with no credit card required.

Its privacy defaults are the reason it leads this category. Mentalyc states that it never trains its AI on your sessions, deletes audio once the note is ready, and keeps an anonymised transcript only until you delete it. That is a stronger starting position than many AI tools, and it is exactly the kind of sentence you should look for before trusting any notes tool with a session. Even so, a drafted note is a draft, not a record. Read it fully, correct anything it invented, softened, or missed — AI summaries do hallucinate, and in a clinical note a hallucinated sentence is not a typo, it is a false record — and only then sign it. Never let an AI note go into a file unsigned, and never record a session without the client's clear, specific, informed consent, given before the session starts, with the right to refuse or withdraw it.

## Upheal: AI notes with telehealth and scheduling built in

[Upheal](https://www.upheal.io/pricing) is the broader alternative to Mentalyc: it is a transcription-first note taker and a practice system in one, bundling telehealth video, scheduling, a client portal, and payments, with insurance billing now listed as a live feature for US users. Its free tier is genuinely usable for trying the core idea: basic AI notes from past sessions, plus telehealth calling without AI, using text-to-note, dictation, or uploads, for individual therapy only. The AI Notes plan is $39 a month flat, with unlimited sessions, unlimited AI notes by video, audio, dictation, or uploads, support for individuals, couples, and families, all note templates, built-in telehealth with automated notes, and treatment plans.

The privacy picture needs a careful read. Upheal is HIPAA-compliant, with a completed SOC 2 Type II audit, AES-256 encryption at rest, TLS encryption in transit, and a signed business associate agreement. But one comparison reports that Upheal uses de-identified data to improve its AI unless you opt out, where Mentalyc does not train on sessions at all. Sources can differ on details like this, and privacy pages change, so before you use Upheal with real clients, open its current privacy and security pages yourself, find the opt-out, and set it the way you want it before the first session, not after. For an Indian practice, Upheal's telehealth and scheduling bundle is attractive, but its billing features are again US-shaped, and its per-session billing on the EHR plan counts scheduled sessions and telehealth calls as billable sessions even when no AI note is generated, which is a detail worth checking on its pricing page before you choose a plan.

## Calendly: booking without the message tennis

[Calendly](https://calendly.com/pricing) solves the smallest, most annoying problem in a therapy practice: the ten-message exchange to find a time. You set your working hours, buffers between sessions, and how far ahead clients can book, and you share one link. The free plan includes one event type, one calendar connection, one-on-one scheduling, a customisable booking page, and a browser extension. That is enough for a solo psychologist with one session length. The Standard plan, at $10 per seat a month billed yearly, adds unlimited event types, multiple calendars, automations and reminders, and connections to Stripe and PayPal for collecting payment before a meeting. The Teams plan, at $16 per seat a month, adds round-robin and team scheduling for group practices.

For India, Calendly's strength is its simplicity. It works from India on a normal connection, clients need no account to book, and it connects to Google Calendar and Zoom or Google Meet. Its weakness is that it is a booking tool, not a clinical system: it holds names, emails, and appointment times, but it is not where clinical notes belong. Keep that boundary clean. Also check payment support for India before promising it to clients: Stripe and PayPal connections are listed on its pricing page, but whether they accept the payment methods your clients actually use, in rupees, is something to test with a small real booking before you announce it as your payment route.

## Google Calendar: appointment scheduling you may already have

Google Calendar's appointment scheduling does much of what Calendly does, inside a tool many psychologists already use every day. You create a booking page, set your availability, buffers, and booking limits, and clients pick a slot without needing an account. Personal users can create an appointment scheduling link for free. The catch, reported in Calendly's own comparison, is that the full set of scheduling options requires a Google Workspace subscription, with Business Starter at $6 per user a month, Business Standard at $12, and Business Plus at $18, each on a yearly commitment. If you already pay for Workspace for your practice email, this may cost you nothing extra. If you do not, Calendly's free plan is the cheaper starting point.

Whichever booking tool you choose, two habits protect your practice more than any feature. First, build in buffers: back-to-back sessions with no gap leave no time for notes, water, or a client who runs five minutes over, and a calendar that looks efficient on screen becomes exhaustion by Thursday. Second, keep your booking page professional and minimal: session length, mode (in person or online), and a short note about cancellations are enough. A booking page is not the place for clinical promises, and a client should never learn your fees, your cancellation policy, or your limits to confidentiality for the first time from a calendar link.

## Notion: organising the practice, not holding the clinical record

[Notion](https://www.notion.com/pricing) is free for individuals, including databases, subtasks, dependencies, custom properties, basic forms and sites, and Notion Calendar, with Plus at $10 per member a month adding file uploads, custom forms, and collaborative blocks. For a psychologist, that makes it a genuinely good place for practice admin: session-plan templates, reading lists, supervision notes without identifying details, workshop planning, fee and expense tracking, and a library of worksheets and resources you reuse with clients.

The line to hold is bright. Notion is a general productivity tool, not a clinical record system, and it is not designed for identifiable client health data. Use it for templates, anonymised supervision reflections, and business admin, and keep identifiable clinical notes, recordings, and assessments in a system built for health data, with encryption, access controls, and an audit trail. If you are tempted to keep a client database in Notion, stop and ask three questions: who else can access this workspace, what happens if the account is breached, and could I explain this choice to the client and to my ethics body? If any answer is uncomfortable, the note does not belong there.

## Otter.ai: transcription for supervision, training and dictation

[Otter.ai](https://otter.ai/pricing) transcribes speech into searchable text, and it is useful in a therapy practice in specific, limited ways: transcribing your own supervision reflections, dictating a note after a session, or capturing a training workshop you attended. Its Basic plan is free and includes 300 transcription minutes a month, live transcription, speaker identification, and apps for iOS and Android, working with Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet. The Pro plan is $16.99 a month, or $8.49 a month billed yearly, with 1,200 in-app recording minutes, up to 90 minutes per meeting, and advanced search and export.

Transcribing a real therapy session with a general transcription tool is a different decision from transcribing your own dictation, and it should be treated as one. A session transcript is the most sensitive record a practice can hold, and a general tool's data-use and retention terms are not written for clinical work. If you ever transcribe a session, you need the client's explicit consent first, a clear reason why a transcript is needed rather than a note, and a plan for deleting the recording and transcript when they are no longer needed. For most psychologists, Otter's honest role is dictation and supervision, not session capture. ## Using these tools in India: what actually changes

The first India difference is money. Most serious therapy tools are priced in US dollars, and a $49 or $69 monthly subscription is a real cost for a solo Indian practice, especially in the early years when client numbers are still growing. Start free where you honestly can — Calendly's free plan, Google Calendar scheduling, Notion's free plan, Upheal's free tier, Otter's free minutes — and pay only when a tool is saving you measurable hours every week. A tool that costs more than the admin time it saves is not a practice investment; it is a subscription habit.

The second difference is payments and reminders. Indian clients live on UPI and WhatsApp, and it is tempting to run a practice through both. UPI payment links from your bank or a payment provider are fine for fees, but keep payment and clinical conversation separate: a payment message should say nothing about why the client is seeing you. WhatsApp is convenient for reminders, but it is not a secure clinical channel. A reminder that says "your appointment is tomorrow at 5 pm" is reasonable; a clinical note, an assessment result, or a discussion of session content over WhatsApp is not. If a client prefers WhatsApp, agree in advance what it will and will not be used for, and write that agreement down.

The third difference is language and access. Many of your clients may be more comfortable in Hindi, or in a regional language, than in English, and most AI tools covered here work best in English. Do not let a tool's English-only intake form, note template, or automated reminder become a barrier for a client. Check whether the tool handles the languages you actually practise in, test its booking page on a cheap Android phone on mobile data — because that is how many clients will reach you — and keep a phone-booking option for clients who cannot or will not book online. Good practice technology widens access; it never narrows it to the clients who are already comfortable with apps.

The fourth difference is the law and ethics frame, and it is the most important one. Under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, information obtained during care or treatment must be kept confidential, with only narrow exceptions such as disclosure to a nominated representative or where required by law. Under the DPDP Act, 2023, personal data must be processed on the basis of free, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent, with clear notice about what is collected and why, easy withdrawal of consent, reasonable security safeguards such as encryption, access controls, and access logs, and respect for rights to correction and erasure. Tele-psychotherapy guidance from NIMHANS-linked sources adds practical duties: verify the client's identity, share your own credentials including your RCI registration number where applicable, obtain informed consent that covers the technology itself and its confidentiality risks, explain the limits to confidentiality, document sessions in a uniform format, and never record or share session content over email or social messaging apps. Some of that guidance has also cautioned against AI-based interventions in tele-sessions unless the concerned agency has approved them, which is a further reason to treat AI notes as an admin aid you review and sign, not as a clinical intervention in itself. None of this is legal advice, and the DPDP rules and professional guidance are still evolving, so check the current official text and your professional body's position before you rely on any summary, including this one.

## What to avoid

Avoid recording a session without explicit, prior, written consent that names the tool, the purpose, and how long the recording will be kept. Avoid pasting identifiable client material into a free general chatbot to "summarise" it, because a general chatbot is not a clinical system, its data-use terms are not written for therapy, and you cannot control where that text goes next. Avoid letting an AI tool set the treatment plan, diagnose, or decide risk on its own: AI can draft and organise, but clinical judgement, including risk assessment, stays with you, and a tool that claims otherwise is selling something it cannot deliver. Avoid storing identifiable notes in a shared drive, a general notes app, or a chat group, and avoid keeping client data on a personal device with no passcode, no encryption, and no backup plan. Avoid promising a client that a tool is "fully private" or "HIPAA-certified, so it is safe in India": neither claim is accurate on its own, and a client who consents on the basis of a promise you cannot keep has not given informed consent at all. Finally, avoid building your whole practice on a single free tier: free limits shrink, tools close, and pricing changes, as TherapyNotes' December 2026 change shows, so keep your own exportable copies of your records in a format you can move.

## FAQ

**Can an AI tool replace a psychologist or therapist?**

No. AI tools can draft notes, manage bookings, and organise admin, but they cannot hold a therapeutic relationship, exercise clinical judgement, or assess risk in a real session. Any tool that claims to replace a therapist should not be trusted with your practice or your clients.

**Do I need client consent before using an AI notes tool?**

Yes. Get clear, specific, informed consent before recording or processing session material with any AI tool, explaining what is recorded, which tool processes it, where the data is stored, how long it is kept, and that consent can be withdrawn. Record that consent in the client's file, and do not record if consent is refused.

**Is HIPAA compliance enough for a practice in India?**

No. HIPAA is United States law. In India, you must also consider the DPDP Act, 2023, the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, and your professional ethics code and registration requirements. A tool can be HIPAA-compliant and still need a separate check for Indian law, data location, and consent.

**Which tool is best for a solo psychologist just starting out in India?**

Start free: Calendly or Google Calendar for bookings, Notion for practice admin and templates, and a private, secure system for clinical notes. Add a paid notes tool such as Mentalyc or Upheal only when note-writing is genuinely taking hours each week and you have consent and privacy checks in place.

**Can I use WhatsApp for appointment reminders and client messages?**

A short appointment reminder is reasonable, but WhatsApp is not a secure clinical channel. Do not send clinical notes, assessment results, or session content over WhatsApp, and agree with each client in advance what WhatsApp will and will not be used for.

**Should I record therapy sessions for AI notes?**

Only with explicit prior consent, a clear clinical or admin reason, and a plan for storage and deletion. Many practices do better with post-session dictation, where you speak a short summary into a notes tool yourself, than with full session recording, which creates a far more sensitive record.

**What happens to my notes if a tool raises its price or shuts down?**

You can lose access, which is why export matters. Before subscribing, check that you can export your notes and client list in a usable format, keep your own regular exports, and never let a single tool be the only copy of a clinical record.

**Are free AI tools safe for client data?**

Free is not a privacy rating. Some free tools are careful with data and some are not, and terms change. Read the privacy policy, check whether the tool trains its AI on your data, look for an opt-out, and never put identifiable client material into a free general-purpose chatbot.

**Do these tools work in India without a VPN?**

The tools in this article are ordinary web services, accessible from India on a normal connection, and none of them lists a VPN requirement. Billing is usually in US dollars, some payment and insurance features are US-only, and data may be stored outside India, so check pricing, payment support, and data location before subscribing.

**Where can a client in distress get help in India?**

Call Tele MANAS on **14416**, India's free, confidential, round-the-clock mental health helpline, or go to a doctor, counsellor, or hospital near you. AI tools and articles are not emergency services and must never be used as a substitute for urgent help.

## Final verdict

Start with Calendly and a private notes system; add Mentalyc or Upheal only with written client consent, and never let an AI note go unsigned. That order — bookings first, private notes second, AI notes last and only with consent — keeps the admin gains without trading away the confidentiality that therapy depends on. Re-check prices and privacy pages before you pay, keep your own exportable records, and let your ethics code, not a tool's marketing page, decide what belongs in your practice.
