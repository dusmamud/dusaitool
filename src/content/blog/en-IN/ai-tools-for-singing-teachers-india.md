## The direct answer

A singing teacher in India does two jobs at once: teaching the student to sing in tune, and keeping them practising between classes. The hardest part is usually the second one, because a student who practises for ten minutes a day improves far faster than one who only sings during the weekly class. Indian classical music adds its own demands, with riyaz, sargam, alankars and taal, and a teacher cannot sit beside every student every day. That is where AI tools help: apps that listen to pitch, give a tanpura and tabla to practise with, and make practice fun enough that students actually do it. The direct answer is simple: use Riyaz for structured Indian classical practice, Smule for fun singing with backing tracks, Vanido for pitch and ear training, iTabla Pro for tanpura and tabla accompaniment, and ChatGPT to plan lessons and write practice schedules.

These tools work because they fill the gap between classes. A student can open the Riyaz app, sing sargam with a tanpura drone, and get instant feedback on whether each note was in tune. A teacher can set a practice plan, listen to a student's recording, and use the next class for correction instead of repeating the same warm-up. But there is an honest limit: no app can hear the things a good teacher hears, such as breath control, voice quality, or whether a student is straining their throat. Apps are practice partners, not teachers.

## 1. Riyaz app: daily practice built for Indian classical music

The Riyaz app is made for Indian classical singers, which is exactly why it suits teachers in India. It offers structured lessons in sargam, alankars and ragas, with a tanpura drone and pitch feedback that shows whether each note was in tune. A teacher can assign specific lessons to a student and ask them to practise a set number of minutes each day, and the student gets a clear, game-like path to follow instead of a vague instruction to practise more. The basic practice features are free, and premium adds more lessons and features, so check current pricing on the official page before paying.

How to start: pick one alankar for the whole class this week and ask every student to practise it in the app for ten minutes a day. Watch out for: the app scores pitch, not voice quality or expression, so praise the student who sings beautifully even when the score is lower.

## 2. Smule: make singing fun so students keep coming back

Smule is a social singing app where students sing popular songs, film songs and devotional songs with backing tracks, alone or as a duet with another singer. For a teacher, its value is motivation: a shy student who will not sing in class will often sing happily into a phone, and a teenager who finds classical practice boring will practise a film song for an hour. The teacher can listen to the recordings, hear where the pitch slips, and use the fun song as a bridge to the serious lesson. Smule is free to use, and VIP is paid, so check the official page before upgrading.

How to start: ask students to record one song a week on Smule and send you the link, then discuss one thing they did well and one thing to fix. Watch out for: Smule is a public social app, so students should use private settings and never share personal details or meet strangers from the app.

## 3. Vanido: pitch training with instant feedback

Vanido is a vocal training app that gives daily exercises for pitch, breathing and range, with real-time feedback on whether you hit each note. It starts with a short test to find the student's vocal range, then builds a personal daily routine, which is useful for a teacher managing many students at different levels. A student who practises fifteen minutes a day with Vanido usually improves pitch accuracy faster than one who only sings in class. The app is free with premium exercises, so check current pricing on the official page before paying.

How to start: ask each new student to take the range test in the first week, so you know their comfortable notes before choosing songs. Watch out for: if a student reports pain, hoarseness or a sore throat, stop the exercises and rest the voice; an app cannot tell the difference between effort and strain.

## 4. iTabla Pro: a tanpura and tabla that never gets tired

iTabla Pro is the professional tanpura, tabla and metronome app used by many Indian classical musicians, and it is the closest thing to having an accompanist in your pocket. It gives a realistic tanpura drone in any scale, tabla thekas in many taals, and a metronome, all adjustable in tempo and pitch. For a teacher, it means every student can practise with proper accompaniment at home instead of singing alone in silence, and for the class it means a reliable drone that never goes out of tune. It is a paid app, so check the current price on the official page before buying.

How to start: set the tanpura to the student's scale before the lesson, and teach them to do the same at home. Watch out for: accompaniment helps practice, but a student should also practise without it sometimes, so they learn to hold the sur on their own.

## 5. ChatGPT: lesson plans, schedules and parent messages

ChatGPT handles the paperwork of a singing teacher: a weekly lesson plan for a beginner, a practice chart for a student preparing for an exam, or a polite message to parents explaining what the child should practise. Give it the student's level, the raga or song, and the time available, and it drafts a plan you can edit. It can also explain a concept in simple words, such as what a taal is, or suggest warm-up exercises for a group class. The free plan is enough for daily teaching work, and paid plans add more features, so check current pricing on the official page.

How to start: ask it to draft a four-week practice plan for a beginner student, then adjust it to your own method before sharing it. Watch out for: AI can get Indian classical terms wrong, such as raga rules or taal cycles, so check every detail against your own knowledge before teaching it.

## Using AI in India: singing teachers

Singing teaching in India has its own shape, and the tools should fit it. First, tradition: Indian classical music is taught guru-shishya style, with listening and imitation, so use apps for practice between classes, never as a replacement for the teacher's ear. Second, language: students may sing in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi or another language, so choose apps that support the language and the songs your students actually sing. Third, exams and performances: many students prepare for Prayag Sangeet Samiti, Gandharva Mahavidyalaya or school exams, so plan practice around those syllabuses and performance dates.

## What to avoid

Avoid any app that promises to make a student a singer in thirty days, because voice training takes months of steady practice. Avoid letting students strain their voices to hit high notes for a score, and avoid practising when the throat is sore. Avoid teaching a raga rule or a taal cycle from a chatbot without checking it yourself, because AI gets Indian classical details wrong. Avoid posting a child's singing video publicly without the parent's written permission, and avoid sharing a student's phone number or address in any singing group.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best free app for riyaz in India?**
The Riyaz app is built for Indian classical practice, with sargam, alankars and pitch feedback, and its basic features are free. For accompaniment, iTabla Pro is the professional choice, though it is a paid app.

**Can an app really teach my child to sing?**
No. An app can give practice, pitch feedback and accompaniment, but only a teacher can hear voice quality, correct breathing and protect a young voice from strain. Use apps for practice between classes, not as a replacement for the teacher.

**How much should a singing student practise each day?**
For most students, fifteen to thirty minutes of focused daily practice beats two hours once a week. A teacher can set a small daily target in the Riyaz app and check it at the next class.

**Which app is best for tanpura and tabla at home?**
iTabla Pro is the standard choice among Indian classical musicians, with realistic tanpura, many taals and a metronome. It is a paid app, so check the current price on the official page.

**Is Smule safe for children?**
Smule is a public social app, so children should use private settings, never share personal details, and only connect with people they know. A teacher or parent should check the settings and review recordings before anything is posted publicly.

**Can ChatGPT write lesson plans for singing classes?**
Yes, it can draft weekly plans, practice charts and parent messages, which saves a teacher time. But always check Indian classical details, such as raga rules and taal cycles, against your own knowledge, because AI can get them wrong.
