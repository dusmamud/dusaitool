Public speaking in India is rarely about one big stage. It is the class presentation in college, the sprint demo at work, the wedding toast, the school assembly speech, the sales pitch to a client, the viva answer, and the job interview where you must sound calm even when your heart is racing. Most people do not need a new personality to speak well. They need private repetition, honest feedback on pace and filler words, and one or two chances to speak in front of real people before the day that counts. That is exactly what AI speaking tools are good at, and exactly where they stop being useful.

The short answer for India is this: start free with **Yoodli** for judged practice sessions and **PowerPoint Speaker Coach** if you already use PowerPoint, because both cost nothing to begin with and both tell you plainly how fast you spoke, how often you said um and uh, and whether you were reading your slides instead of speaking to your audience. Add **Orai** if your main problem is delivery drills on your phone, with short daily practice on pace, clarity, and energy. Use **Speeko** if you want guided lessons and exercises rather than just a score after each session. Consider **VirtualSpeech** only if you want course-style learning with simulated audiences, because it is a paid subscription, not a free starter tool. And do not buy any tool before you have also practised in front of real humans, because no app can teach you how to handle a live question, a silent room, or an audience member who disagrees with you.

This article is written for India on purpose: students, freshers, teachers, managers, founders, and anyone who speaks in English, Hindi, or Hinglish, on a normal phone and laptop, with no assumption that you own a paid plan, a virtual-reality headset, or a US account. All of the tools below work in India without a VPN, through a browser or a normal app store download. Prices and free limits change often, so re-check the official pricing and help page of any tool before you pay or before you promise yourself or anyone else a deadline based on a free plan. Where sources disagreed during verification, this article says so instead of picking the friendlier number.

If you read nothing else, remember three rules. First, a tool that scores your delivery does not judge whether your content is true, well structured, or right for your audience, so pair a delivery tool with a script check by a knowledgeable human. Third, the cheapest confidence builder in this whole article is not an app at all: it is speaking once a week in front of people who will clap, correct you kindly, and ask you a question you did not prepare for.

## Yoodli: the best free starting point for judged practice

Yoodli is an AI roleplay and speaking practice platform. You choose a scenario, such as a presentation, a pitch, a team update, or a keynote, and you practise speaking it. You can also set the kind of audience you are speaking to, and after your session you get a report on pacing, filler words, clarity, conciseness, and overall presence, along with concrete suggestions for what to improve. You can also practise follow-up questions, which matters enormously in India, because the hardest part of most talks is not the prepared speech but the question-and-answer session afterwards, when a manager, professor, or client asks something you did not expect.

The free Starter plan is genuinely free to begin with, and that is why Yoodli leads this article. But its limit is strict and easy to misunderstand, so read it carefully. Yoodli's own pricing page says that any practice session over 30 seconds counts as a roleplay, that the Starter plan includes 5 lifetime sessions, that Pro includes up to 10 roleplays per week, and that Advanced includes unlimited roleplays. Older guides and other accounts have quoted different free numbers, such as around three sessions a month, and that disagreement is exactly why this article names the pricing page as the source to re-check. Treat the free plan as five serious rehearsals, not as unlimited daily practice. Pro costs 8 dollars a month billed annually, and Advanced costs 20 dollars a month billed annually, with Advanced adding unlimited roleplays, data exclusion from AI training, and a reimbursement letter for professional development.

Use Yoodli free in a specific way. First, write your talk properly before you open the app, because practising a weak script five times only makes you fluent at a weak talk. Second, use your first free session on the real talk you must deliver, not on a test topic, so the feedback is about the words you will actually say. Third, after each session, pick exactly one fix for the next session, such as slowing down in the opening minute, or cutting the word actually, because trying to fix pace, fillers, structure, and eye contact at once usually fixes none of them. Fourth, practise the question-and-answer round at least once, because a confident talk followed by a panicked answer still leaves the audience unsure about you.

Yoodli is especially useful for Indian speakers who translate in their heads from Hindi or another Indian language into English while speaking. Filler words like um, uh, and actually often appear at the exact moment of translation, when your brain is searching for the English word. Yoodli shows you that pattern in a report, which turns a vague feeling of I am not fluent into a specific, fixable observation, such as you use fillers most in the first thirty seconds, or when you move from one slide to the next. That specificity is the whole point of an AI coach. It does not make you fluent. It shows you where you lose fluency, so your practice has a target.

Yoodli has limits you should know before you rely on it. It analyses delivery patterns, not the truth or strength of your argument. It will not tell you that your sales claim is exaggerated, that your technical explanation is wrong, or that your example will offend part of your audience. It also cannot judge body language the way a room full of people can, and a webcam score for eye contact is not the same as holding the attention of a tired audience at 4 pm. Use Yoodli to rehearse in private, then test the same talk on two or three real people before the real event.

## Orai: short daily drills for pace, clarity, and energy

Orai is a mobile app built for short, focused delivery practice. Instead of long courses, it gives you practice scenarios and live feedback on filler words, pace, clarity, and energy, with coaching tips after every session and progress trends over time. It is available on iOS and Android, which makes it the easiest tool on this list to use on a bus, during a lunch break, or in the ten minutes before a meeting, when you do not have a laptop open but you do have your phone.

Orai's pricing is clear, but its trial has a catch that matters in India. Orai's own pricing page offers a 7-day free trial of Pro, and says plainly that a credit card is required and that you will not be charged if you cancel before the trial ends. After the trial, Pro costs 12 dollars a month billed monthly, or 49.99 dollars a year on the annual plan, and there is also a 149-dollar one-time lifetime payment. That means Orai is not a free tool in the same sense as Yoodli's Starter plan. It is a paid tool with a short trial. If you do not have an international card, or if you do not want to hand card details to a trial, do not plan your practice around Orai, because the trial will stop, and the habit will stop with it.

Use Orai well if you do try it. Pick one drill type for a week, such as pace, and do it daily for five minutes, rather than jumping between pace, energy, clarity, and scenarios every day. Record the same opening paragraph for three days in a row, because the comparison across days teaches you more than three different speeches on three different days. And set a reminder to decide before day seven whether you will pay or cancel, because a trial that quietly becomes a subscription is not a practice plan, it is a billing accident. If you cancel, keep the habit with Yoodli's remaining sessions, Speaker Coach, or a daily voice-note recording on your own phone, which costs nothing and still lets you hear yourself.

Orai is best for speakers whose content is already strong but whose delivery is rushed, flat, or full of fillers. It is less useful if you do not yet know what you want to say, because drills polish delivery, they do not write your talk. If you are preparing a class presentation, a demo, or a pitch, write the script first, then use Orai to deliver it at a steady pace. If you notice your energy score is low, the fix is usually not to shout, but to vary your pitch, pause after important lines, and speak slightly slower than feels natural, because most nervous speakers speed up without realising it.

## Speeko: guided lessons when you want a coach, not just a score

Speeko is an AI speech coach with courses, daily exercises, warm-ups, and AI roleplay conversations, available on iOS, macOS, Android, and the web. Where Yoodli gives you a practice room and a report, and Orai gives you drills, Speeko gives you a curriculum: lessons to learn, exercises to do, and feedback on both delivery and content. That structure suits people who practise better when someone tells them what to do today, rather than leaving them alone with a record button and a score.

Speeko's free tier gives basic insights, and its help material describes free basic feedback, AI roleplay, selected course content, and a daily warm-up across its platforms. Personalised feedback, real-time guidance, and premium exercises require a paid plan. Speeko's subscription page lists Pro at 29.99 dollars a month, or 8.33 dollars a month billed annually, with a 7-day free trial, and the free course access covers the first module of each course. In plain terms, Speeko is a paid coach with a free taste, not a free daily tool. The annual price is far better value than the monthly price, but only if you actually practise for a year, so do not buy a year of coaching for a talk that is next week.

Speeko works best for a speaker who wants a habit. Use the daily warm-up every morning for a week before you judge it, because one warm-up tells you nothing, while seven warm-ups show you whether your pace and filler words are improving or just varying randomly. Use the first free module of a course to learn one technique properly, such as structuring an opening, rather than skimming five courses and finishing none. And if you use Speeko's roleplay conversations, treat them as rehearsal for handling interruptions and follow-up questions, which is where most real talks wobble, not as a replacement for speaking to humans.

For Indian users, Speeko's guided style has a particular advantage and a particular limit. The advantage is that it teaches speaking as a skill with steps, which helps students and early-career professionals who were never taught presentation skills in school or college, where speaking well is often expected but rarely taught. The limit is that an app's idea of good delivery is general, not Indian. It will not know that your audience expects a formal opening in a school function, a quick business update in a standup, or a story-led pitch to a client in a different city. Learn the technique from the app, then adapt the tone, examples, and formality to your actual audience, because a technique that sounds confident in a lesson can sound stiff in a real room.

## PowerPoint Speaker Coach: the free rehearsal tool you may already own

PowerPoint Speaker Coach, previously called Presenter Coach, is the most underused tool on this list, because many people already have it and have never opened it. If you use PowerPoint for Microsoft 365, you can rehearse a real slide show with the Coach, on Windows, Mac, web, Android tablets, and iPad. You open your presentation, choose Rehearse with Coach in the Slide Show tab, and speak your talk while the Coach gives on-screen guidance about pacing, pitch, filler words, informal speech, euphemisms, culturally sensitive terms, and whether you are simply reading the text on the slide. When you finish, you get a Rehearsal Report with statistics and suggestions, and you can rehearse again with those suggestions in mind.

Speaker Coach is the best tool on this list for one specific job: rehearsing the exact deck you will present. Yoodli and Orai rehearse you, but Speaker Coach rehearses you with your slides, which is where real talks fail, because speakers either read every bullet point, or rush past a slide they do not understand, or spend four minutes on the introduction and thirty seconds on the conclusion. Speaker Coach catches the reading-the-slide problem directly, and its report tells you how long you spent overall, which helps you fit a ten-minute talk into ten minutes instead of discovering during the real event that you are only halfway through at minute nine.

The honest limit is access. Speaker Coach is included with PowerPoint for Microsoft 365, so it is free if you already have that subscription through college, work, or a personal plan, but it is not a standalone free app you can download without PowerPoint. If you do not have Microsoft 365, do not buy it just for Speaker Coach, because Yoodli's free Starter plan covers the same delivery feedback without a subscription. If you do have it, use it for every important deck, because there is no reason to present slides you have never spoken aloud. Speak the whole deck at least twice, once to learn the timing, and once to fix the slides where you stumbled, because a stumble usually means the slide is confusing, not that you are a bad speaker.

Speaker Coach also teaches a lesson that applies to every tool here: your slides are not your speech. If the Coach keeps telling you that you are reading the slide, the fix is not to memorise the slide, it is to shorten the slide, so that it holds a heading and one idea, while the explanation lives in your voice. Audiences remember what you said and how you said it far longer than they remember a wall of bullet points, and a speaker who reads slides aloud is really just a slow, nervous teleprompter.

## VirtualSpeech: courses and simulated audiences, at a paid price

VirtualSpeech is a course-based platform for soft-skills training, including public speaking, with practice types covering roleplays, speeches, coaching, and exercises, and with simulated audience practice on the web and in virtual reality. It is built for individuals who want a structured library, and for teams, schools, and institutions that want to train many people with progress tracking. Its individual subscription is listed at 45 dollars a month or 399 dollars a year, while team and education pricing is tailored to size and rollout, so you should ask for a quote rather than guessing a team price.

VirtualSpeech makes sense in a narrow set of Indian situations. It makes sense for a college placement cell, a training institute, or a company learning team that wants many students or employees to practise presentations with feedback and tracking, because buying one subscription per person for a short course is cheaper than hiring a trainer for every batch. It makes sense for a speaker who is genuinely afraid of audiences and wants to practise in a simulated room before facing a real one, because a virtual audience that watches you, even without judgement, is a gentler first step than a real hall. It does not make sense as a first tool for a student with one presentation next week, because the price is high, the setup takes time, and the free tools above will improve your next talk faster.

If you do use VirtualSpeech, use it for the fear, not just the feedback. Practise the opening two minutes repeatedly in the simulated room, because the opening is where stage fright is worst, and once you survive the opening, the rest of the talk usually flows. Practise handling a pause, too, because beginners fear silence and fill it with um, sorry, and actually, while confident speakers pause on purpose, look at the audience, and then continue. A simulated audience is useful precisely because it lets you practise silence without embarrassment, and that lesson transfers directly to a real room.

## India use: how to practise when English is not your first language

Public speaking advice written for native English speakers often misses the Indian reality, which is that many confident speakers think in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Telugu, or another Indian language, and speak in English at work or college. That translation step is not a weakness. It is a skill, and it needs practice in the same way that a cricketer practises a new shot. The tools above help most when you practise in the language you will actually speak on the day, not in the language you are most comfortable thinking in.

Start with this weekly routine, which costs nothing. On day one, write your talk in simple sentences, with short words, because simple sentences are easier to deliver under pressure than long, clever ones. On day two, record yourself speaking the talk on your phone, and listen back once, noting only your pace and your three most common filler words. On day three, use one free session on Yoodli, or one rehearsal with Speaker Coach, on the real talk, and pick one fix. On day four, speak the talk to one friend, colleague, or family member, and ask them one question only: what was my main point? If they cannot answer, your structure needs work, not your voice. On day five, speak the talk standing up, at full volume, because sitting and mumbling at a laptop is not rehearsal, it is reading. On day six, rest, because a tired speaker sounds tired, and no app fixes exhaustion.

For Hindi and Hinglish talks, be careful with a specific trap. Some AI tools are trained mostly on English delivery, so their feedback on pace, fillers, and clarity may be less reliable for Hindi, Hinglish, or heavily accented English. That does not make the tools useless, but it means you should trust their English feedback more than their Hindi feedback, and you should always test a Hindi talk on a Hindi-speaking listener before you trust an app score. If you are preparing for an English interview or presentation, practise in English even if your first language is Hindi, because a talk that sounds strong in Hindi can sound hesitant in English if you have never spoken it aloud in English. For help with that step, see the related guide on [AI tools for learning English in India](/blog/ai-tools-for-learning-english-india/).

For students and job seekers, connect this practice to the next step in your career. A class presentation, a demo, and an interview answer are the same skill in different clothes, so the delivery practice you do here also helps in interviews, and the interview practice guide on [AI tools for interview prep in India](/blog/ai-tools-for-interview-prep-india/) covers the content side that delivery tools do not judge. If your talk uses slides, the guide on [free AI presentation makers in India](/blog/free-ai-presentation-makers-india/) can help you build a cleaner deck, but remember that a beautiful deck never saves a talk you have not rehearsed aloud.

Do not ignore the non-AI option in India. Toastmasters clubs meet in cities across India and online, and the usual way to try one is to attend a meeting as a guest, which lets you watch real speakers, hear real feedback, and, when you are ready, give a short prepared speech to people who want you to improve. Toastmasters is not an AI tool, it does not give you a pace score, and it will not fit in your pocket, but it gives you the one thing no app can: a real audience that laughs, frowns, asks questions, and claps when you finish. The best plan for most Indian speakers is both, not either: apps for private repetition during the week, and a real room for the courage that only a real room teaches.

Practical India details matter too. Practise with the internet connection you will actually have, because a tool that needs a fast connection will fail you in a college hostel, a small-town office, or a conference hall with weak Wi-Fi. Download or prepare offline where possible, keep your slides on your own device as well as in the cloud, and always carry a backup, such as a PDF of your slides on your phone, because technology fails most often at the exact moment you need it most. Practise with the microphone and room you will use, too, because a phone microphone in a quiet room sounds nothing like a hall microphone with echo, and a talk that sounds clear at home can sound rushed and muffled on stage.

## What to avoid

Avoid planning your whole preparation around a tool that has shut down. Poised, once a well-known AI communication coach for meetings, announced that it was shutting down on 8 October 2026, and its app page now says it has retired and its content has been deleted. Older articles, videos, and lists still recommend Poised as if it were live, so if you find a guide that tells you to sign up for Poised, that guide is out of date, and you should not follow its signup steps, pay for a plan, or build a deadline around it. The same warning applies to any tool on a list that has not been updated in months: check that the tool is still live before you invest practice time in it.

Avoid paying for a yearly plan before you have finished a free trial or a free tier. A yearly plan only saves money if you practise for a year, and most people buy a year of motivation in January and stop practising in February. Pay monthly first, or use the free tier until you hit its limit, and only then decide whether the paid features actually changed your speaking. Avoid also handing your credit card to a trial you do not intend to monitor, because Orai's trial requires a card, and a forgotten trial becomes a subscription charge, not a speaking habit.

Avoid practising only in private. Private practice builds fluency, but it also builds a false sense of readiness, because an app never interrupts you, never looks bored, and never asks a hostile question. If your talk matters, you must speak it to at least one real person before the event, even if that person is a friend on a video call. Avoid practising only the easy parts, too. Most speakers rehearse the opening ten times and the middle once, because the middle is where the hard explanation lives, so rehearse the hardest two minutes more than the easiest two minutes, and rehearse the ending until you can finish cleanly without trailing off.

Avoid trusting an app score more than your audience. If an app says your pace is perfect but your friend says you sounded rushed and hard to follow, your friend is right, because your friend is the audience. If an app flags a filler word that your audience did not notice, fix it only if it appears often, not because a score told you to chase perfection. And avoid copying a speaking style that is not yours. A slow, dramatic style that works for a keynote can sound strange in a team standup, and a fast, energetic style that works for a sales pitch can sound anxious in a classroom. Use the tools to remove the habits that distract from your message, not to replace your voice with someone else's.

Avoid also the trap of endless tool comparison. Reading twenty lists of speaking tools feels like preparation, but it is not preparation, and it is the most common way that students and professionals waste the week before a talk. Pick one free tool today, rehearse your real talk in it today, and speak it to one person tomorrow. That single cycle teaches you more than a week of reading about tools, because speaking is a physical skill, like batting or driving, and no amount of reading replaces doing it.

A short honest-limits note on money: every price in this article was checked against the tool's own pricing or help page during verification, but prices, free limits, trial rules, and even whole tools change without warning, as Poised's shutdown shows. Re-check the official pricing and help page before you pay, before you enter card details, and before you promise a teacher, manager, or client that a free plan will cover your preparation. If a price you see on a checkout page differs from a price in this article, trust the checkout page and the official pricing page, not this article, and tell yourself that the difference is the reason this warning exists.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which AI tool is best for public speaking practice for beginners in India?**

Start with Yoodli's free Starter plan for judged practice, and PowerPoint Speaker Coach if you already have Microsoft 365, because both are free to begin with, both work in India without a VPN, and both give you a clear report on pace and filler words. Add a real audience, such as a Toastmasters guest visit or a practice talk to friends, as soon as you can, because beginners improve fastest when private app feedback and real human feedback are combined.

**Can AI tools really reduce stage fear?**

They can reduce one part of stage fear, which is the fear of the unknown, because private repetition makes the words familiar, and a report tells you what to fix instead of leaving you guessing. They cannot fully remove stage fear, because stage fear also comes from being watched and judged by real people, and only real audiences teach you to stay calm while being watched. Use apps to make the talk familiar, and use real practice to make being watched familiar, and the fear usually drops to a level you can speak through.

**Do these tools work for Hindi or Hinglish speeches?**

They work best for English speeches, because most of their delivery models and feedback are built around English. For Hindi or Hinglish talks, use the tools for general habits, such as pace, pauses, and filler words, but test the talk on a Hindi-speaking listener before you trust an app score, because the app may misjudge Hindi pacing, pronunciation, or natural Hinglish phrasing. Practise in the exact language you will speak on the day, not in a translation you hope to produce live.

**Is Yoodli really free?**

Yes, to begin with, but with a strict limit. Yoodli's pricing page says the free Starter plan includes 5 lifetime roleplay sessions, where any session over 30 seconds counts as a roleplay. That is enough for a few serious rehearsals of a real talk, but it is not unlimited daily practice. Pro costs 8 dollars a month billed annually, and Advanced costs 20 dollars a month billed annually. If you see an older guide quoting a different free number, trust the current pricing page, because free limits change and older guides go stale.

**Is Orai free?**

Orai is not free in the same way as Yoodli. It offers a 7-day free trial of Pro, but its pricing page says a credit card is required, and you must cancel before the trial ends to avoid being charged. After the trial, Pro costs 12 dollars a month, 49.99 dollars a year, or 149 dollars as a one-time lifetime payment. If you do not want to enter card details for a trial, start with Yoodli or Speaker Coach instead, and use Orai only if you are ready to decide within seven days.

**Do I need a virtual-reality headset for VirtualSpeech?**

No, not necessarily. VirtualSpeech offers simulated audience practice on the web as well as in virtual reality, so you can practise without a headset, though the most immersive audience simulation uses virtual reality. Given its individual price of 45 dollars a month or 399 dollars a year, it is best treated as a course investment or a team purchase, not as a casual first tool, and you should try the free tools first and only pay if you specifically want course-style simulated audience practice.

**Can PowerPoint Speaker Coach replace a human coach?**

No, but it replaces the most common excuse for not rehearsing, which is that nobody is available to listen. Speaker Coach listens privately, reports on pace, fillers, and slide-reading, and lets you rehearse again immediately, which is exactly what you need for a slide deck. A human coach still does what Speaker Coach cannot: judge whether your argument is convincing, whether your story fits your audience, and whether your ending lands. Use Speaker Coach for rehearsal, and a human for judgement.

**How long should I practise a talk before delivering it?**

Practise the full talk aloud at least three times: once to learn the timing and find the confusing slides, once to fix those slides and the hardest two minutes, and once standing up, at full volume, as close as possible to real conditions. If the talk is important, add one practice in front of a real person, because that practice reveals problems that private rehearsal hides, such as a joke that does not land, an example that confuses people, or an ending that trails off. Three private rehearsals plus one human rehearsal is a realistic minimum, not an ideal maximum.

**What is the biggest mistake beginners make with speaking apps?**

The biggest mistake is practising random topics instead of the real talk, and chasing a perfect score instead of a clear message. An app score is a training aid, not the goal. The goal is that your audience understands your main point, remembers one story or example, and trusts you enough to act on what you said. If you finish a practice session with a high score but no clear main point, you have polished the delivery of a talk that will still fail, so fix the message first, then polish the delivery.

**Should I pay for a speaking tool before my first big talk?**

Usually no. Use the free tier or trial for your first talk, speak to at least one real person, and only pay if you hit a free limit that actually blocked you, such as running out of Yoodli's five sessions before your talk series ended, or needing daily drills that only a paid plan provides. Paying before you have a habit usually buys a subscription, not a skill, while paying after you have a habit buys more of something you already use.

## Final verdict

Start free with Yoodli and PowerPoint Speaker Coach, add Orai drills for pace, and practise live with a Toastmasters club before any paid plan.

That verdict is deliberately boring, because good speaking preparation is boring: write a clear talk, rehearse it aloud, fix one thing at a time, and speak it to real people before the day that counts. The AI tools in this article make the private part of that routine faster and more honest, by showing you your pace, your fillers, and your slide-reading habits instead of letting you guess. They do not replace the public part, and anyone who promises that an app alone will make you confident is selling you a subscription, not a skill. Start today, with the free tools, on the real talk you must give next, and let the first rehearsal, not another list of tools, tell you what to fix next.
