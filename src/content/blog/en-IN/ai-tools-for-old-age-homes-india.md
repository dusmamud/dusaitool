## The direct answer

If you run an old age home in India, or you are choosing one for a parent, the best AI tools are the ones that solve four daily problems: keeping resident care records in order, making fair staff rosters, reminding everyone about medicines and appointments on time, and keeping families honestly informed. No single app does all four perfectly, and no app replaces trained, caring staff. The sensible starting point is free and familiar: WhatsApp for family updates and video calls, Google Gemini voice for residents who find typing difficult, and Google Calendar or Google Keep for medicine and appointment reminders. If the home is large enough that paper registers and spreadsheets are causing mistakes, a purpose-built Indian elder-care system such as Silvora Care or ElderConnect is worth a trial, but only after you have seen a written price, because both use custom pricing rather than a published flat rate.

Safety deserves its own place in this list. A fall is one of the most serious risks for an older resident, and a watch with fall detection, such as an Apple Watch, can call for help when the wearer cannot reach a phone. It cannot detect every fall, so it is a back-up, not a replacement for staff checks and call bells. For families worried about a parent's rights, pension, or legal problem, the government's toll-free Elder Line, 14567, gives information, guidance and emotional support, and it can coordinate help on the ground. That helpline is free to call, and it is a better first step than trusting a chatbot with a legal or pension question.

This article explains each tool in plain language, what it costs in India, where it works well, and where it falls short. Prices and free limits change often, so before paying for anything or promising a family a deadline based on a tool, re-check the official pricing and help page of that tool. Where sources disagree, this article says so. Nothing here is medical, legal or financial advice: this is general information only, and health, money and legal decisions must be checked with a doctor, a pharmacist, a qualified professional, or an official source.

## 1. Silvora Care: a whole-home system built for Indian elder care

Silvora Care describes itself as a complete elder-care home management platform for care homes, retirement communities and senior-living operators in India. Instead of separate registers for admissions, staff and medicines, it puts resident intake, staff scheduling, care plans and family communication into one system. Its public pages report more than 500 residents managed and more than 50 care homes on board, which suggests it is a real, operating product rather than a demo, though a home should still ask for references from homes of a similar size before signing anything.

The features that matter most for daily work are the practical ones. Resident management keeps medical history, care plans and emergency contacts in one place, so a new nurse on night duty does not have to hunt through paper files. The Smart Scheduler, available on its SmartCare plan, generates staff rosters based on resident needs, shift preferences and leave patterns, and the company claims this cuts scheduling conflicts sharply. Health pattern analysis watches resident vitals and activity patterns and flags unusual changes early, and mood assessment tracks emotional wellbeing over time rather than only physical health. Family communication runs through WhatsApp, which matters in India because relatives actually read WhatsApp, and the company claims family updates become several times faster this way.

Pricing is the honest catch. Silvora lists three plans, StandardCare, AdvancedCare and SmartCare, but all three are priced as custom pricing based on capacity. There is no flat monthly figure a home can budget from the website alone. That is normal for business software, but it means the home must get a written quote that states the number of residents and staff included, what happens when the home grows, and whether WhatsApp messages or support cost extra. Ask for a trial with real resident data, and ask what happens to your records if you cancel. A system that holds care records must let you export them.

How to start: list the three biggest paperwork problems in your home, for example missed medicines, roster arguments, or families complaining they hear nothing. Then ask Silvora for a demo that solves those three problems specifically, not a general sales tour. If the demo cannot show a roster being built and a family update being sent, it has not proved its value.

Watch out for: AI health alerts are warnings, not diagnoses. A flagged pattern still needs a nurse or doctor to check the resident in person. Never let an alert, or the absence of an alert, replace a physical round.

## 2. ElderConnect: one care timeline for nurses, managers and families

ElderConnect, also known as CareConnect, is another Indian platform for elder-care and facility management, and it pitches itself around an assistive clinical AI co-pilot. Its core idea is a single care timeline that connects bedside nurses, facility administrators and families, so everyone sees the same record instead of three different versions of it.

The features worth noting are clinical and operational. Resident 360 profiles hold medical history, vitals, allergies, dietary constraints, fall-risk levels and emergency family contacts. A visual bed and occupancy matrix shows rooms by wing and care tier, which helps a manager admit a new resident without double-booking a bed or placing someone in the wrong ward. Voice-assisted SBAR handovers are designed for shift changes: SBAR is the standard Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation format nurses use to hand over a patient, and voice assistance can make that handover faster and more complete. Multi-site governance matters if one organisation runs several homes, because a head office can compare homes without waiting for monthly reports.

As with Silvora, pricing is not published as a flat rate on its public page, so a home must ask for a written quote and a trial. The two systems overlap heavily, so the right choice is usually the one whose trial your nurses actually enjoy using. A beautiful dashboard that nurses avoid is worse than a plain register they fill in honestly.

How to start: run a one-week trial on a single wing, not the whole home. Ask the nurses one question at the end of the week: did this save time, or did it add typing? If the answer is that it added typing, the system is not ready for your home, whatever the brochure claims.

Watch out for: voice handovers and AI summaries must be checked by the nurse who gives them. An AI summary that drops an allergy or a changed dose is dangerous, so treat every AI-written handover as a draft until a human has read and signed it.

## 3. WhatsApp: the family update tool that already works

WhatsApp is the most important tool in this article for family trust, not because it is the most advanced, but because it is the one every family already has. A daughter in Delhi, a son abroad, and a sister in the same city can all receive the same update without learning a new app, remembering a new password, or paying a subscription. WhatsApp itself does not charge for messages or calls, though mobile data charges may apply, so home Wi-Fi is the best option for long video calls.

For a care home, WhatsApp works best as a routine, not as random messages. A short daily or weekly update template builds trust: how the resident slept, whether medicines were taken, one pleasant detail such as a walk in the garden or a song the resident enjoyed, and anything the family needs to decide. A fixed time for updates, for example every evening, stops families from worrying in silence and stops staff from being interrupted all day by individual calls. Group chats per family keep one resident's information private to that family, and a broadcast-style routine avoids the mistake of posting one resident's news in a general group.

Video calls need almost no learning. Open the chat, tap the video camera icon, and wait for the other person to answer. During a call, only three buttons matter at first: the microphone, the camera switch, and the red button to end the call. For residents who cannot hold a phone, a staff member can hold it or prop it on a stand, and a speaker or loudspeaker mode lets the whole room join a birthday call. Google Meet is a useful second option for larger family meetings, because it works from a link in a browser and its free tier allows group meetings of up to 100 participants and 60 minutes, with one-to-one mobile calls having no stated time limit.

How to start: agree one update template, one fixed time, and one staff member responsible for sending updates each day. Try it for two weeks with five families before rolling it out to the whole home.

Watch out for: never share one resident's health details in a group that includes other families, and never send bank details, passwords, or identity documents over WhatsApp. Health information on a personal phone also needs care: use the home's official phone or account, not a staff member's personal chat history, so records survive when staff change.

## 4. Google Gemini voice: letting residents ask without typing

Google Gemini is Google's AI assistant, available as an app on Android phones and built into many newer Android devices. Its most useful feature for residents is Gemini Live, a voice mode that lets a person have a spoken conversation instead of typing. The resident taps the Live icon, speaks a question, interrupts if needed, and hears the answer. Typing on a small screen is hard for many older people, especially with small text, stiff fingers, or weak eyesight, and speaking is natural.

Gemini supports Indian languages including Hindi, which matters greatly in an old age home where many residents are more comfortable in Hindi or another Indian language than in written English. A resident can ask about the weather, ask for a bhajan or a cricket score, ask for a newspaper article to be explained in simple words, or practise a few English sentences. For staff, Gemini can help draft a polite family update, simplify a notice into easy Hindi, or suggest questions to ask a doctor before a visit. The free version through the Gemini app is enough to try, and paid Google AI Pro plans add more features that most residents will not need on the first day.

How to start: sit beside the resident for the first conversation, raise the phone volume, and try three or four short questions. Keep the first session short. The goal is confidence, not a long lesson, and one pleasant success matters more than ten features explained at once.

Watch out for: Gemini can be wrong, especially about dates, times, medical details, or local information, so important facts must be double-checked with a trusted source or a family member. Never share bank passwords, one-time passwords, or full personal documents with any chatbot, and never use it as a substitute for medical advice.

## 5. Amazon Alexa: a speaker for the room, in Hindi and Hinglish

Many residents do not want another screen, and that is where a smart speaker helps. Amazon Alexa, on an Echo speaker or smart display, lets a resident speak to the room: ask for music, hear the news, set a reminder, or ask a question, without picking up a phone. Alexa has supported Hindi in India since 2019, and its newer Alexa+ version, launched in India in September 2026 as an Early Access programme, supports English, Hindi and Hinglish, including switching between languages inside one sentence, which matches how many Indians actually speak.

For an old age home, the practical uses are reminders and company. A resident can say, in Hindi or Hinglish, that they want a reminder for medicine at nine, or ask for devotional music in the morning. In a common room, one Echo Show can play bhajans, show photos, or run a video call with family. Alexa+ during Early Access is free for eligible customers, and reporting says that after Early Access it will remain free for full Prime members while other users will need a Prime plan or a standalone subscription reported at 2,000 rupees per month. That later price is reported by several news sources, but it is exactly the kind of figure that must be re-checked on Amazon's official Alexa page before a home budgets for it or promises it to a family, because Early Access terms and eligibility can change.

How to start: put one speaker in a common room first, not in every bedroom. Let residents request music and reminders for a week, and watch which residents enjoy it and which find it confusing. Buy more only for the residents who actually use it.

Watch out for: a speaker in a shared room hears everyone, so do not discuss private health or financial matters near it, and teach staff how to mute it. A voice assistant is also not an emergency device: if a resident falls and cannot speak clearly, a call bell, a staff round, or a fall-detection watch is the safety net, not a speaker.

## 6. Medisafe and free reminders: making sure medicines are taken on time

Missed medicines are one of the most common and most preventable problems in elder care. Two kinds of tools help, and a home should understand the difference. Free built-in reminders, such as Google Calendar, Google Keep and Google Assistant, let staff set a reminder by speaking one sentence, for example, reminding a resident to take medicine every day at nine in the morning. They repeat automatically, they cost nothing beyond a Google account, and they need no new subscription. Their limit is honest: a notification cannot confirm that a medicine was actually taken, and it will not alert a family member if the reminder is ignored.

Medisafe is a dedicated medicine app that goes further. It tracks doses, refills and appointments, and its Medfriend feature lets a family member be notified if a dose is missed. That family alert is genuinely valuable for a home, because it adds a second pair of eyes. But families in India should know an important limit honestly: outside the United States, Medisafe offers a 14-day free trial and then requires a subscription to continue using it, so it is not a permanently free option here. Its drug-to-drug interaction checker is also described as available only in the United States and when the app is set to English, so Indian families should not rely on it for interaction checks and must ask a pharmacist instead.

How to start: use free Google reminders for every resident from day one, because they cost nothing and work immediately. Add Medisafe only for residents whose families want missed-dose alerts and are willing to pay after the trial, and set it up together with the family so everyone understands what the alert means and who acts on it.

Watch out for: an app reminder is not a medicine record. Staff must still sign a real medicine register when a dose is given, and any change in dose, timing, or medicine must come from a doctor's prescription, not from an app suggestion.

## 7. Apple Watch fall detection: a safety net, not a guarantee

A fall can leave a resident unable to reach a phone or a call bell, and that is the gap a fall-detection watch tries to close. On an Apple Watch SE or later, an Apple Watch Series 4 or later, or an Apple Watch Ultra, fall detection uses motion sensors to recognise the pattern of a hard fall. When it detects one, it taps the wearer's wrist, sounds an alarm, and shows an alert. The wearer can dismiss it or call emergency services. If the watch detects that the wearer has been immobile for about a minute, it begins a 30-second countdown, then automatically contacts emergency services and, after the call, sends a message to emergency contacts with the location. For users aged 55 or older, fall detection is turned on automatically during setup, and wrist detection must be turned on for the automatic call to work.

For an old age home, this is a useful extra layer for residents who are mobile enough to walk around the home but at risk of falling, especially at night or in a bathroom. It is not, however, a replacement for staff rounds, call bells, non-slip floors, grab rails, and good lighting, because Apple itself states clearly that the watch cannot detect all falls, and high-impact activity can sometimes be mistaken for a fall. A GPS-only watch also depends on a nearby iPhone for calling in many situations, while a cellular model can call on its own where cellular service is available, so a home must check which model a resident actually has before relying on it.

How to start: for any resident who wears one, check three things with a family member: fall detection is on, wrist detection is on, and emergency contacts in Medical ID are correct. Then test the alert flow in words, without faking a fall, so the resident knows what the watch will do and how to cancel a false alert.

Watch out for: a watch needs charging, and a watch left on a bedside table protects nobody. Put charging on the daily routine, just like medicines, and never promise a family that a watch guarantees help after a fall, because it does not.

## 8. Elder Line 14567 and honest record-keeping: free help and paper that survives

Not every problem in an old age home needs an app. Elder Line, the national helpline for senior citizens, can be reached toll-free on 14567. It is run under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment as part of the Atal Vayo Abhyuday Yojana, and government releases say it is operational in 32 states and union territories, functions from 8 am to 8 pm on all days except national holidays, and provides information, guidance, emotional support, intervention, rescue and grievance help. The same ministry reports that it supports 734 senior citizen homes through grants under its Integrated Programme for Senior Citizens. The helpline coordinates help and referrals; it does not give direct financial assistance, and that distinction should be explained honestly to families so nobody waits for money that will not come.

Alongside the helpline, the least glamorous tool is often the most valuable: a simple shared record. A spreadsheet or a shared document that lists each resident's medicines, allergies, doctor, family contacts, and daily notes, kept up to date by staff, survives app trials, staff changes, and internet outages better than any single paid system. A home that cannot keep one shared sheet accurate is not ready for a complex AI system, and a home that can keep it accurate will get far more from any paid system it later buys.

How to start: print the Elder Line number, 14567, on the home's notice board and in the welcome note given to every new resident and family. Then appoint one staff member to own the shared record, and check it once a week for missing entries.

Watch out for: never hand a resident's full medical record, identity documents, or bank details to a helpline, chatbot, or app that does not need them. Share only what the help requested actually requires.

## Using AI in an Indian old age home: what actually works

The pattern that works in Indian homes is small and human. Start with tools families already trust, add one paid system only when paper is genuinely failing, and keep a human in charge of every AI output. Language comes first: a tool that works only in written English will fail in a home where residents speak Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, or another Indian language, so test every tool in the resident's own language before buying it. Internet comes second: a home with weak Wi-Fi needs tools that work on mobile data or offline, and staff should know what to do when the internet stops, because care does not stop. Cost comes third: free tools with honest limits beat expensive tools nobody uses, and a custom-priced system must be compared on total yearly cost, including support and messages, not on the first quote.

Staff training matters more than the choice of app. One short training session, one printed cheat sheet in the staff's language, and one named person who can answer questions will do more than ten features. Residents need the same respect: explain what a tool does, ask permission before putting a speaker or a watch on a resident, and let a resident refuse a tool without losing care. Dignity is not a feature to add later; it is the reason families trust a home at all.

For families choosing a home, ask four questions on your visit: how do you send me updates, and how often; who checks that medicines were actually taken, not just reminded; what happens if my parent falls at night; and can I see my parent's care record? The answers to those four questions tell you more about a home than any brochure about artificial intelligence. ## What to avoid

Avoid any tool that promises to replace doctors, nurses, or family attention. An AI chatbot cannot examine a resident, and an AI alert cannot lift someone who has fallen. Avoid tools that demand full identity documents, bank details, or passwords before showing any value, and avoid any app that will not let you export your own care records. Avoid buying a system for the whole home on the strength of one sales demo: trial it on one wing, with your own nurses, for at least a week. Avoid a speaker or camera in a bedroom without the resident's clear permission, because privacy in a care home is already fragile. Avoid trusting AI answers about medicines, pensions, or the law without checking a doctor, a pharmacist, or an official source, and avoid promising a family a result or a deadline based on an AI tool, because prices, limits and features change, and the official pricing and help page must be re-checked before paying or promising anything. Finally, avoid the trap of collecting data nobody reads: a dashboard that nobody checks each morning is decoration, not care.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best AI tool for an old age home in India?**
There is no single best tool, because a home needs records, rosters, reminders and family updates, and different tools do each job best. Start with WhatsApp for family updates, Google Gemini voice for residents, and free Google reminders for medicines. Add a purpose-built system such as Silvora Care or ElderConnect only when paper registers are causing real mistakes, and only after a trial and a written price.

**Can AI replace nurses or carers in an old age home?**
No. AI can organise records, build rosters, send reminders and flag unusual patterns, but it cannot examine a resident, give personal care, lift someone who has fallen, or notice the small changes in mood and appetite that an experienced carer sees. Every AI output in a care home must be checked by a trained human before it changes what happens to a resident.

**How much does elder-care software cost in India?**
It depends on the home's size, because the Indian systems described here use custom pricing based on capacity rather than a published flat rate. Free tools such as WhatsApp, Google reminders and the Elder Line helpline cost nothing beyond data charges, while a full care-home system needs a written quote covering residents, staff, messages and support. Always re-check the official pricing page before paying, because prices and limits change.

**Which free tools work best for a small old age home?**
WhatsApp for family updates and video calls, Google Calendar or Google Keep for medicine and appointment reminders, Google Gemini voice for residents who prefer speaking, and the toll-free Elder Line on 14567 for information and guidance. Together these cover communication, reminders, companionship and official help without any subscription, though mobile data charges may still apply.

**Is WhatsApp safe for sharing a resident's health updates?**
WhatsApp messages are end-to-end encrypted, but safety also depends on how the home uses it. Share updates only with that resident's own family, never in a mixed group, use the home's official phone rather than a staff member's personal account, and never send bank details, passwords, or identity documents over WhatsApp. Health details should stay within the family group that needs them.

**Can an Apple Watch really call for help after a fall?**
Yes, in the right conditions. On a compatible Apple Watch with fall detection and wrist detection turned on, a hard fall followed by about a minute of immobility starts a 30-second countdown and then an automatic call to emergency services, with a message to emergency contacts afterwards. But Apple states the watch cannot detect all falls, it needs a cellular, satellite or Wi-Fi calling connection, and it must be worn and charged. It is a safety net, not a guarantee, and it never replaces staff checks.

**What is Elder Line 14567, and who can call it?**
Elder Line is India's national toll-free helpline for senior citizens, run under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment. Any senior citizen, or a family member or well-wisher calling on their behalf, can call 14567 for information, guidance, emotional support, and coordination of help including intervention and rescue where needed. It functions from 8 am to 8 pm except national holidays, and it coordinates referrals rather than giving direct financial assistance.

**Should an old age home use a voice speaker in residents' rooms?**
Only with the resident's clear permission, and a common room is a better first step than a bedroom. A speaker is useful for music, news and reminders in Hindi or Hinglish, but it hears the room it sits in, so private health or financial conversations should not happen near it. Teach residents and staff how to mute it, and remember it is not an emergency device: a fall still needs a call bell, a staff round, or a fall-detection watch.

**How do families check that medicines were actually taken, not just reminded?**
A reminder alone proves nothing, so a home should keep a signed medicine register where staff record each dose given, and families should ask to see it. Apps with family alerts, such as Medisafe's Medfriend feature, can add a second check by notifying a relative about a missed dose, but they still depend on someone recording the dose honestly. If a home cannot show you a medicine record, that is a warning sign whatever apps it claims to use.

**What should a family ask before choosing an old age home that uses AI tools?**
Ask how updates are sent and how often, who checks that medicines were actually taken, what happens if a resident falls at night, whether you can see the care record, and whether the home can export your parent's records if you leave. Also ask what happens when the internet fails. A home with clear, honest answers to those questions is safer than a home with an impressive dashboard and vague answers.

## Final verdict

Start with WhatsApp, Gemini voice and free reminders; add a care-home system like Silvora only after a trial and a written price.

That verdict is deliberately boring, and that is the point. An old age home does not need the cleverest AI; it needs records that are accurate, rosters that are fair, medicines given on time, and families who hear the truth regularly. Free tools do most of that today, and a paid Indian care system earns its place only when it proves, on one wing, with your own nurses, that it makes those four jobs easier. Check prices and limits on the official pages before paying, keep a human in charge of every AI output, and judge any tool by one question: did today go better for the residents? If the answer is yes, keep it. If not, no brochure should persuade you otherwise.
