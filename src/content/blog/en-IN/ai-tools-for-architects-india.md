## The direct answer

If you are an architect, interior designer, or architecture student in India, the most useful AI tools right now are Maket and Planner 5D for early floor plans, Snaptrude and Autodesk Forma for concept and site studies, D5 Render and Veras for visualisation and rendering, and Midjourney for early concept imagery. No single tool does everything well, and none of them produces a drawing you can submit for building approval or hand to a contractor without a qualified professional checking it first.

That last point matters more in India than in most marketing copy. Indian building permission depends on the local authority's bye-laws, which vary by state and city, and sanction drawings in India are signed by the owner and by a Council of Architecture-registered architect (or, for smaller plots, an engineer under the applicable rules). An AI-generated plan may look convincing while getting a setback, staircase width, ventilation rule, or parking requirement wrong. Treat every AI output in this article as a starting point for design discussion, not as a finished or compliant drawing.

The good news is that the free starting points are genuinely usable for learning and for early client conversations. Maket offers a free plan with one-time credits, Planner 5D has a free plan with unlimited projects, Snaptrude starts free with a limited credit pack, and D5 Render has a free Community version for non-commercial use. The honest limits are just as important: free plans usually block exports, cap credits, add watermarks, or forbid commercial client work. Read the licence before you send a free render to a paying client, and always re-check the official pricing page before paying, because prices and credit limits change often.

## How to choose: match the tool to the stage of work

Architects waste the most money when they buy a rendering tool to solve a planning problem, or a plan generator to solve a presentation problem. A simple stage-wise split avoids that.

At brief and feasibility stage, the question is whether the programme fits the site at all. That is where Forma, Finch, and Snaptrude help: you test massing, unit mix, daylight potential, and area metrics before anyone falls in love with a single plan. At concept plan stage, the question is room arrangement, adjacency, and circulation. That is where Maket and Planner 5D help, because they return something a client can react to within minutes. At visualisation stage, the question is light, material, and atmosphere. That is where D5 Render and Veras help, because they work with or around a real model rather than inventing a scene from text alone. Midjourney sits even earlier: it helps when you need a visual direction before any model exists, and it should never be mistaken for a design proposal.

A practical workflow for a small Indian practice could look like this. First, test two or three site options in Forma or Snaptrude and keep the area and daylight notes. Second, generate two or three residential layout options in Maket, then rebuild the chosen option properly in your normal CAD or BIM software. Third, render the key views in D5 Render or Veras, and label every AI-assisted image as a concept visual when you share it. Fourth, have the registered architect on the project re-draw, dimension, and check the submission set against the local bye-laws. The AI saves hours in the first three steps; it does not remove the fourth step.

## Maket: the fastest way to get a residential plan to react to

Maket is a residential floor plan generator that works through a guided conversation. You describe the plot or home in plain language — number of floors, approximate area, room count, style, and which rooms should sit near each other — and it returns plan options with doors, windows, and furniture included, plus a live 3D view and rendered images. For an Indian client meeting, that speed is the point: instead of debating an abstract brief for a week, the client can look at a plan, reject the kitchen position, and ask for a change in the next sentence.

The free plan is genuinely useful for testing, with 30 one-time credits and single-floor generation reported on its pricing summary. The catch is exports. PDF and DXF exports are reported to start on the Homeowner plan at around $20 per month, so the free plan is for exploration and client discussion, not for taking a drawing into your documentation workflow. Prices and credit rules change, so confirm the current Maket pricing page before you promise a client a deliverable or a deadline that depends on an export.

Maket is best for independent houses, small residential projects, and early options. It is not a substitute for structural design, services design, or local code checking, and its output still needs to be re-drawn and verified in your professional software. Use it when the client needs options fast; do not use it as the drawing set.

## Planner 5D: plan image in, editable 2D and 3D project out

Planner 5D is the most approachable tool on this list for homeowners, interior designers, and students, and it is also a handy client-communication tool for architects. You can start from a room photo, an uploaded plan image, or simple room shape and style choices, and refine the result in a 2D and 3D editor. Its plan-to-3D feature accepts common image formats including PNG, JPG, PDF, and DXF/DWG files, which makes it easy to test an old scanned plan or a rough sketch.

The free plan costs $0 and includes unlimited projects across web and mobile, plus access to about half of the furniture catalogue. That is generous for learning and for room-layout experiments. Premium, listed at $19.99 per month or $59.99 billed annually, adds the full catalogue of more than 10,000 furniture items, plan-to-3D conversion, standard renders, and budget tracking. Planner 5D itself warns that its output should not be treated as a substitute for professional review before a permit submission, and that warning deserves to be repeated to every client who sees a Planner 5D image.

Planner 5D is best for interiors, room layouts, furniture placement, and quick 3D views of a home. It is not a BIM tool, and it will not produce a compliant sanction drawing. Where it shines in India is the small-project conversation: a family renovating a flat can see furniture, colours, and walking space before buying anything, and a designer can test three living-room arrangements in an evening.

## Snaptrude: from a text brief towards a BIM model

Snaptrude is the most ambitious tool on this list for practising architects. It aims to go from a text brief or an uploaded project document all the way to a concept design with programme, stacking, diagrams, and a BIM model that can be exported to Revit. Its AI agents analyse the site, setbacks, height limits, and climate inputs, generate a structured programme of spaces, assign dimensions, stack spaces across storeys, and produce presentation-ready diagrams. For a firm doing housing, offices, or institutional concept work, that is a much more professional starting point than a single pretty plan image.

The free plan starts at $0 with 300 one-time credits, one free project, and access to the AI design agent plus programme and presentation modes. The important limits are that the free plan has no exports and no BIM mode, so you cannot take a free-plan model into Revit. The Pro plan adds weekly credits, unlimited projects, BIM mode, exports, drawing sheets to PDF, design options, and daylight and illuminance studies. If your test depends on the Revit export, test it on a paid plan or a trial rather than assuming the free plan will do it.

Snaptrude's own positioning is concept design through schematic design, after which work continues in Revit. That is an honest boundary, and it fits Indian practice well: use Snaptrude to explore options and win the client conversation, then do detailed design, structure coordination, services, and submission drawings in your established BIM and CAD workflow, checked against local rules.

## Autodesk Forma: site, massing, and early analysis before the plan

Autodesk Forma is site-scale software for early design. Instead of starting with a single room, you start with the site: massing options, area metrics, daylight potential, noise, wind, and embodied carbon analysis, with a connected workflow into Revit and Autodesk Construction Cloud. For a developer-led housing scheme, a campus building, or a tight urban plot in a city like Mumbai, Bengaluru, or Delhi, those early numbers often decide whether a scheme is worth pursuing at all.

Forma Site Design is offered as a 30-day free trial, and it is also included at no extra cost for AEC Collection subscribers. That makes the trial a good way to test one real site, but it also means Forma is not a free-forever tool for a small practice. After the trial, it is a subscription product, so check the current Autodesk pricing and whether your existing Revit or AEC subscription already includes it before buying a separate licence.

Forma is best for feasibility and early-stage design decisions, not for room-by-room house plans. Autodesk has also announced experimental generative layout features inside Forma, but experimental features deserve extra caution: outputs vary, and an experimental layout still needs the same professional checking as any other AI output. Use Forma to compare site options and to support a client or developer discussion with analysis, then carry the chosen massing into detailed design.

## D5 Render: fast, realistic rendering with a genuinely free version

D5 Render is a real-time rendering tool for architects and designers, and it is the strongest free starting point for visualisation on this list. The free Community version supports image, panorama, and video rendering with unlimited projects, while its AI features come with 50 trial credits. Pro unlocks unlimited AI features, the full asset library, and unlimited tours and presentations, along with higher video output limits.

The licence distinction is the thing most people miss. D5's own help material describes the Community licence as designed for non-commercial purposes such as learning, personal projects, and evaluation, and states that commercial, revenue-generating, or professional work requires a Pro licence. That means a student can learn rendering for free, and a hobbyist can visualise their own home for free, but a practice sending renders to paying clients should budget for Pro and read the current licence before using Community output commercially.

D5 Render works best when you already have a model from SketchUp, Revit, Rhino, Archicad, or a similar tool and you need beautiful views quickly, including walkthrough video and panorama tours for clients who cannot read plans. Real-time rendering still needs a reasonably capable computer, so check D5's current system requirements against your machine before promising a same-day rendered walkthrough. For an Indian practice, the client value is obvious: a family that cannot interpret a floor plan can immediately understand a rendered living room, a courtyard in afternoon light, or a walkthrough of a flat.

## Veras: AI visualisation inside the software you already use

Veras, by Chaos, is an AI visualisation tool that runs inside Revit, SketchUp, Rhino, Archicad, and other modelling software. Instead of exporting your model to a separate renderer and starting again, you stay in your model, set a view, and use AI to explore materials, lighting, and atmosphere while keeping the underlying geometry as your guide. That geometry link is Veras's main advantage over a pure text-to-image tool: the render stays recognisably your building, even as the AI reinterprets its surfaces and mood.

The free entry is a 14-day trial with 100 credits for image and video generation, roughly equivalent to about ten images or ten AI videos at the quoted sizes. The trial then converts to a paid annual licence — listed at $348 per year for a named licence and $528 for a floating licence, before taxes — unless you cancel at least a day before the trial ends. That auto-conversion rule is worth putting in your calendar the day you start the trial. Prices are stated by Chaos as subject to change, so re-check the Veras trial page before starting.

Veras is best for practising architects who already model in Revit or SketchUp and want fast design-option renders for client meetings, competitions, or internal reviews. It is not a replacement for a final photorealistic render, and it is not a drawing tool. Use it to test how a facade material, a courtyard, or an evening light condition feels, then confirm the chosen direction with a proper render and a proper drawing.

## Midjourney: concept imagery and mood boards, not drawings

Midjourney is a text-to-image tool that remains popular with architects for early-stage concept visuals. You describe a scene in words — materials, light, viewpoint, mood, architectural references — and it generates atmospheric images that work well for mood boards, competition entries, and early client brainstorming. A skilled prompt can test a courtyard typology, a brick-and-timber material palette, or a monsoon-light atmosphere in minutes, long before any model exists.

The Basic plan is listed at $10 per month, or $8 per month billed annually, with about 200 image jobs per month, and higher plans at $30, $60, and $120 per month add more fast GPU time and features such as unlimited relaxed image generation and private Stealth Mode on Pro and Mega plans. There is no free plan in the pricing checked for this article, so budget for at least the Basic plan if you want to use it for client work, and check the current Midjourney pricing page, since plans and limits change.

The limit that matters most is geometry. Midjourney outputs are completely disconnected from your model: the AI can alter proportions, invent structure that cannot stand up, merge rooms, or draw staircases that lead nowhere. Treat every Midjourney image as a mood reference, label it as concept imagery when you share it, and never present it as a proposal drawing. Used that way, it is excellent. Used as a plan, it is misleading, and in a client relationship that confusion is expensive.

## Finch: many layout options from one massing, for larger projects

Finch is a generative design tool that works from building volumes taken from Rhino, Revit, or Forma, and then generates floor plates, corridors, stairwells, a unit mix around the corridor, and unit plans drawn from a firm's own plan library. Its strength is option testing: instead of drawing one residential floor plate and defending it, a team can compare many plates, see the trade-offs in unit mix and circulation, and go deeper into the strongest options faster.

The free plan includes the editing tools, which makes it possible to learn the workflow without paying. The important pricing detail is that AI-powered floor plan generation and code and compliance checks are reported to sit in the Enterprise plan, listed at €14,500 per year for three seats, while the Basic plan at €79 per month adds unit mix and circulation features. Those figures come from third-party pricing summaries checked for this article and should be confirmed on Finch's own pricing page, because enterprise pricing in particular changes and is often negotiated. For a small Indian practice doing single houses, Finch is probably too much tool at too high a price; for a firm doing multi-family housing or developer schemes, the option-testing value may justify a proper paid evaluation.

Finch is best for early building design exploration on larger projects, especially where unit mix and circulation drive the commercial result. It still needs design judgement and project-specific calibration, and any compliance check it offers must be treated as a prompt for a human check against Indian local rules, not as proof of compliance.

## Using these tools in India: bye-laws, climate, clients, and costs

India is not one market for architecture, and AI tools do not know that unless you tell them. A setback that works in one city may fail in another, floor area ratio and height limits depend on the local plan, and parking, fire, and ventilation requirements differ by building type and authority. Some tools describe themselves as optimised for other countries' regulations, and none of the tools on this list should be assumed to understand your city's bye-laws. The safe rule is simple: generate options with AI, then verify dimensions, setbacks, coverage, height, parking, staircase, and ventilation requirements against the current local bye-laws and the National Building Code guidance that your authority applies, with the registered architect on the project signing off.

Climate is the second India-specific test. A render that looks beautiful in soft northern light may mislead a client about heat gain through west-facing glass in Ahmedabad, glare in a Jaipur summer, or damp and ventilation during a Mumbai monsoon. Use daylight and analysis features in Forma or Snaptrude as a starting conversation, but confirm orientation, shading, ventilation, and material choices with local climate knowledge and, where the project warrants it, a proper thermal or daylight study. A courtyard, jaali screen, deep verandah, or shaded balcony is not just a visual style; it is a climate decision, and AI imagery alone will not make it correctly.

Clients are the third test. Many Indian homeowners and developers do not read plans fluently, and that is exactly where 3D views and renders earn their fee. A short rendered walkthrough or a panorama tour on a phone often communicates more than ten plan sheets, and it reduces the late-stage changes that happen when a client finally understands a space only after construction starts. Be honest in that conversation: say which images are concept visuals, which are renders of the actual model, and which drawings are the approved set. That honesty protects both the client and the practice.

Costs and hardware are the fourth test. Most of these tools are priced in US dollars or euros, so Indian buyers should check the final price including taxes and any currency conversion before paying, and should check whether an annual plan is genuinely cheaper than a monthly plan for the length of the project. Real-time rendering tools also need capable hardware, so a laptop that runs a plan generator happily may still struggle with a large rendered scene. Test the free version or trial on your actual machine, on your actual project file, before committing a practice to a paid plan.

## What to avoid

Avoid presenting any AI plan or render as approved, structural, or construction-ready. AI tools in this category do not produce structural engineering, load calculations, column placement, mechanical and electrical layouts, or code-compliant construction documentation, and they do not carry a licensed professional's stamp. If a client, contractor, or authority needs that level of document, it must come from the qualified professionals on the project.

Avoid paying for a tool before testing it on a real project. Free credits run out, trials auto-convert, and export or BIM features often sit on a higher plan than the one advertised most loudly. Start the trial on a live project, test the exact export you need — PDF, DXF, DWG, or Revit — and check the licence for commercial use before the trial ends.

Avoid sharing AI images without labels. A Midjourney concept, a Veras option render, and a D5 render of the final model are three different things, and a client who cannot tell them apart may approve the wrong one. Label concept imagery as concept imagery, and keep the approved drawing set clearly separate from the visual exploration.

Avoid feeding confidential client information into a public AI tool without checking its privacy terms. Site addresses, client names, land documents, and unreleased project designs should not be pasted into a prompt or uploaded to a tool whose data handling you have not read. For sensitive projects, prefer tools with clear business or enterprise terms, and ask the vendor directly if the terms are unclear.

Avoid letting the tool choose the design. If every option looks the same, the brief is probably too vague, or the tool's defaults are doing the designing. Change the brief, add constraints from the site and the client, test a contradictory option on purpose, and keep the architect's judgement — on light, ventilation, privacy, circulation, and cost — as the final filter.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which AI tool is best for floor plans for architects in India?**

For fast residential options, start with Maket, and for an editable 2D and 3D project from a plan image, start with Planner 5D. For a BIM-oriented concept workflow that can move towards Revit, test Snaptrude. None of them replaces a checked, compliant drawing set, so pick the tool that fits the project stage and rebuild the chosen option in your professional software.

**Can AI tools make building approval drawings in India?**

No, not on their own. Approval depends on local bye-laws that vary by state and city, and sanction drawings are signed by the owner and a Council of Architecture-registered architect or an eligible engineer under the applicable rules. AI tools can generate options and discussion material, but the submission set must be prepared, dimensioned, and checked by the qualified professionals on the project.

**Are these AI architecture tools free in India?**

Some starting points are free: Planner 5D has a free plan, D5 Render has a free Community version for non-commercial use, Maket and Snaptrude have limited free credits, and Forma and Veras offer time-limited trials. Exports, BIM mode, commercial use, and unlimited AI features usually require a paid plan, so read the licence and the current pricing page before relying on a free tier for client work.

**Which AI rendering tool is best for architects?**

For fast real-time rendering from an existing model, D5 Render is the strongest free starting point, with Pro needed for commercial work. For AI visualisation inside Revit, SketchUp, Rhino, or Archicad, Veras is the more integrated choice. For pure concept imagery before any model exists, Midjourney is best, but its images are mood references, not renders of your building.

**Do I need a VPN to use these AI tools in India?**

No VPN requirement was found for the tools on this list in the sources checked for this article; they are international web and desktop products usable from India with a normal internet connection. If a tool, trial, or payment page behaves differently for you, check the vendor's help page and your network before assuming a workaround is safe or permitted.

**Will AI replace architects?**

No, and this list shows why. AI is fast at options, imagery, and early analysis, but it does not take legal responsibility, does not know your city's bye-laws unless you check them, does not coordinate structure and services, and does not supervise construction. The architects who benefit most are the ones who use AI to explore more options and communicate better, while keeping professional judgement and responsibility firmly with a human.

## Final verdict

Start with Maket or Planner 5D for plans and D5 Render for visuals, and let a registered architect check every drawing before approval.
