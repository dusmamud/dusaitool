## The short answer

For most journalists in India, the strongest free starting stack is simple: Otter.ai or open-source Whisper for turning interviews into text, Google's Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM) for making sense of a pile of documents, Perplexity for cited web research, and Google's Fact Check Explorer together with Indian fact-checkers such as BOOM, Alt News and the Press Information Bureau's Fact Check Unit for verification. Add Grammarly's free tier for clean copy. No single tool replaces reporting, source-checking, or an editor — these tools save time on the mechanical work so that the human work, talking to people and checking primary documents, gets more attention.

That stack matters because Indian newsrooms, freelancers, and student journalists often work in more than one language, on tight deadlines, and on phones or shared laptops. A tool that only works well in English, needs a VPN, or hides its real limits behind a trial is a poor fit. Every tool below was checked for whether it is usable in India without a VPN, what its free tier actually gives, and where it can go wrong.

A warning that applies to the whole article: prices and free limits change often. The figures quoted here come from the tools' own pricing and help pages at the time of writing. Before you pay, or before you promise an editor a deadline that depends on a free limit, re-check the official pricing or help page of that tool. Where sources disagreed or a limit was described differently on different pages, this article says so rather than picking the friendlier number.

## Transcription: the biggest time-saver in a newsroom

### 1. Otter.ai — the easiest interview transcriber

Otter.ai records live audio, transcribes meetings on Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet, identifies speakers, and lets you search the transcript. Its Basic plan is free and includes 300 monthly transcription minutes, live transcription, speaker identification, and apps for iOS and Android. Its Pro plan is listed at $16.99 a month, or $8.49 a month when billed annually, with 1,200 in-app recording minutes and up to 90 minutes per meeting.

For a journalist, the practical workflow is this: record the interview with the interviewee's consent, upload or transcribe it in Otter, then read the transcript while listening back to the unclear parts. Otter is good, not perfect. Names of people and places, numbers, and words spoken over traffic noise or a bad phone line are exactly where it makes mistakes. Never lift a quote straight from an Otter transcript into print without listening to that passage yourself. That single habit prevents most transcription errors from reaching publication.

Otter's free tier is genuinely useful for a freelancer doing a few interviews a month, but 300 minutes runs out fast during an election season or a long investigation. Plan around it: transcribe the interviews that need verbatim quotes, and use shorter summaries or notes for background conversations.

### 2. Whisper — free, offline, and good for Indian languages

Whisper is OpenAI's open-source speech recognition model, available on GitHub. It can transcribe speech in many languages, translate non-English speech into English, and identify the spoken language. There are six model sizes, from tiny (39 million parameters, about 1 GB of video memory) to large (1,550 million parameters, about 10 GB), so you can trade speed against accuracy depending on your computer.

Whisper's advantage for Indian journalists is control. There is no monthly minute limit, no subscription, and the audio does not have to be uploaded to a company's server if you run it locally. That matters when an interviewee has spoken on condition of confidentiality, or when the recording contains sensitive material. The cost is technical: you need Python, some comfort with the command line, and patience on an ordinary laptop, where the larger models run slowly. If you are not comfortable with that setup, Otter or Descript is the better first step, and Whisper is worth learning later, ideally with help from a technically minded colleague.

Whisper also handles Hindi and other Indian languages better than many journalists expect, but it is not magic. Code-switching — the Hinglish mix of Hindi and English that dominates real Indian interviews — still trips it up, and it can mishear proper nouns. As with Otter, listen back before publishing any quote.

### 3. Trint — transcription built for newsrooms

Trint is designed for journalists and media teams: upload audio or video, get a transcript you can edit like a document, highlight key moments, collaborate with colleagues, translate into more than 50 languages, and export captions. Its trial is a 7-day free trial of the Advanced plan, which lets new users upload 3 audio or video files, with the first 5 minutes of each file transcribed. Subscribing unlocks full transcriptions of those files.

That trial structure means Trint is best tried on a real, short piece of work — one interview, not a vague experiment — so you can judge whether its editor and collaboration features are worth paying for. For a newsroom that transcribes daily, Trint's editing workflow can be faster than juggling separate tools. For a solo freelancer, the free tiers of Otter and Descript, or Whisper, usually cover the same need at no cost.

### 4. Descript — when the story is also audio or video

Descript transcribes audio and video and then lets you edit the media by editing the text: delete a sentence from the transcript and it disappears from the audio. Its free plan gives 1 media hour a month, 100 AI credits as a one-time grant, and 720p exports with a watermark. Paid plans start with Hobbyist at $16 a month for 10 media hours and watermark-free 1080p exports.

Descript earns its place here because many journalists now also produce podcasts, reels, and video explainers. If your final product is only a written article, Otter or Whisper is simpler. If you need captioned clips for social media from the same interview, Descript lets you do transcription, editing, and captions in one place instead of three.

## Research: from a pile of documents to a usable brief

### 5. Gemini Notebook — research grounded in your own sources

Gemini Notebook, formerly NotebookLM, is Google's AI research assistant. You upload documents, PDFs, slides, web links, YouTube links, or audio, and it answers questions using only those sources, with citations back to them. Google says each source can contain up to 500,000 words or up to 200 MB for uploaded files, and that uploaded sources are never used to train its models and stay private unless you share the notebook.

For journalists, that grounding is the point. A general chatbot will happily answer a question about a policy with a confident-sounding paragraph that mixes up two different years. Gemini Notebook, used properly, answers from the budget speech, the gazette notification, or the report you actually uploaded, and shows you where it found the answer. In India it is available free with a Google account, with higher limits on qualifying Workspace plans, and it appears on Google's India subscriptions page at ₹0 a month with a Google account.

Use it for the unglamorous middle of reporting: feed in a 200-page report, a set of RTI replies, or ten press releases, and ask for a timeline, a list of contradictions, or every mention of a particular company. Then verify each answer against the cited source before using it. NotebookLM-style tools are also increasingly used inside newsrooms to brief reporters quickly, which is useful — but a briefing is not a story, and an AI summary of a document is not a substitute for reading the key pages yourself.

### 6. Perplexity — cited answers for fast web research

Perplexity is an AI search tool that answers questions with citations to web sources. Its free tier offers search with accurate answers and citations, access to basic models, and limited daily usage. Its Pro plan is listed at $17 a month when billed annually, with higher limits, premium data sources, and the ability to pick a preferred model per query.

Perplexity is useful at the start of a story: who said what, when did a scheme launch, what does the official data say? Because every answer carries citations, you can click through to the original source in seconds, which is exactly the habit good research requires. The trap is stopping at the Perplexity answer. Treat it as a research assistant that finds sources, not as a source itself. If Perplexity cites a news article, read the article. If it cites a government page, open the government page. If a claim has no citation, treat it as unverified.

### 7. Google Pinpoint — for leaks, archives, and large document sets

Pinpoint, from Google's Journalist Studio, is a free research tool built for journalists working with large collections of documents. You can upload files or pull them from Google Drive, search across thousands of documents including handwritten material, images, and email archives, transcribe and search audio and video, turn similarly structured tables into sortable spreadsheets, and share collections with colleagues. Collections are private by default.

Most daily stories do not need Pinpoint. Investigations do. If you receive a trove of municipal records, court filings, or company documents, Pinpoint's ability to search across all of them at once, filter by people, organisations, and locations, and jump from a transcript passage back to the audio is genuinely powerful. It is also a good reminder that the best research tool is often the boring one: a searchable archive beats a clever chatbot when the story depends on what a document actually says.

### 8. ChatGPT and Gemini — useful general assistants, with strict limits

General chatbots such as ChatGPT and Google's Gemini app are available in India with free tiers — Gemini's India page lists a free tier at ₹0 a month with a Google account, including its Flash-Lite model, image generation, Deep Research, and Gemini Live. They are good for brainstorming questions for an interview, drafting a structure for a story, simplifying a dense paragraph so you can check your own understanding, or turning rough notes into a first draft.

They are bad, on their own, as sources of fact. Both tools can produce confident, well-written statements that are wrong about dates, statistics, names, and quotations. For journalism, the rule is simple: use chatbots to think and to draft, never to establish a fact. Every factual claim in a chatbot draft needs a primary source — an official document, a dataset, a named person on the record — before it goes anywhere near publication. If you cannot find that source, the claim does not go in.

## Fact-checking: the part no AI tool can do for you

### 9. Google Fact Check Explorer — search existing fact-checks first

Before you spend hours verifying a viral claim, check whether an independent fact-checker already has. Google's Fact Check Explorer lets journalists and fact-checkers search fact-checks published by independent organisations around the world, and it can also search by image: upload an image or paste its link to see whether it has appeared in an existing fact-check. Google's own blog describes this as a way to find fact checks in search results and to dig deeper with the Explorer, including through its Fact Check Tools API.

The workflow is fast: copy the claim, search the Explorer, read the full fact-check, and check its evidence. If a reputable fact-checker has already debunked a video as being from another country or another year, that saves your newsroom a day of work. But remember the limit: the Explorer only surfaces fact-checks that exist. A claim with no fact-check attached is not verified; it is simply unchecked.

### 10. Indian fact-checkers: BOOM, Alt News, and PIB Fact Check

India has a strong fact-checking ecosystem, and Indian journalists should know it by name. BOOM, founded as a broader investigative effort in 2014 and operating as a fact-checking organisation since November 2016, is certified by the International Fact-Checking Network and works in Hindi, English, and Bengali. It verifies claims against two or more independent sources, cites tangible evidence, and runs a WhatsApp tipline for suspicious content.

Alt News describes a similar method: monitoring social media and mainstream media for dubious claims, then researching them with tools including Google reverse image search and InVID for video frames, date-filtered searches, contact with local authorities or police for sensitive rumours, and reference to primary or official data. Its methodology page is worth reading in full, because it is effectively a free training course in verification.

The Press Information Bureau's Fact Check Unit, set up in November 2019, tackles fake news about the Government of India, its ministries, departments, and public sector undertakings. Users can send queries over WhatsApp, email, or a web portal, and the unit publishes fact-checks categorised as Fake, Misleading, or True after checking government open-source information and verifying with the concerned organisation. For any claim about a central government scheme, announcement, or statistic, the PIB Fact Check Unit and the ministry's own website are the first places to look — not a chatbot, not a viral post.

These organisations are not AI tools, and that is the point. Fact-checking is a method — find the primary source, check it against a second independent source, show your evidence — and AI tools can speed up the searching, but they cannot supply the judgement. A journalist who only uses AI summaries and never opens a primary document is not fact-checking; they are rewording someone else's homework.

### 11. Reverse image search and InVID — checking photos and videos

Two techniques deserve a permanent place in every Indian journalist's toolkit. First, reverse image search: take a viral photo, search for it, and see whether it appeared earlier in a different context — a flood photo from another state, a protest photo from another year. Second, InVID-style video verification: break a video into frames and reverse-search those frames, since a video that looks new may be an old clip with a new caption.

Alt News explicitly lists both techniques in its methodology, which is a good sign of how standard they are in professional fact-checking. They cost nothing, need no subscription, and catch a large share of viral misinformation before it spreads further. If you do one thing after reading this article, practise reverse image search on the next viral photo you see in a family WhatsApp group. It takes two minutes, and it will change how you read forwarded content forever.

## Writing and editing: clean copy, faster

### 12. Grammarly — a second pair of eyes on English copy

Grammarly's free tier catches spelling and grammar mistakes and shows tone suggestions, while its Pro plan is listed at $12 a month and adds full-sentence rewrites, fluency suggestions, citation consistency, AI-generated text detection, and accidental plagiarism detection, with 2,000 AI prompts a month per member against 100 on the free tier.

For journalists writing in English as a second or third language — which describes a large share of Indian newsrooms — Grammarly is a genuinely useful safety net for deadline copy. It is not an editor, though. It will happily polish a sentence that is factually wrong, and its plagiarism and AI-detection features are aids, not verdicts. Use it after the facts are settled, not instead of settling them. For Hindi and other Indian-language copy, Grammarly's value is limited, since its strength is English; for those stories, a careful human sub-editor and a style guide matter more than any tool.

## Using AI tools in India: language, connectivity, and cost

Indian journalism has constraints that a generic global tools list ignores. First, language. Interviews arrive in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Malayalam, and a dozen other languages, often mixed with English in the same sentence. Tools that advertise multilingual support — Otter, Trint, Whisper — handle this better than tools built only for English, but none of them is flawless on code-switched speech. Budget time to correct transcripts, and keep the original audio until the story is published.

Second, connectivity and hardware. Not every district reporter has a fast connection or a new laptop. Whisper running locally avoids upload delays but needs a capable machine; Otter and Descript need a stable connection for uploads but run fine in a browser. Pick the tool that fits the reporter's actual setup, not the one with the longest feature list. All the tools in this article are usable in India without a VPN through their normal websites and apps, but free-tier limits and payment in dollars can still be a barrier for freelancers — another reason to start with the free tiers and only pay when a tool has clearly earned its cost on real stories.

Third, cost in rupees. Most of these tools price in US dollars, so the rupee cost moves with the exchange rate and with bank charges. Before subscribing, check the price in your account's checkout page, not just a blog post, and check whether the plan renews automatically. A tool that costs $17 a month sounds small until it renews for a year while you are on a break between assignments.

Fourth, source protection. This is the most serious India-specific concern. Uploading an interview with a whistleblower, a survivor, or a source who spoke off the record to a cloud transcription service creates a copy of that material outside your control. Read the tool's privacy policy, check whether it trains models on your uploads — Gemini Notebook says it does not — and prefer local tools such as Whisper, or your newsroom's own systems, for genuinely sensitive material. When in doubt, ask your editor, and never upload material you promised to keep private without thinking through where it goes.

## What to avoid

- **Publishing AI output without checking it.** A fluent paragraph is not a verified fact. Every date, number, name, and quote needs a primary source, especially before publication.
- **Uploading sensitive source material to cloud tools without thought.** Transcription services, chatbots, and research tools all store what you give them in some form. Sensitive interviews belong on local tools or newsroom systems, not on a free consumer account.
- **Trusting a single AI summary of a document.** Summaries drop caveats, merge two different claims, and occasionally invent a detail. Read the cited pages yourself for anything load-bearing in the story.
- **Planning a deadline around a free limit you have not tested.** A 300-minute transcription allowance, a 7-day trial, or a 1-hour media allowance runs out at the worst moment. Test the limit on a small story first.
- **Paying before trying.** Every tool here has a free tier or a trial. Use it on real work, with real deadlines, before spending money. If the free tier does not help on a real story, the paid tier rarely will.
- **Using AI to write about a community you have not spoken to.** AI tools are good at producing generic text about a place or a community. That generic text is not reporting. If the story is about people, talk to people.
- **Forgetting the correction.** AI tools make mistakes, and so do journalists. When an error reaches publication, correct it promptly and transparently, the way BOOM describes doing in its own methodology. A fast correction protects readers and your credibility far more than quietly editing a page.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which free AI tool is best for transcribing interviews in India?**
Otter.ai's free Basic plan, with 300 transcription minutes a month, is the easiest starting point for most journalists. If you are comfortable with technical setup and want no monthly limit and local processing, open-source Whisper is the stronger long-term choice.

**Can AI transcription handle Hindi and Hinglish interviews accurately?**
Partly. Whisper, Otter, and Trint all support multiple languages, but code-switched Hinglish, strong regional accents, and noisy field recordings still cause errors, especially with names and numbers. Always listen back to any passage you intend to quote directly.

**Is NotebookLM still available, or has it become Gemini Notebook?**
Google has renamed NotebookLM as Gemini Notebook. It remains the same kind of tool: an AI research assistant grounded in the sources you upload, with citations, rather than a general chatbot. If you see older guides mentioning NotebookLM, they are describing the same product under its previous name.

**How do I fact-check a viral WhatsApp forward before sharing or reporting it?**
Start with Google's Fact Check Explorer and a reverse image search for any photo or video frame. Then check Indian fact-checkers such as BOOM and Alt News, and for government claims, the PIB Fact Check Unit. If no fact-check exists, verify the claim against a primary source — an official document, a dataset, or a named person on the record — before treating it as true.

**Are these AI tools legal and safe to use for journalism in India?**
Using transcription, research, and writing tools is not itself restricted, and all the tools listed here are accessible in India without a VPN. The legal and ethical duties sit with the journalist: get consent before recording, protect source confidentiality, respect copyright when uploading others' material, and do not publish unverified claims. When a story involves legal risk, check with your editor or a media lawyer rather than relying on a tool's terms page.

**Do I need to pay for Perplexity, ChatGPT, or Gemini to do good research?**
No, not to start. All three have free tiers that are enough for early-stage research, finding sources, and drafting. Paid tiers raise usage limits and unlock stronger models, which helps heavy users, but they do not remove the need to open and verify the original sources yourself.

**What is the biggest mistake journalists make with AI tools?**
Treating an AI answer as a source. AI tools are excellent at finding documents, transcribing speech, and drafting text, but the source of a published fact must always be a document, a dataset, or a person — never the AI tool's own output. Keep that line clear and most other mistakes become easy to avoid.

**Can AI tools replace a sub-editor or fact-checker?**
No. Grammarly can catch spelling and grammar errors, and fact-checking tools can surface existing fact-checks, but neither can judge whether a story is fair, whether a source is credible, or whether a claim needs a second independent confirmation. Those judgements are the core of journalism, and they stay human.

## Final verdict

Start with Otter or Whisper, Gemini Notebook, and Fact Check Explorer — and always check the original source.

That stack costs nothing to try, works in India without a VPN, and covers the three jobs where AI genuinely helps journalists: turning speech into text, making sense of documents, and finding existing verification. The discipline around it matters more than the choice of tool. Record with consent, keep the original audio, check every quote by listening back, open every cited source, and never publish a fact whose only support is an AI-generated paragraph. Do that, and AI tools become what they should be for journalism: a faster way to do the mechanical work, leaving more time for the reporting, the questions, and the judgement that no tool can supply. Prices and free limits will change, so re-check the official pricing and help pages before paying or promising a deadline — and when a tool stops earning its place on a real story, drop it without sentiment and keep the one that does.
