## The direct answer

If you play chess in India and want to improve with AI tools, start with Lichess. It is free, it works in India without a VPN, and it gives you engine analysis, puzzles, opening study and a "learn from your mistakes" review without a paid subscription. Add Chess.com Game Review when you want a guided, beginner-friendly explanation of a game, and add Chessable when you want structured courses and spaced repetition for openings and tactics. You do not need to pay for anything in your first month. Pay only later, and only for one tool, when you can name the exact limit that is blocking you.

That order matters because chess improvement is not about having the strongest engine. Every serious engine today is far stronger than any human player. What matters is whether the tool shows you your own repeated mistakes, gives you puzzles built from those mistakes, and helps you remember the right idea next time. A tool that only tells you the best move in one position will not fix a weakness that appears in twenty games.

## Lichess: the free starting point

Lichess is a free, non-profit chess platform, and it is the strongest free starting point for an Indian player. Its own features page says that all features are free for everybody, and that a Patron donation does not unlock extra features. That is unusual, and it is why Lichess belongs first in this article.

Open any game you played on Lichess and ask for computer analysis. The analysis board runs Stockfish in your browser, and cloud engine analysis is listed as unlimited. The deeper, server-side post-game report, the one that gives you an accuracy percentage, an average centipawn loss figure and labels such as inaccuracy, mistake and blunder, is capped at 40 games a day. That cap is the same for a free account and for a donating Patron, so paying does not buy you a bigger number. For almost every club and casual player, 40 deep reports a day is far more than enough. If you play five games a day, you will never touch the cap.

Lichess also gives you the two training tools that matter most after analysis. First, puzzles: unlimited tactical puzzles taken from real user games, plus Puzzle Streak, Puzzle Storm and Puzzle Racer for speed and pattern practice. Second, "learn from your mistakes": after a analysed game, Lichess can turn your own blunders into exercises, so you practise the exact position you got wrong instead of a random puzzle. That loop, play, analyse, re-practise your own mistakes, is the single most effective habit in this article.

Beyond that, Lichess offers a global opening explorer built from billions of games, a personal opening explorer built from your own games, shareable Studies where you can save annotated analysis, Chess Insights that break down your play by phase, opening and time control, and a seven-piece endgame tablebase. All of these are listed as unlimited on the free account. The app is available on Android and iOS, and the site works in a normal mobile browser, which matters if you mainly play on a phone.

One honest limitation: Lichess does not talk to you like a coach. It shows engine lines, evaluations and labels, but you must learn to read them. If you are a complete beginner and engine lines look like meaningless numbers, pair Lichess with Chess.com Game Review or DecodeChess for explanations, and keep Lichess as your free analysis home.

## Chess.com Game Review: the guided review

Chess.com Game Review is the most polished guided review for beginners. After a game, it gives you an accuracy score, labels moves as brilliant, good, inaccuracy, mistake or blunder, picks out key moments, and lets you retry the critical positions. Paid members also get a coach that explains moves in words. The self-analysis board lets you step through engine lines, turn the evaluation bar and suggestion arrows on or off, and save your analysis to your library.

The free limit is the part to understand clearly. Chess.com's own help material states that the free Basic tier gets 1 full game review analysis per day. Reopening a review you already ran does not count against that daily limit, so you can revisit yesterday's review as often as you like. Unlimited Game Review requires a Premium membership, and cloud analysis plus the deepest engine settings are Diamond-only features. Chess.com's own documentation has not always stated the free number in the same place, and some paying Gold members have publicly misunderstood whether Gold removes the cap, so do not assume a mid-tier plan removes it. Check the current tier comparison page before you upgrade.

Use the free review wisely. Do not spend it on a three-minute bullet game you barely remember. Spend it on your longest, most serious game of the day, the one where you actually tried. Then spend the rest of your study time on Lichess, where analysis is not metered the same way, or on puzzles. That combination gives you a guided explanation for one game and unlimited engine checking for everything else.

Chess.com also offers bots at many levels, lessons and puzzles, and a large player pool for finding games at any hour, which suits Indian time zones well because the site is busy around the clock. The free tier limits some of these, for example the number of bots, so again, check the tier page rather than assuming.

## Stockfish: the engine underneath almost everything

Stockfish is the free, open-source chess engine that powers much of the analysis on Lichess and in many other tools. It has used a neural network evaluation since 2020, so the old distinction between "engine" and "AI" no longer means much here: Stockfish is a neural-network AI combined with a very fast search. Different sources report different Stockfish versions at different times, for example Stockfish 16, 17, 18 and 19 have all appeared in recent tool pages, because tools update at different speeds. Where sources disagree, this article says so rather than picking one version number and pretending it is universal. What does not change is the point: Stockfish is free, it is stronger than any human, and it runs on ordinary hardware.

You can run Stockfish yourself, on a computer or a phone, inside a chess board app that supports engines. That gives you full control over depth and the number of lines shown, and your games never have to leave your device. It is the right choice when you want to analyse an opening line deeply, prepare for a specific opponent, or check a position offline, for example on a train or during a power cut, after the app and engine are installed.

What Stockfish alone will not do is coach you. It will show you that a move loses about a pawn of evaluation, but it will not explain the idea in words, build you a study plan, or remind you to practise that pattern tomorrow. That is why this article pairs it with Lichess, Chessable or DecodeChess rather than recommending it as a complete training system on its own. If you only install an engine and stare at evaluations, you will learn less than someone who does twenty puzzles a day.

## Chessable: courses and remembering what you study

Chessable is a training platform built around courses and spaced repetition. Its MoveTrainer system schedules reviews of positions so that you revisit an opening line or a tactical pattern just before you would otherwise forget it. Its own help centre says that signing up is free and will always remain so, that a selection of free courses and all essential features including MoveTrainer are free, and that paid PRO subscriptions and premium courses are optional upgrades, never required to use Chessable.

That makes Chessable a good second or third tool, after Lichess. Use it for structured study: pick one opening course or one tactics course, work through it in short daily sessions, and let the repetition system do the remembering. Do not collect ten courses at once. Chess improvement from courses comes from finishing one short course and actually playing its lines, not from owning a library.

Chessable also suits players who like clear structure. Lichess gives you freedom and unlimited tools but no curriculum, while Chessable gives you a path: lesson, exercise, review, repeat. If you are preparing for a school, college or club tournament and you want a plan rather than random puzzles, that structure is worth more than a stronger engine.

## Aimchess: finding the pattern across many games

Aimchess, now part of Chess.com, looks across many of your games instead of analysing one game at a time. It scores skill areas such as opening play, tactics, endgames and time management against players at your rating, and it builds puzzles from your own mistakes. It connects to Chess.com, Lichess and Chess24 accounts, so it can study games you played elsewhere.

This is useful at exactly the point where single-game analysis stops helping. One blunder is an accident. The same kind of blunder in fifteen games is a weakness, and Aimchess is designed to name it. A player who keeps losing rook endgames, or keeps hanging pieces in time trouble, will see that pattern here faster than by scrolling through old games one by one.

Aimchess offers a free tier, and its Premium plan has been reported at about $7.99 a month. Treat that price as a check-before-you-pay figure, not a promise, because pricing pages change and regional pricing may differ. Start on the free tier, connect one account, and look at what it says about your last few dozen games. If the weaknesses it names surprise you, the tool is earning its place. If it only tells you what you already knew, keep your money and keep doing puzzles on Lichess.

## DecodeChess: explanations in plain language

DecodeChess answers a different question from the other tools: not just what the best move is, but why. It explains positions and moves in plain language, with the aim of teaching the idea behind a move rather than only the evaluation number. For a player who finds engine lines hard to read, that explanation layer is genuinely helpful.

Its free tier is small and honest: 1 decoded game per day, with decoded positions and deep explanations held back for paid plans. The paid Unlimited plan has been listed at $8.25 a month on monthly billing, or $84 a year on annual billing, with a seven-day money-back guarantee mentioned on its pricing page. Again, re-check that page before paying, because prices and guarantees change.

DecodeChess is best used sparingly, for one instructive game or one confusing position a day, rather than as your main analysis tool. Decode the game that confused you most, read the explanation slowly, write down the idea in your own words, and then practise that idea in puzzles. A plain-language explanation you cannot restate yourself has not yet become knowledge.

## Human-like sparring: Maia bots and Chess.com bots

Not every training game should be against a superhuman engine. Stockfish at full strength will beat you in ways that teach you nothing, because its moves are beyond human planning. Maia, a research AI that imitates human play at different rating levels, is available as bots on Lichess and gives you fixed-level sparring: an opponent that plays, and blunders, more like a human at your level. Chess.com bots serve a similar purpose, with personalities and levels that let you practise a specific kind of position without the pressure of a rated game.

Use sparring for a specific purpose, not as a replacement for playing real people. For example, play ten games against a bot at your level with one opening you are learning, and analyse each one on Lichess afterwards. That is deliberate practice. Playing a hundred casual bot games without analysis is entertainment, and there is nothing wrong with entertainment, but do not confuse it with improvement.

## A weekly routine that actually works

Tools only help if they become a routine. Here is a simple week that uses only free tools, with paid tools as optional extras.

First, play fewer, longer games. Three rapid games a week, analysed properly, teach more than thirty bullet games you never review. After each rapid game, run the Lichess analysis, note your two biggest mistakes, and use "learn from your mistakes" to practise those exact positions until you solve them.

Second, do tactics every day. Fifteen to twenty minutes of puzzles on Lichess, daily, beats a two-hour puzzle binge once a month. Mix puzzle types: some fast puzzles for pattern recognition, some slow puzzles where you calculate fully before moving. If a puzzle theme keeps beating you, for example forks or discovered attacks, Lichess practice courses cover those themes directly.

Third, study one opening idea a week on Chessable or in a Lichess Study, and then play it in your games that week. Write down the first five moves, the main idea, and the most common reply. After the week, check your games in that opening with the Lichess opening explorer and see where you left the preparation.

Fourth, once a week, look across your games rather than at one game. Use Lichess Insights or Aimchess to ask: where do I lose most often, in the opening, the middlegame or the endgame, and am I losing on time or on the board? Then set next week's puzzle and study time to match the answer. If you lose on time, play longer time controls. If you lose endgames, study endgames, not openings.

Fifth, keep a simple notebook, paper or digital, with one line per week: the mistake you repeated, the idea you learned, and the opening you practised. After a month, that notebook is a better record of your progress than your rating graph, because ratings swing with form and opponents while learned ideas stay.

## Using these tools in India

All the tools in this article are web or app based and work in India on a normal internet connection, without a VPN. That is worth stating plainly, because many "best AI tools" lists include tools that are blocked, unusable or payment-blocked in India, and this article only recommends tools that an Indian player can actually open and use.

A few India-specific practical points help. On a mobile data connection, Lichess is comparatively light and works well in a mobile browser, while long cloud analysis and video courses use more data, so save those for Wi-Fi. If you share a phone or a family computer, use your own account so your games, puzzles and course progress stay separate. If you pay for any tool, check whether it accepts Indian payment methods such as UPI or Indian cards before you commit, because some international checkouts do not, and check whether the price shown includes taxes. Do not use a VPN to get around a payment or regional restriction, and do not share accounts to split a subscription unless the tool's own terms allow it.

Language is another practical point. Most of these tools are in English, and Lichess supports many languages, but chess notation itself is universal: piece letters, coordinates and engine numbers mean the same thing in any language. If English explanations are hard, DecodeChess-style plain explanations and Chessable's visual board drills can help, and you can keep your own notebook in Hindi, English or any language you think in.

For school and college players, these tools fit around tournament preparation. Use Lichess Studies to prepare an opening with a coach or a friend, share the study link, and annotate it together. Use puzzles for daily warm-up before a tournament game, not for learning a brand-new opening on the morning of the match. And remember that online ratings and engine accuracy scores are training feedback, not official titles: real over-the-board improvement still needs slow games, a real board and, ideally, a coach or a club.

## What to avoid

Avoid paying for five tools at once. A stack of subscriptions does not make you stronger, and it makes it harder to know which tool actually helped. Start free, add one paid tool at a time, and cancel anything you have not opened in two weeks.

Avoid engine worship. If your study is only watching Stockfish lines without calculating yourself first, you are training your eyes, not your calculation. Always guess the move before you look at the engine, and always ask why the engine's move works. If you cannot explain it, you have not learned it.

Avoid only studying openings. Beginners lose most games on tactics and blunders, not on move ten of an opening. If your puzzle accuracy is low, spend your time there before buying an opening course. Openings matter more as you get stronger, not less, but the order still starts with tactics.

Avoid cheating, and avoid tools that encourage it. Using an engine during a rated online game, or during an online tournament, is cheating, and it can get your account closed. All the tools in this article are for analysis and training after the game, or for practice games that are clearly not rated competition. Keep that line clean: engine on for study, engine off for rated play.

Avoid trusting any single number. An accuracy score, a centipawn loss figure and a rating are all useful, but they all move with the opponent, the time control and the engine depth. Judge yourself over twenty games, not one, and judge a tool over a month, not a day.

Avoid copying a paid plan from a creator or a friend without checking your own limit. If someone says a tool is "free", check what free means: one game a day, forty games a day, free courses only, or fully free are very different offers, and this article has tried to state each one exactly.

## FAQ

**Which is the best free AI chess tool in India?**

Lichess, because its analysis board, puzzles, opening explorer, studies and insights are free and unlimited for a free account, with only the deep server-side game report capped at 40 games a day. No other tool in this article gives that much for free.

**Is Chess.com Game Review free?**

Partly. The free Basic tier gives 1 full game review per day, and you can reopen a review you already ran without using another daily credit. Unlimited reviews and some advanced analysis features need a paid Premium membership, so use the free review on your most serious game of the day.

**Do I need Stockfish on my own computer if I use Lichess?**

No, not to start. Lichess already runs Stockfish for you in the browser and in its cloud analysis. Install Stockfish locally only when you want deeper control, offline analysis, or preparation that you do not want to run through a website.

**Can AI tools really make me a better player, or do they just show engine moves?**

They help when you use them as training, not as answers. Puzzles from your own mistakes, spaced repetition on Chessable, and weakness reports from Aimchess all turn engine output into practice. Simply reading the best move once, without practising it, rarely changes your next game.

**Which tool is best for tactics?**

Lichess puzzles, because they are unlimited, drawn from real games, and paired with "learn from your mistakes" exercises from your own games. Chessable adds structured tactics courses with repetition, which suits players who want a set curriculum rather than endless puzzles.

**Which tool is best for understanding why a move is bad?**

DecodeChess, because it explains ideas in plain language rather than only showing an evaluation number, and Chess.com Game Review, because its coach explanations and move labels are designed for beginners. Use either one for your most confusing game, and Lichess for everything else.

**Are these tools legal for tournament preparation?**

Yes, for study and preparation before a game. Analysing your past games, studying openings and solving puzzles with AI tools is normal modern preparation. Using an engine during a rated game or a tournament game is not allowed, and online platforms treat it as cheating.

**How much should I pay for chess AI tools?**

Start at zero. Most players improve for months on Lichess alone, plus the free tiers of Chess.com, Chessable and DecodeChess. Pay only when a free limit, such as one review a day, is regularly stopping a study habit you already have, and pay for one tool at a time.

**What if my internet is slow or I mostly play on a phone?**

Use the Lichess mobile app or mobile site, which is comparatively light, do puzzles daily, and save deep analysis and video courses for Wi-Fi. Stockfish in a phone board app can also analyse offline once installed, which helps during travel or outages.

**Should a beginner start with openings or tactics?**

Tactics first. Beginners lose games on hanging pieces and missed forks far more often than on opening theory. Do daily puzzles, learn from your own mistakes on Lichess, and add one simple opening at a time only after your tactics are steady.

## Final verdict

Start free on Lichess for analysis and puzzles, add Chess.com Game Review for guided reviews, and pay only when a limit actually blocks you.
