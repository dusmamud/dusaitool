A library runs on three jobs done every day: putting new books into the catalogue correctly, lending them and getting them back on time, and keeping member records straight. When any one of those jobs still lives in a paper register, the library spends its staff time searching for books, chasing overdue returns by memory, and retyping the same member details again and again. The tools in this article replace that register, and the newer ones add artificial intelligence on top: smarter search, automatic catalogue drafts, chatbots that answer reader questions, and reports that show which books actually get borrowed.

The short answer for India is this: start with [Koha](https://koha-community.org/) if you want a full, free, open-source library system that you control, because it covers cataloging, circulation, membership and a public catalogue with no licence fee. Start with [Librarika](https://www.librarika.com/) instead if you want something running today in a browser with no installation, because its free Basic plan covers up to 2,000 records with unlimited patrons and check-outs. Choose [SOUL 3.0](https://soul.inflibnet.ac.in/) if you are a college or university library, because it is built by INFLIBNET for Indian academic libraries with multilingual support and member self-registration. Choose [e-Granthalaya 4.0](https://egranthalaya.nic.in/) if you are a government library, because it is the National Informatics Centre platform made for government libraries, with cloud hosting and a shared union catalogue. Consider [LIBSYS Neo](https://www.libsys.co.in/products/library-solutions/library-management-system) only when you want a paid, AI-first system with a chatbot and predictive reports, and get its price in writing first, because its official pages do not publish a price list. Consider Evergreen if you are part of a group of libraries sharing one catalogue, and consider [Alma](https://exlibrisgroup.com/products/alma-library-services-platform/) from Ex Libris only if you are a large academic institution that needs print, electronic and digital collections managed in one enterprise platform. Use a general AI chatbot only as a drafting helper for catalogue records, never as the catalogue itself, because studies disagree on how accurate its records are and every study agrees a human cataloguer must check the result.

This article is written for India on purpose: school, college, public and government libraries, Hindi and other Indian languages in the catalogue, books bought in Indian markets, members who need SMS or email reminders rather than app notifications, normal broadband or even patchy connectivity, and budgets that range from zero to enterprise. Every tool below is normally accessible from India on a standard internet connection, with no VPN requirement found in the sources checked. Prices and free limits change, so re-check the official pricing and help page of any tool before you pay, promise a deadline to readers, or move a whole collection onto it. Where sources disagreed, this article says so instead of picking the friendlier number.

## Koha: the free system to try first

[Koha](https://koha-community.org/) is the world's first free and open-source library automation package, and it is the strongest starting point in this article because it does the whole job without a licence fee. Its own description lists acquisitions, circulation, cataloging, serials management, authorities, flexible reporting, label printing, multi-format notices, and offline circulation for when internet access is not available. For a library, that list translates into plain work: a new book gets a proper catalogue record, a member borrows it with a scan, a hold is placed when it is out, a fine is tracked when it is late, and the public catalogue, called OPAC, lets readers search and manage their own accounts from a browser.

Cataloging in Koha follows library standards, which matters more than it sounds. It supports MARC 21 and UNIMARC records and protocols such as Z39.50, SRU, SIP2 and NCIP, which means your library can copy catalogue records from other libraries instead of typing every book from scratch, and can connect self-checkout machines where you have them. Member management covers registration, borrowing history and messaging, and the system works for a single-branch library as well as for a consortium of many branches. It is also multilingual and translatable, which helps a library whose collection spans Hindi, English and other Indian languages.

The honest cost of Koha is not money for the software but responsibility for running it. Koha is distributed under the Free Software General Public License version 3, so there is no vendor lock-in and you can export your data and change support company. But somebody still has to install it, update it, back it up, and restore it when something breaks. A school or public library with no technical person should either use a hosted Koha service from a support company, with the price agreed in writing, or start with a hosted tool like Librarika below and move to Koha later. A college with an IT team, on the other hand, can run Koha itself and spend its budget on books instead of licences. Before you commit, test it with your real work: catalogue fifty real books including some in Hindi, lend and return twenty of them, place holds, print spine labels, and send an overdue notice. If those five tasks work smoothly, Koha will serve you for years.

## SOUL 3.0: built for Indian colleges and universities

[SOUL 3.0](https://soul.inflibnet.ac.in/) is the Software for University Libraries, developed by the INFLIBNET Centre for college, university and other academic libraries. The current edition was unveiled on February 24, 2021, and it is designed around the daily housekeeping of an academic library: acquisition, catalogue, circulation, serial control, administration, and a web-based public catalogue. For an Indian college, its most useful features are the ones that match Indian conditions: Unicode-based multilingual support for Indian and foreign languages, compliance with MARC21 and related bibliographic standards, support for RFID and self check-out through NCIP 2.02 and SIP 2 protocols, and a companion Android application that offers the features of the web-based public catalogue.

Membership and lending are handled thoroughly. The circulation module covers membership, transactions, inter-library loan, overdue charges and reminders, stock verification, book bank and group issue, and customised member cards, with email and SMS sent for issue, return and reminder transactions. Members can register themselves online, import from an Excel worksheet is supported, and a camera can capture a member photograph. The catalogue module supports copy cataloguing in MARC 21 format, authority files for personal names, corporate bodies and subject headings, catalogue card and spine label printing, and barcode and book card generation. That combination means a college library can move from registers to a proper system without losing the Indian-language part of its collection.

The limit to check before you plan around SOUL is its terms. The pages checked for this article did not publish a price list, so do not budget a number you read on a third-party site. Contact INFLIBNET through its official site, confirm whether your institution qualifies, what the current terms are, what training and support are included, and how data export works if you ever change systems. Also confirm the current version and support position, because SOUL 3.0 dates from 2021 and software support arrangements change. If you are a school or public library rather than an academic one, SOUL is probably not your route, and Koha or Librarika will answer faster. For a school library specifically, see our guide to [AI tools for school management in India](/blog/ai-tools-for-school-management-india/).

## e-Granthalaya 4.0: the government library platform

[e-Granthalaya](https://egranthalaya.nic.in/) is a digital platform developed by the National Informatics Centre, part of the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, for government libraries. It provides an integrated library management system, a digital library module, cloud hosting, and a library portal with a public catalogue, and its current version 4.0 is described as a cloud-ready application with a centralised database for a cluster of libraries. It uses PostgreSQL, an open-source database, as its back end, and it is hosted on the NIC National Cloud, known as Meghraj, for government libraries on request.

Its modules cover the full job: database and library administration, books acquisition, cataloguing, circulation, serials control, budgets, a public catalogue, micro-documents, e-books, and digital library integration, with import and export in MARC, MARCXML, ISO 2709, Excel, Access and CSV formats, shared cataloguing, and integration with barcode, SMS, email and RFID. Online member services run through a single-window access system, so members of a government library can search, request and track items without visiting in person for every step. The scale claimed on its portal runs into thousands of libraries, tens of millions of titles and millions of members, though different NIC pages give slightly different totals, so treat the exact totals as approximate and the direction as clear: this is a large, established government system, not a new experiment.

Eligibility is the key fact. e-Granthalaya is for government and semi-government libraries, which apply by sending a filled and stamped request form with a covering letter to NIC. A private school, private college or community library should not plan around it, and should use Koha or Librarika instead. A government library, on the other hand, should talk to NIC before buying any commercial system, because e-Granthalaya already bundles hosting, a union catalogue for resource sharing, and rollout support through NICSI-empanelled agencies. Ask NIC in writing about the current version, the hosting arrangement, training for your staff, and the migration path for your existing registers or old software, and re-check those details before promising readers a launch date.

## LIBSYS Neo: the paid AI-first option

[LIBSYS Neo](https://www.libsys.co.in/products/library-solutions/library-management-system) is the most explicitly AI-marketed system in this article. Its vendor describes it as a next-generation AI-powered library management system with AI-driven cataloging, smart search and discovery with personalised recommendations, an AI chatbot and virtual assistant answering reader queries around the clock, unified management of print, digital and electronic collections, API-driven integration with institutional repositories, authentication systems and RFID, and AI-powered insights with predictive modelling and automated reports. For a large library, the chatbot alone can be a real saving: routine questions about opening hours, availability and renewals get answered without staff time, and the discovery search helps readers find a book even when they remember only a topic or a half-remembered title.

The honest warning is about price and proof. The vendor pages checked did not publish a price list for LIBSYS Neo, so its cost is quote-based and must be confirmed in writing. A third-party listing, TechJockey, puts LIBSYS 10, the earlier web-based version, from Rs 5,00,000 excluding GST, or Rs 5,90,000 including GST, but that is a reseller listing for LIBSYS 10, not a vendor price for Neo, and the two should not be treated as the same quote. Sources also describe LIBSYS as used by universities, research institutions, corporate and public libraries, but the exact customer count is vendor-reported, not independently audited. Before paying, ask for a written quote that lists the modules included, the number of users and records covered, chatbot and messaging costs, RFID and hardware costs, annual support and upgrade charges, data export terms, and contract length. Then run a trial with your own catalogue data and your own readers asking real questions, because an AI chatbot that answers confidently but wrongly about availability is worse than no chatbot at all.

## Librarika: the fastest free start for a small library

[Librarika](https://www.librarika.com/) calls itself the free integrated library system, designed for schools, colleges, organisations and home libraries, and it is the easiest recommendation in this article for a small library that needs to start this week. You create your library online, with no software to install, and the free Basic plan covers 2,000 records with unlimited patrons, unlimited staff, unlimited check-outs, a public catalogue, and email reminders. For a small school library, a coaching library, a housing-society library or a personal collection, 2,000 records is often enough for a year or more, and the unlimited check-outs mean lending is never the thing that hits the limit.

The paid plans are refreshingly plain. Silver costs 299 US dollars per year plus applicable taxes for 10,000 records, and Gold costs 499 US dollars per year plus applicable taxes for 15,000 records, with extras such as hold queues, custom greetings, multiple colour schemes, no banner ads, single sign-on and SIP2 integration on the paid tiers. Prices are in US dollars and tax is added at checkout based on billing address, so an Indian library should confirm the final rupee amount before paying. The limit to watch is the record count: when your collection crosses 2,000 titles, you either pay or move to Koha, so keep an export of your data from the start and test that export before you depend on it. Also note that Librarika is a hosted service run by a small company, not a government system, so check its backup and support terms the same way you would check any cloud tool that holds your member list.

## Evergreen: the open-source choice for library groups

Evergreen is an open-source integrated library system built for public libraries and, above all, for consortia: groups of libraries that share one catalogue and lend to each other's members. Its modules cover cataloging, circulation, acquisitions, serials control and an online catalogue, and its circulation rules can be set by library, patron type and item type, with courtesy reminders before due dates as well as standard overdue notices. Patron records support multiple categories with different loan rules, and linked accounts let family members be associated with each other. Cataloging supports MARC records, retrieval of records from external sources through a built-in Z39.50 client, and grouping of editions and formats of a title together.

Evergreen makes sense in India in one specific situation: several libraries that already cooperate, such as a group of public libraries in a district or a chain of schools, want one shared catalogue with central management. For a single small library, Koha is simpler and has wider support in India, and Evergreen's consortium strength would be complexity you do not need. Evergreen is free software, but setting it up for a consortium is technical work, so budget for skilled help and confirm that support is available to you in India before choosing it. Test the shared-catalogue flow with two branches, not one, because the whole point of Evergreen is how branches share, transfer and lend across each other.

## Alma: the enterprise platform for large academic libraries

[Alma](https://exlibrisgroup.com/products/alma-library-services-platform/) from Ex Libris is a library services platform, a step above a traditional library management system, because it manages print, electronic and digital materials in one unified workflow instead of treating them as separate collections. Its vendor highlights Alma Analytics for data-driven decisions, a central knowledge base of electronic resources so staff do not manually synchronise records, artificial intelligence features, more than 200 APIs for integration with institutional systems, and a linked-data approach to discovery. Paired with the Primo discovery service, it gives readers a single search across everything the library holds or subscribes to.

Alma is the right scale only for large institutions. It is a cloud subscription sold on a quote basis, with no public price found on the pages checked, and its cost, implementation time and training needs are all far above the free and small-library tools in this article. A university with large electronic subscriptions, multiple campuses and a research collection will get value from unified management and analytics. A school or small college will not, and should not let an enterprise demo set its budget. If you are evaluating Alma, ask for a written proposal covering the subscription, implementation, migration of your existing catalogue, training, annual increases, and exit terms including data export, and speak to at least one comparable Indian institution already running it before signing.

## AI cataloging assistants: useful drafts, not finished records

Beyond full library systems, general AI chatbots can draft catalogue records, and this is where the evidence needs care. A study using ChatGPT 4 for bibliographic description found that AI could significantly assist cataloging efficiency, was highly satisfactory at correcting errors in existing MARC21 records, and could convert catalogue card images into RDA-compliant MARC21 records, though in tests from January 2024 it struggled to convert Sinhala and Tamil data from images directly into MARC21 records. Another study reached a harsher conclusion: ChatGPT could generate MARC records, but their accuracy was questionable and correcting them took additional cataloguer time, so the efficiency gain was not automatic. A third line of work found AI helpful for drafting descriptive cataloging and access points, including generating tables of contents from uploaded files, but only with constant vigilance over titles, ISBNs, call numbers and subject headings, and with a daily limit on how many records could be generated.

The fair summary, where sources disagree, is this: AI is a good drafting assistant for cataloging and a bad unsupervised cataloguer. Use it to turn a title page photograph or a publisher description into a first draft, to clean up an existing record, or to suggest subject headings, and then have a cataloguer verify the title, author, ISBN, call number and subject headings against the book itself and against authority files before the record goes live. Never paste member names, phone numbers, addresses or borrowing history into a free chatbot, because that is personal data leaving your library's control. Never let an AI assign the only copy of a call number without a human check, because a wrong call number is a book lost on the shelf even though the catalogue says it exists. And keep the AI work outside your system of record: draft in the chatbot, verify, then enter the verified record into Koha, SOUL, e-Granthalaya or whichever system you run.

## Using these tools in India: language, connectivity and members

India adds four practical tests that a demo from another country will not cover. First, language. A catalogue that only handles English well will fail a library whose readers borrow Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Bengali or Urdu books. SOUL's Unicode-based multilingual support and Koha's multilingual design are genuine advantages here, and you should test them with real titles in your readers' languages, including searching by those titles, before you choose. Second, connectivity. A library with unreliable internet needs offline circulation, which Koha provides, or a hosted tool used carefully with regular exports, not a system that stops working the moment the connection drops. Third, reminders. Indian readers respond to SMS and email far more reliably than to notifications inside an app they rarely open, so check whether your chosen tool sends SMS and email for issue, return and overdue events, as SOUL and e-Granthalaya do, and what the messaging costs are. Fourth, identity and membership. A member card with a photograph, a barcode or RFID tag, self-registration for new members, and import of existing member lists from a spreadsheet are the features that decide whether enrolment week is smooth or chaotic, so test all four with real members.

Budget is the fifth test, and it separates this list cleanly. Zero budget means Koha self-hosted if you have technical help, or Librarika free up to 2,000 records if you do not. An academic budget means SOUL through INFLIBNET. A government budget means e-Granthalaya through NIC. A large institutional budget with electronic subscriptions means a quoted conversation with LIBSYS or Alma. Mixing those up, buying an enterprise system for a 500-book library or self-hosting Koha with nobody to maintain it, is how libraries end up paying for software nobody uses. ## What to avoid

Avoid buying before a real-data trial. A demo with clean sample books hides the problems your collection will show: duplicate titles, books without ISBNs, Hindi titles typed inconsistently, members with the same name, and old fines nobody recorded. Avoid any tool that will not show you a working data export before you pay, because a library system that holds your catalogue and member list hostage is a risk, not a purchase. Avoid pasting reader personal data into free AI chatbots, and avoid publishing AI-generated catalogue records without a cataloguer's check, because errors in the catalogue become lost books on the shelves. Avoid judging an AI chatbot by its demo answers and avoid judging a library system by its feature list; judge both by your own fifty books, your own twenty loans, and your own readers asking real questions. Avoid promising a launch date to readers or management until the official pricing and help pages have been re-checked, because free limits, prices and eligibility rules change, and a deadline built on last month's price list is a deadline you may not keep. Finally, avoid the trap of digitising everything at once: catalogue the most borrowed section first, lend from the new system for a month, fix what breaks, and then expand.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which library tool is completely free in India?**

Koha is free software under GPL version 3, with no licence fee, though hosting and support may cost if you hire help. Librarika is free up to 2,000 records on its Basic plan. e-Granthalaya is provided for eligible government libraries through NIC, and SOUL is supplied to academic libraries through INFLIBNET, with terms to confirm directly. No tool is free of all costs: every option needs staff time, and hosted or supported options add money costs on top.

**Can a small school or public library use Koha without a technician?**

It can use Koha through a hosted support company, which charges for setup and maintenance, or it can start with Librarika in a browser with no installation. Running Koha on your own server without technical help is not recommended, because updates, backups and repairs still need somebody skilled. Choose the hosted route if you have no technician, and keep Koha as the later step when you do.

**Which tool should a government library in India choose?**

Start with e-Granthalaya through NIC, because it is built for government libraries, includes cloud hosting and a union catalogue, and comes with rollout support. A government academic library should also check SOUL through INFLIBNET and compare the two on your own requirements before deciding, since the right answer depends on whether you are primarily a government departmental library or a university library.

**Can AI write catalogue records on its own?**

No, not reliably enough to publish unchecked. Studies show AI can draft MARC21 records, correct errors in existing records, and suggest subject headings, but other studies found significant errors that took cataloguer time to fix, and early tests struggled with some Indian-language scripts from images. Use AI for drafts, verify every field against the book, and enter only the verified record into your library system.

**How do members get reminders for overdue books?**

Koha, SOUL, e-Granthalaya and Librarika all support notices for lending events, with the exact channel depending on the tool: SOUL and e-Granthalaya support email and SMS, Librarika's free plan includes email reminders, and Koha supports multi-format notices. Confirm the channel, the cost of SMS credits if any, and whether reminders are automatic or need staff to trigger them, before you promise readers automatic reminders.

**Do these tools work in Hindi or other Indian languages?**

SOUL offers Unicode-based multilingual support for Indian and foreign languages, and Koha is multilingual and translatable, so both are credible choices for Indian-language collections. e-Granthalaya and LIBSYS also describe multi-language support. Whatever you choose, test search and display with real titles in your readers' languages first, because a feature listed on a website and a catalogue that actually finds a Hindi title are two different things.

## Final verdict

Start free with Koha or Librarika, choose SOUL or e-Granthalaya if you qualify, and pay for LIBSYS or Alma only after a written quote.

This article is general information only, not professional procurement advice. Library systems hold member personal data and institutional records, so check the official pricing, help and privacy pages, and your institution's own rules, before buying, migrating a collection, or sharing member details with any tool.
