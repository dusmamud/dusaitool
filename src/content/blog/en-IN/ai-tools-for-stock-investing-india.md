## Direct answer first

If you are a beginner in India who wants to research stocks properly, you do not need an expensive terminal, and you do not need to follow tips on social media. You need three kinds of tools, and all three have useful free versions that work in India without a VPN. First, you need a fundamentals tool that shows you a company's sales, profit, debt, and ratios over many years in one clean page. Second, you need a screener that lets you filter thousands of listed Indian companies down to a short list that matches criteria you understand, such as low debt or steady profit growth. Third, you need a portfolio tracker that shows what you already own, how it is spread across sectors and company sizes, and how it compares with a broad benchmark such as the NIFTY 50.

The short answer is this: start free with Screener.in for fundamentals, Tickertape for screening, and Trendlyne for easy-to-read scores and plain-English screener creation. Add StockEdge if you like ready-made scans and market breadth views on your phone, and use Groww's portfolio analysis only to understand holdings you already have, not as a reason to buy something new. Use a general AI assistant such as ChatGPT or Gemini only to explain a term or summarise a public document that you then verify yourself, never to pick stocks for you, and never by sharing your demat login, password, OTP, or bank details with any chatbot or tool. That combination covers research, screening, and tracking without paying anything at first, and it keeps the decision with you rather than with an algorithm, an influencer, or a trending list.

This article is deliberately educational. It explains what each tool does, what its free version actually includes, where its paid version begins, and how a beginner can use it step by step. It will not tell you which stock to buy, which stock will rise, or when to sell. Any tool, video, or message that guarantees returns, promises a fixed monthly profit, or pressures you to act today should be treated as a warning sign, not as research. Real research is slow, boring, and checkable: you read the numbers, you read what the company itself says, you compare similar companies, and you write down why a company interests you before you spend any money.

## How to use these tools as a beginner

Before opening any screener, fix a simple routine that takes about thirty minutes per company. First, open the company on Screener.in and read its sales, operating profit, net profit, and debt over ten years, not just one good quarter. A single good quarter can be a one-time gain, a seasonal effect, or an accounting change, while a ten-year view shows whether growth is steady, lumpy, or declining. Second, read the automated pros and cons checklist, but treat it as a starting list of questions, not a verdict. If it flags rising debt or falling margins, open the detailed schedule and see the numbers yourself. Third, compare the company with two or three peers in the same industry on the same ratios, because a profit margin that looks low for a bank may be normal for a retailer, and a growth rate that looks high may be ordinary for its sector. Fourth, check recent announcements, results, and credit rating changes on the same page, and then check the company's own investor presentation or annual report for what management itself claims. Fifth, write one paragraph in your own words: what the company sells, how it makes money, what could go wrong, and what would make you change your mind. If you cannot write that paragraph, you have not finished researching, no matter how many scores agree with each other.

A screener is the opposite job: it does not tell you what is good, it only narrows a very large list. India has thousands of listed companies, and no beginner can read all of them. A screener lets you say, in plain terms, "show me companies with low debt, positive cash flow, and a history of profits," and then gives you a manageable list to read one by one. The danger is adding too many filters until only two or three companies remain and then assuming those survivors must be winners. That is not what a filter proves. It only proves they passed your filters on past data. A good habit is to start with two or three filters you genuinely understand, read ten results properly, and only then add a filter if you can explain why it matters. Trendlyne's plain-English screener creation can help here, because you can type a question and see the query it builds, but you should still read that query and check whether it means what you intended.

Portfolio tracking is the third habit, and it matters even if you own only a few stocks. Most beginners remember their winners and forget their losers, or they own five companies from the same sector without realising it. A tracker fixes that by showing sector allocation, large-cap versus mid-cap versus small-cap split, and performance against a benchmark over the same period. Groww's portfolio analysis, for example, shows AUM appreciation over time, allocation by sector and by market capitalisation, XIRR that accounts for the timing of buys and sells, and benchmarking against the NIFTY 50. Trendlyne's free portfolio report similarly looks at performance, risk, returns, and sector diversification for equity and mutual fund portfolios. The point is not to chase the benchmark every month. The point is to notice concentration: if one sector or one company dominates your portfolio, a single bad event in that area hurts far more than a diversified portfolio would feel it.

## Screener.in: read one company properly

Screener.in is the best starting point for a beginner who wants to understand a single Indian company, because it puts long financial history, ratios, and filings on one page without asking you to learn a trading terminal first. Its features page describes financial data for listed Indian companies, queries over ten to fifteen years of financial data, announcement and regulatory filing tracking in one place, and an overview of any listed company in minutes. On a company page you can click into line items to see detailed schedules, for example breaking expenses into raw material and employee costs, which helps you see whether rising costs come from inputs, wages, or something else. You can export data to Excel, build your own models, and upload your own Excel sheet with formulas so future exports follow your format. Peer comparison lets you compare historical performance across companies, volume charts show delivery percentages, and credit rating tracking flags qualitative changes that numbers alone may miss.

The free account is genuinely useful, and its limits are stated plainly on its premium page. A free Hobby Investor account at zero rupees per year can follow up to fifty companies, set ten stock alerts, use twenty key insights per month, five insights-table views per month, ten AI summaries per month, eighteen quick ratios, fifteen comparison columns, two screen alerts, and two phrase alerts. The Premium plan for active investors was listed at 4,999 rupees per year, with unlimited company follows, eight hundred stock alerts, unlimited key insights, AI summaries and segment results, sixty quick ratios, fifty-five comparison columns, seventy-five screen alerts, and prioritised email delivery. Those numbers may change, so check the official premium page before paying, and do not buy Premium until the free limits actually block work you do every week. For most beginners, the free tier covers months of learning, because reading ten companies deeply teaches more than screening a thousand shallowly.

A practical way to use Screener.in is to pick a company you already know as a customer, open its page, and answer five questions from the data alone. Is sales growing over ten years or only in the last year? Is profit growing with sales, or is profit flat while sales rise, which may mean margins are shrinking? Is debt rising faster than profit? Does the company generate cash from operations, or does it report profit without cash? And what do the pros and cons say that you can verify in the schedules? If you practise this on familiar companies first, the ratios stop being abstract and start meaning something: a falling margin in a company whose products you buy feels different from a number in a textbook. Save the companies you study in a watchlist, turn on a small number of alerts for results and announcements, and revisit them after each quarterly result rather than checking prices every day.

## Tickertape: filter the market to a readable short list

Tickertape is the best screening-first tool in this list, because its screener is built around more than two hundred filters, pre-built screens, custom filters, and a custom stock universe. Its own screener guide explains that you can filter by basic measures such as volatility and one-year historical revenue growth, and by premium measures such as one-year forward EPS growth, analyst ratings, and fundamental score. You can combine filters, for example large-cap companies in a sector you follow with a return on equity and return on capital employed you understand, and then sort the results by the measure you care about. A custom stock universe lets you group favourite stocks by a theme in one place without re-applying filters each time, which is useful when you want to track, say, the companies in one industry over several months.

Tickertape also gives each stock a scorecard covering performance, valuation, and profitability, plus red flags, analyst ratings, price forecasts, and revenue and EPS forecasts. Those forecasts and scores are helpful as questions, not as answers. An analyst rating is an opinion from a particular analyst at a particular date, and a forecast is an estimate that will be revised when results arrive. A beginner should use the scorecard to decide what to read next — for example, if valuation looks high, read why earnings are expected to grow; if red flags appear, read what they refer to — rather than treating a high score as a buy signal. Tickertape's portfolio analysis, available in fuller form on paid plans, lets you link multiple demat accounts, compare holdings and metrics with the market, see a diversification score and red-flagged assets, and track forecast and performance tiers.

On pricing, Tickertape's Pro page listed monthly billing at 399 rupees per month, three months at 899 rupees, and twelve months at 2,999 rupees, working out to about 250 rupees per month on the annual plan. Pro adds premium screens, more than sixty custom filters, advanced forecasts, data export, and customisable filter options. The free version still lets you browse stocks, use basic screens, and learn the interface, so the sensible path is to learn screening free, save a few screens you actually reuse, and only consider Pro when you hit a specific paid filter or export limit repeatedly. As with every tool here, re-check the official pricing page before paying, because these amounts and the exact feature split between free and Pro change.

## Trendlyne: scores, SWOT, and plain-English screeners

Trendlyne is the friendliest tool in this list for a beginner who wants a quick, structured snapshot before diving into raw numbers. Its features page lists DVM scores, which combine durability, valuation, and momentum into one stock score based on financials, valuation, and technicals. It also lists a stock checklist score that evaluates fundamentals, technicals, and valuations quantitatively, a SWOT analysis summarising a company's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats, real-time screeners that update through the day, delivery volume data, FII and DII activity tracking, sector dashboards covering more than thirty sectors, and a free portfolio report covering performance, risk, returns, and sector diversification. One of its most beginner-friendly features is screener creation with AI: you ask a question in plain English, and the tool converts it into a working screener query. That is genuinely useful for learning, because you can see how a sentence becomes a set of conditions, and you can then edit those conditions yourself.

The free tier covers a surprising amount. Real-time screeners, superstar portfolios that track the disclosed moves of well-known investors, the stock checklist score, portfolio analysis with tax and profit-and-loss summaries, delivery volume data, SWOT analysis, the ETF dashboard, and the watchlist and portfolio newsfeed were all listed as free on the features page. Some features are freemium, meaning part is free and part needs a paid plan: DVM scores, AI screener creation, market breadth analysis, conference call AI summaries, and multiple watchlist support fall in that middle group. Premium-only features include technical indicators across timeframes, DVM score screeners, backtesting, the forecaster dashboard of institutional analyst estimates, and screener alerts at fifteen-minute, thirty-minute, or hourly intervals. Trendlyne offers Pro and Pro Plus subscriptions for deeper access, with monthly or annual billing, and the exact limits differ between plans, so compare the official subscription page against what you actually use before upgrading.

Two cautions apply specifically to Trendlyne. First, a DVM or checklist score is a summary of past and current data, not a prediction, and two tools can score the same company differently because they weight factors differently. If Tickertape and Trendlyne disagree, that disagreement is information: it tells you which factor each tool emphasises, and you should read the underlying numbers to decide which factor matters more for that company. Second, superstar portfolios show what famous investors disclosed, often with a delay, and copying them without understanding why they bought, at what price, and with what risk tolerance is not research. Use superstar tracking to discover companies to study, then study them on Screener.in exactly as you would any other company. Trendlyne's conference call AI summaries can also save time, but an AI summary of an earnings call can miss tone, omit a difficult question, or simplify a caveat, so listen to or read the key parts of the call yourself when a company matters to your decision.

## StockEdge: ready-made scans and daily market context

StockEdge suits beginners who prefer a phone app and ready-made ideas to study, rather than building every screen from scratch. Its blog and pricing pages describe more than five hundred advanced scans on paid plans, plus investment themes, investor portfolios, momentum scores, fundamental scores, market breadth, sector rotation, and an F and O zone. The app's market home shows live prices of major benchmark indices such as NIFTY 50, NIFTY Bank, and NIFTY 500, the advance-decline ratio during market hours, top news, trending stocks, FII and DII activity, IPOs, and block deals. That daily context helps a beginner learn how the market moves as a whole, rather than staring at one stock in isolation. The analytics dashboard gathers key events as brief snippets with edge insights, lists of stocks fulfilling technical and fundamental scans, chart patterns identified with AI assistance, trading strategies, investment ideas, and edge reports in one place.

StockEdge states that more than sixty percent of its features are free to use, which makes the free app a reasonable place to learn scanning habits before paying. The paid Premium plan was listed with an annual billing option working out to about 125 rupees per month when billed as 1,499 rupees per year, alongside a monthly option, and it unlocks the full set of advanced scans, combination scans, downloads, and premium club access. As always, verify the current pricing page, because promotional pricing and plan names change, and a discounted headline rate may differ from the renewal rate. The learning point for StockEdge is the same as for every scan-based tool: a scan result is a starting list, not a recommendation. A stock can appear in a momentum scan precisely because it has already risen sharply, which may mean opportunity, or may mean you are arriving late. Read the company, check why the scan picked it, and decide whether the reason still holds at today's price.

StockEdge also helps with breadth and sector thinking, which beginners often skip. Market breadth — how many stocks are advancing versus declining — tells you whether a rising index is carried by a few large companies or by a broad set of companies. Sector rotation views show which sectors are gaining attention and which are cooling. Neither view tells you what to buy, but both stop a common beginner mistake: assuming that because the index rose, every stock rose, or because one sector is popular this month, it will stay popular. Use these views to ask better questions, such as whether your portfolio is concentrated in the currently popular sector, and whether you would still be comfortable holding those companies if that sector cooled for a year.

## Groww: track what you already own

Groww is different from the other tools here because it is primarily a broker app, not a standalone research website. Its portfolio analysis feature, available inside the stocks holdings tab, is still worth including because many Indian beginners already hold stocks through Groww, and understanding those holdings is a research skill in itself. Portfolio analysis shows AUM appreciation over time against the amount invested, allocation by sector and by market capitalisation, XIRR that accounts for the timing of buys and sells rather than just the start and end values, and benchmarking against the NIFTY 50. It also breaks down individual stocks inside each sector, so you can see whether your sector exposure comes from one company or several. Groww describes itself as a platform for stocks, mutual funds, ETFs, IPOs, bonds, and more on NSE and BSE, with TradingView charts, price alerts, an intraday screener, and portfolio insights covering performance, allocation, risk, dividends, and XIRR.

The honest limit of Groww for research is that it is built around your own account. It is excellent for answering "what do I own, and how is it doing?" and weaker for answering "what should I study next?" compared with Screener.in or Tickertape. That is fine, because those are different jobs. A good monthly routine is to open portfolio analysis once a month, not every day, and note three things: your sector split, your large-cap versus mid-cap versus small-cap split, and your XIRR versus the NIFTY 50 over the same period. If one sector has grown to dominate your portfolio simply because those stocks rose, that is concentration created by success, and it still deserves a conscious decision: are you comfortable with that weight, or would you prefer to rebalance over time? Daily checking, by contrast, usually produces noise, anxiety, and impulsive trades, none of which is research. Groww's own app listing notes free demat account opening and zero annual maintenance charges, but brokerage, taxes, and other charges still apply to trades, so read the official charges page before assuming any trade is free.

Beginners should also separate the broker from the research. It is convenient to research and trade in the same app, but convenience can shorten the gap between an idea and a trade to seconds, which is exactly when impulsive decisions happen. A useful discipline is to keep a short written rule for yourself: no trade on the same day you first discover a stock. Research it on Screener.in, screen it on Tickertape or Trendlyne, sleep on it, and only then decide. Tools do not enforce that discipline for you, but a written rule plus a monthly tracking routine makes it much easier to keep.

## General AI assistants: explain and summarise, never decide

General AI assistants such as ChatGPT and Gemini work in India without a VPN, and they can genuinely speed up learning if used in the right role. Ask them to explain a term in simple language, for example what return on capital employed means, or why a company reports both standalone and consolidated numbers. Ask them to summarise a public annual report, investor presentation, or earnings call transcript that you provide or link, and to list the questions that document leaves unanswered. Ask them to turn a screener result into a study checklist, or to compare how two companies describe the same risk in their own words. Used this way, an AI assistant is a tutor and a reading aid, and it can turn an intimidating fifty-page report into a manageable first pass.

The limits are just as important. AI summaries can invent numbers, mix up years, quote a figure from one company as if it belonged to another, or present an outdated fact as current. A chatbot does not know your risk tolerance, your time horizon, your tax situation, or your other holdings unless you tell it, and even then it is not a registered adviser. Never share your demat login, trading password, PIN, OTP, bank login, or full account number with any chatbot, tool, or person who asks for it in a chat window, because no legitimate research tool needs those credentials to show you public fundamentals. Never accept a chatbot's stock pick, price target, or "buy now" suggestion as research, and never act on an AI-generated summary without opening the source document and checking the key numbers yourself. If the chatbot and Screener.in disagree on a number, Screener.in and the company's own filing win, and the chatbot's answer should be discarded.

A safe prompt pattern is: "Explain this term for a beginner, give one example, and tell me what to check in the annual report." An unsafe pattern is: "Which stock should I buy today for maximum profit?" The first pattern teaches you to read better. The second pattern asks the tool to decide for you, and any tool that answers it confidently is doing you a disservice, because it cannot know your situation and cannot guarantee an outcome. Keep AI in the tutor role, keep numbers in the filing and screener role, and keep decisions in your own role, ideally after discussion with a registered adviser if the amount matters to you.

## India-use section: what works here, what to check, and where to learn free

All six tools in this article work in India without a VPN, because they are built for Indian markets or, in the case of general AI assistants, officially available in India. Screener.in, Tickertape, Trendlyne, and StockEdge focus on NSE and BSE listed companies, Indian indices, and Indian filings, which is exactly what a beginner investing in India needs. Groww operates as a SEBI-registered broker for Indian exchanges. General AI assistants are available in India through their official apps and websites. If any tool ever asks you to use a VPN to reach Indian stock data, treat that as a red flag and prefer an official Indian source instead, because legitimate Indian market data does not require circumventing access controls.

For free learning, start with official investor education before paid courses. SEBI's investor education site publishes reading material on securities markets, KYC, IPOs, corporate actions, mutual funds, exchange-traded funds, REITs, InvITs, grievance redressal, and safeguarding against securities market frauds, in English, Hindi, and several other Indian languages. That material is free, regulator-published, and written for beginners, which makes it a better first textbook than any paid "stock market course" advertised on social media. Exchange and depository education pages similarly explain how buying, selling, demat accounts, and corporate actions actually work. A beginner who reads the SEBI material on corporate actions will understand dividends, bonuses, and splits far better than someone who only watches price charts, and that understanding prevents several common mistakes, such as assuming a bonus issue creates free money.

Practical India-specific checks belong in every research routine. Check whether a company is listed on NSE, BSE, or both, and whether its shares trade regularly, because a rarely traded stock can be hard to sell at a fair price even if its fundamentals look good. Check promoter holding, pledging, and recent insider or bulk and block deals where the tool shows them, because sharp changes can signal stress or confidence that deserves reading, not automatic copying. Check FII and DII activity at the market level, not as a buy signal for one stock, but as context for whether foreign and domestic institutions are broadly buying or selling. Check the company's filings on the exchange rather than relying only on a news headline, because headlines simplify, and the filing itself states the exact numbers, dates, and conditions. And check charges, taxes, and settlement timelines on your broker's official pages, because a research tool that shows a price does not show the full cost of a trade.

Language and access also matter in India. Many of these tools offer mobile apps, which suits beginners who learn on a phone, but a phone screen is a poor place to read a ten-year financial table. A good split is to discover and track on the phone, and to read company pages and annual reports on a larger screen when possible. If English financial terms are a barrier, use a general AI assistant to explain terms in Hindi or another language you are comfortable with, and use SEBI's Hindi and regional-language material alongside English sources, but always verify numbers in the original filing regardless of the language you learned in. ## What to avoid

Avoid guaranteed-return promises in every form. No legitimate tool, adviser, or influencer can guarantee a return from stocks, and anyone who promises a fixed monthly profit, a doubling in a set time, or a "sure-shot" stock is either mistaken or misleading you. SEBI's own fraud-awareness material warns against exactly these promises, and the correct response is to stop, save the message, and not send money. Avoid paid tips groups, jackpot calls, and "premium" WhatsApp or Telegram channels that charge for stock names without disclosing their own holdings, conflicts, or track record in a verifiable way. A genuine research tool shows you data and lets you decide; a tips service asks you to trust a name and act quickly, which is the opposite of research.

Avoid leverage and complex products until you understand the basic ones deeply. Margin trading, futures and options, and leveraged intraday trading can multiply losses as quickly as gains, and several broker apps surface them prominently next to simple stock investing. A beginner who has not yet read ten annual reports has no business using leverage, because leverage turns a normal research mistake into a large loss. Avoid checking prices constantly, trading on every news alert, and buying a stock only because it is trending on social media or because a famous person mentioned it. Trendlyne's superstar portfolios and StockEdge's trending lists are useful for discovery, but discovery is not endorsement, and a stock that everyone is talking about today is often the stock whose easy gains, if any, have already happened.

Avoid sharing credentials, avoid unverified apps, and avoid acting on screenshots. Never enter your demat password, trading PIN, or OTP into a research tool, a chatbot, or a website that is not your broker's official app or site, and never install an investing app from a link in a message rather than from the official app store listing for that broker. Screenshots of profits can be faked, backtested, or cherry-picked from a single good period, and a backtest that looks perfect on past data often fails on future data because markets change. Avoid the temptation to judge a tool by its best screenshot; judge it by whether it helps you read companies, track your own portfolio honestly, and make slower, calmer decisions over months. If a tool makes you trade more often, feel more anxious, or trust it more than your own reading, it is hurting your investing even if its interface looks impressive.

Avoid overconfidence from scores and from AI summaries. When three tools agree, it feels like proof, but they may all be reading the same past financial data, so agreement is not independent confirmation. When an AI summary sounds fluent and confident, it feels like understanding, but fluency is not accuracy. Build a habit of writing down what would change your mind about a company before you invest, and revisit that note after each result. If the reason you liked a company disappears — growth stalls, debt jumps, or management changes strategy — that note helps you reconsider calmly instead of defending an old decision. The best investors are not those who never change their minds; they are those who change their minds when the evidence changes, and who keep records that let them see when it has.

## FAQ

**What is the best free AI tool for stock research in India for a beginner?**
There is no single best tool, because research has three jobs. Screener.in is the best free starting point for reading one company's fundamentals over many years. Tickertape is the best free starting point for screening the whole market down to a short list. Trendlyne is the best free starting point for a quick score, SWOT summary, and plain-English screener help. Start with all three free versions, use each for the job it does best, and pay for nothing until a free limit blocks work you do every week.

**Do I need to pay for Tickertape, Trendlyne, or Screener.in to learn properly?**
No. Each tool's free version covers the core learning work: reading fundamentals, running basic screens, seeing scores and summaries, and tracking a watchlist or portfolio. Paid plans add more filters, alerts, downloads, forecasts, and backtesting, which matter more to active or professional users than to a beginner. Learn free for a few months, note which paid feature you actually miss, and only then compare the official pricing pages. Never buy a paid plan because of a discount timer or a promise that it will make you profitable.

**Can AI tools predict which Indian stocks will go up?**
No, and any tool that claims otherwise should not be trusted. AI tools in this article summarise past data, score companies on past and current measures, filter by criteria you choose, and summarise documents you provide. They cannot predict future prices, earnings surprises, regulatory changes, or market sentiment, and past performance does not predict future results. Use AI to read faster and organise better, not to forecast, and verify every important number against the company's own filing.

**Is it safe to link my demat account or share my portfolio with these tools?**
Linking a demat account or importing a portfolio is a convenience that carries privacy and security trade-offs, so read what data the tool accesses, how it stores it, and how to disconnect it before linking anything. Never share your trading password, PIN, or OTP with any research tool or chatbot, because legitimate research does not need those credentials. If you only want to learn, you do not need to link anything at all: you can study companies and build watchlists without connecting a brokerage account, and add portfolio tracking later only if you understand and accept the tool's data policy.

**How is a screener different from a stock tip?**
A screener is a filter you control: you choose criteria you understand, it returns companies that passed those criteria on past data, and you still have to read each company and decide. A tip is a name someone else chose, usually with urgency and without showing their reasoning, holdings, or risk. Screeners support your own research; tips replace it. If you cannot explain why a screened company interests you in your own words, treat it as unread, not as researched, no matter how impressive the screen looks.

**Should beginners start with large-cap companies or small-cap companies?**
This article does not recommend any category, but for learning, larger and more widely followed companies are usually easier to research because they publish detailed filings, hold regular earnings calls, and have longer financial histories and peer comparisons available. Smaller companies can be harder to research, less liquid, and more volatile, which means a research mistake costs more. Whatever you study, compare companies within the same industry, read ten-year histories, and understand the business before considering size, popularity, or recent price movement as a reason.

**How often should I check my portfolio and rebalance it?**
For most beginners, checking holdings once a month is enough, and checking prices every day usually adds anxiety without adding information.
