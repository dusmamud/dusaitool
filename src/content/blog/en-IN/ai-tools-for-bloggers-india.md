Blogging in India is a different job from blogging in the US or Europe. Most Indian bloggers start on a phone, write in English, Hindi, or Hinglish, earn in rupees through AdSense or affiliate links, and pay for tools with a debit card or UPI where it is accepted at all. The tools that work for them are the ones that run inside WordPress, cost nothing to start, and do not demand a dollar subscription before the first reader arrives. This article covers exactly those tools, with their real free limits, so you can build a blogging workflow that costs zero rupees until your blog starts earning.

The short answer for India is this: start on [WordPress.com](https://wordpress.com/pricing/) free, because it gives you hosting, a theme, and publishing without paying anything, even though free sites show WordPress.com ads to visitors. Draft headlines, outlines, and introductions with [ChatGPT](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/9275245-chatgpt-free-tier-faq) free, because it is the most versatile free drafting tool available in India without a VPN. Use [Jetpack AI](https://jetpack.com/ai/) inside WordPress for titles, summaries, and short text, because its free quota of 20 requests lets you try AI inside your editor before paying. Install the free [Rank Math](https://rankmath.com/ai/pricing/) or [Yoast SEO](https://yoast.com/) plugin for titles, meta descriptions, and sitemaps, because on-page SEO is where most new blogs fail. Track everything with [Google Search Console](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9128668?hl=en), which is completely free, and find what readers actually ask with [AnswerThePublic](https://answerthepublic.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/22617503900187-Is-There-a-Free-Version-of-AnswerThePublic), which gives 3 free searches a day. Clean up the English with [Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/plans) free. That stack costs nothing, and it covers the whole workflow: idea, draft, SEO, publish, and measurement.

## ChatGPT free: drafts, headlines, and outlines

ChatGPT free is the drafting engine behind most Indian blogs that use AI, and for good reason. According to OpenAI's own free tier FAQ, free users get web search, file and image uploads, GPTs, image creation, and 500 MB of library storage, with the default model listed as GPT-5.6 Luna at the time of checking. The practical workflow is simple: ask for ten headline options for one topic, pick the two that sound human, ask for an outline with one section per reader question, and then write the article yourself around that outline. Used this way, ChatGPT saves the hardest hour of blogging, which is the blank page, without turning your blog into copied output.

Two warnings matter here. First, OpenAI itself says the default model and limits can change over time, and that ads may appear in the free tier in certain countries, so do not build a workflow that depends on one exact model name. Second, never publish a ChatGPT draft unchanged. AI invents prices, dates, and statistics with confidence, and a blog that publishes invented facts loses reader trust and Google trust at the same time. Use ChatGPT for structure and phrasing, and check every fact, price, and claim yourself before publishing. ## Jetpack AI: AI inside your WordPress editor

Jetpack AI is the most convenient AI tool for WordPress bloggers because it lives inside the editor you already use. You do not copy text between apps, and you do not need a separate subscription to try it. According to Jetpack's own pricing page, the free tier gives a quota of 20 requests, covering prompt-based content generation, text, images, tables, and lists, adaptive tone adjustment, spelling and grammar correction, and title and summary generation. Twenty requests is enough to test it properly: generate titles for five posts, summaries for five posts, and short introductions for ten, and you will know whether it fits your writing style.

When the free quota runs out, Jetpack AI costs $9.95 per month, with the first year discounted to $4.95 per month when billed yearly, and the paid plan promises high request capacity with a fair usage policy. That price is in US dollars, so Indian bloggers should check the final rupee amount after bank conversion charges before subscribing. The honest rule for Jetpack AI is the same as for every blogging tool in this article: use the 20 free requests first, and pay only when you have published enough posts to know that the tool saves you real time every week.

## Rank Math: free SEO plugin, paid Content AI

Rank Math is the SEO plugin most new WordPress bloggers install first, and the free plugin alone covers the basics well. It handles SEO titles and meta descriptions, XML sitemaps, basic schema, and a content analysis score for each post, all without payment. For a new blog, that is genuinely enough: a clear title, a meta description that matches the article, and a sitemap that Google can read will do more for traffic than any paid AI feature.

Rank Math's AI features are a separate, paid product, and this is where bloggers should read carefully. Content AI, which writes content, suggests internal links, and helps with brand visibility on AI platforms, starts at €4.69 per month on the Starter plan when billed annually, with Creator at €8.59 and Expert at €12.99 per month. Those prices are in euros, so the rupee cost is higher than the number suggests. There is also a free Rank Math PRO trial, but a trial is not a free plan, and it ends. The sensible path is to run the free plugin for your first twenty or thirty posts, learn what the content score actually measures, and only consider Content AI when your blog has enough traffic that better internal linking and content suggestions could plausibly earn the subscription back.

## Yoast SEO: the other free SEO plugin

Yoast SEO is the older, better-known alternative to Rank Math, and the choice between them is mostly habit. The free Yoast plugin covers SEO titles, meta descriptions, readability analysis, and sitemaps, which is the same core job as Rank Math's free plugin. Do not install both at once: two SEO plugins will fight over your titles and sitemap, and the result is worse than either plugin alone. Pick one, learn it, and keep it.

Yoast's newer AI features, including schema aggregation built with Microsoft, an automatically generated llms.txt file for AI platforms, and a bot blocker for controlling which language models can use your content, are presented on Yoast's AI features page as part of its modern SEO offering. Yoast SEO Premium, which carries the advanced features, costs $118.80 per year excluding VAT. For a new Indian blogger, that price is hard to justify before the blog earns, and the free plugin is the right starting point. The llms.txt idea itself is worth knowing even on the free tier: as AI search grows, a clear, structured summary of your site helps AI platforms describe your content accurately instead of guessing.

## Google Search Console: the free tool that tells the truth

Google Search Console is the most important free tool in this entire article, and it is the one new bloggers skip most often. It is a free service from Google that shows how your site appears in search results: which queries bring visitors, how often your pages appear, how often searchers click, whether Google can crawl and index your pages, and whether anything is broken. No AI writing tool can replace that information, because it is data about your own blog, not generic advice.

Setting it up takes about fifteen minutes. Verify your WordPress site, submit the sitemap that Rank Math or Yoast already generated, and then check the performance report once a week. The report will tell you which posts are close to ranking on page one, which queries deserve a better title, and which pages Google has not indexed at all. That weekly habit is what turns blogging from guessing into a system: write, publish, check Search Console, improve the posts that are almost working, and repeat. It costs nothing, works in India without a VPN, and every serious Indian blogger should have it open before buying any paid SEO tool.

## AnswerThePublic: what readers actually ask

AnswerThePublic answers the question every blogger asks first: what are people actually searching for? You type a topic, and it returns the questions, prepositions, and comparisons people search around it, organised as keyword wheels and lists. For a blogger, that list is a content plan: each real question is a heading, a section, or a whole post. According to its own support page, the free plan allows 3 searches per day, with some suggestions and data points locked, and the daily limit resets automatically. Three searches a day is enough for a solo blogger who plans one week of content at a time, because one search on a broad topic returns dozens of questions.

Use it together with Search Console. AnswerThePublic tells you what people might ask, and Search Console tells you what people already ask about your blog. When the two overlap, you have a post worth writing. When a question appears in AnswerThePublic but never in your Search Console data, it may still be worth a post, but treat it as an experiment, not a guaranteed traffic source. And remember that the free plan locks some data points, so if a search returns a surprisingly thin result, it may be the free limit, not a lack of interest in the topic.

## Grammarly free: clean English before publishing

Grammarly free is the last step before the publish button, and it is the simplest tool in this stack. According to Grammarly's own plans page, the free plan costs $0 and covers spelling and grammar mistakes, while Pro costs $12 per month and adds full-sentence rewrites and more. For an Indian blogger writing in English as a second language, the free plan catches the errors that make readers leave: wrong verb forms, missing articles, and doubled words. It does not fix weak arguments or thin content, and it should not be used to rewrite your whole voice into generic corporate English, but as a proofreading pass it is genuinely useful.

The workflow that works is to write the post, run Grammarly free once, accept the clear errors, and ignore suggestions that change your meaning or your tone. Readers in India are used to Indian English, and a blog that sounds like a person is worth more than a blog that sounds like a grammar textbook. If you write in Hindi, Grammarly will not help much, because its strength is English, so Hindi bloggers should proofread by reading the post aloud once, which catches more errors than any tool for a language the tool barely supports.

## WordPress.com free: hosting with no bill

WordPress.com's free plan is how most Indian bloggers should start, because it removes the two costs that stop beginners: hosting and setup. You get a site, a theme, and publishing without paying, and you can move to a paid plan later. The trade-offs are real and should be stated plainly. Free sites display WordPress.com ads to visitors, you do not get a custom domain on the free plan, and storage and customisation are limited. The Personal plan, shown on WordPress.com's pricing page from around $4 per month, removes the ads and adds a free domain for one year. Those prices are in US dollars and change with billing period, so check the pricing page before paying.

For bloggers who already have self-hosted WordPress, the same logic applies in reverse: you do not need WordPress.com at all, because Jetpack AI, Rank Math, and Yoast all work on self-hosted sites too. The free plan matters most for beginners who want to publish their first ten posts without buying hosting, a domain, and a theme before knowing whether they enjoy blogging. If you outgrow it, moving to paid hosting later is normal, and the posts you wrote on the free plan are the proof that blogging is worth paying for. ## Paid tools: only after you earn

Two famous names deserve an honest warning in this article, because older guides still call them free. Surfer SEO, once known for a free tier, now leads with paid plans for AI search visibility, with its pricing page showing $82 per month billed yearly for its entry plan. Writesonic's pricing page now starts with a Starter plan at $79 per month billed annually, focused on AI search visibility, with a free trial rather than a permanent free tier. Both are serious tools for blogs that already earn, and neither belongs in a beginner's stack. The pattern to watch is simple: when a tool's pricing page no longer shows a free plan, guides that still call it free are out of date, and you should trust the pricing page, not the guide.

The same rule applies to Rank Math Content AI and Yoast Premium. They are good products, and a blog earning steady affiliate or AdSense income may justify them, but a blog with ten posts and no traffic does not need them. The order that works in India is: publish twenty posts on free tools, check Search Console for the first signs of traffic, and only then spend on the one paid tool that fixes your biggest bottleneck, whether that is content speed, internal linking, or on-page optimisation.

## Using blogging AI in India: language, payment, and honesty

Blogging in India raises three practical questions that guides written elsewhere ignore. The first is language. Hindi blogs have a large and growing audience, and AI tools handle Hindi drafting better than they did two years ago, but SEO plugins, readability scores, and grammar tools are still built for English first. A Hindi blogger should use ChatGPT or Jetpack AI for outlines and ideas, write the article in natural Hindi, and judge readability by reading it aloud, not by an English readability score. Hinglish blogs sit in between: they work well for Indian readers, but search engines and AI tools handle them less predictably than pure Hindi or pure English, so pick one primary language per post and keep the other for flavour.

The second question is payment. Most blogging tools price in US dollars or euros, accept international cards, and do not accept UPI directly. Indian debit cards often need international transactions enabled, and banks add conversion charges, so a $9.95 tool can cost noticeably more in rupees than the headline price suggests. Before paying for anything, check the pricing page, check whether a free trial needs a card, and set a calendar reminder before the trial ends. A tool that asks for a card before showing any free value is a warning sign, not a feature.

The third question is honesty about AI content. Search engines do not ban AI assistance, but they do penalise thin, copied, or misleading content, and readers can tell when a blog is pure AI output. The bloggers who last in India are the ones who add something AI cannot: personal experience, Indian prices and examples, screenshots from their own setup, and corrections when a tool changes its limits. That is also why this article states free limits with sources and dates: a limit that is true today may change next month, and the official pricing or help page is the only source worth trusting before you pay or promise a deadline. ## What to avoid

Avoid publishing AI drafts unchanged, because invented facts and generic advice are the fastest way to lose both readers and search rankings. Avoid installing two SEO plugins at once, because they conflict over titles and sitemaps. Avoid buying Surfer, Writesonic, or any paid SEO tool before your blog has traffic, because a paid tool cannot create an audience for a blog with no posts. Avoid keyword stuffing, because stuffing the same phrase into every paragraph reads badly and ranks badly. Avoid tools that demand a card for a so-called free plan, and avoid copying another blog's structure sentence by sentence, because that is plagiarism even when an AI does the copying. Finally, avoid believing any single free limit forever: check the official pricing page again before you pay, before you promise a client a deadline, and before you recommend a tool to another blogger.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I start a blog in India for free?** Yes. WordPress.com free gives you hosting and publishing with ads shown to visitors, and the free tools in this article cover drafting, SEO, and measurement. You need a paid plan only for a custom domain, ad removal, or advanced features, and you can decide that after your first posts.

**Which free AI tool is best for blog headlines?** ChatGPT free for ten headline options at once, and Jetpack AI inside WordPress for titles and summaries when you want AI inside the editor. Use both for ideas, then pick the headline that sounds like a person wrote it, not the one that sounds most dramatic.

**Do I need Rank Math or Yoast, or is one enough?** One is enough. Both free plugins cover titles, meta descriptions, and sitemaps. Installing both causes conflicts, so pick Rank Math or Yoast, learn its content score, and keep it for every post.

**Is AI-written content bad for Google rankings?** Google penalises thin, copied, or misleading content, not AI assistance itself. A post that adds Indian prices, personal experience, and checked facts ranks better than a pure AI draft, whatever tool wrote the first version.

**How many free AnswerThePublic searches do I get?** Three searches per day on the free plan, according to its own support page, with some data points locked. That is enough for weekly planning, because one search returns dozens of real reader questions.

**When should I pay for a blogging tool?** When your blog has traffic and one clear bottleneck. If drafting is slow, consider Jetpack AI paid. If internal linking and content suggestions are the bottleneck, consider Rank Math Content AI. Pay for the bottleneck, not for the brand name.

## The final verdict, and a checklist before you pay

Start with WordPress.com free, Jetpack AI's 20 free requests and Search Console. Pay for Rank Math AI only once a post starts earning. Before you pay for anything, run this checklist: publish twenty posts on free tools, check Search Console for real queries, confirm the price on the official pricing page including currency conversion, check whether a trial needs a card, and name the one bottleneck the paid tool will fix. If you cannot name the bottleneck, you do not need the paid tool yet.
